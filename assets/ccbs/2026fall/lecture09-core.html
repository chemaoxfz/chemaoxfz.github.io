Closing the loop: five behaviors of binding and catalysis

Lecture 8 built the model class. This lecture puts it to work on five behaviors that cells compute with: the sign of regulation, ultrasensitivity, multistability, adaptation and oscillation. For each one, an effective rate law and the binding-catalysis model agree where the law was fitted. Moving a total and closing the loop shows what the law hides or forbids.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · Lecture 9 · core · 8 October 2026 (Thu) · 10:40–12:15 · students present, lecturer-backed

Lecture 8 closed the course's first phase with a vector field. Binding and catalysis became the model class of a cell. Its coda closed every production law with removal on the same totals, and asked which steady states exist, whether they are stable and when a circuit oscillates. Unique fast allocation does not answer those questions. It only supplies the vector field.

This lecture opens the second phase by running that vector field. From here each lecture starts from a behavior a cell shows. We take five behaviors in turn. For each, we compare the binding-catalysis model with the effective rate law that circuit biology usually writes instead, and we find where they part.

What this lecture enables

Derive an effective rate law as a limit of a binding-catalysis model, and match the two. Then change a total, close the loop and read the behavior from the dynamics. Recognize three outcomes: the law hides a difference between mechanisms, forbids a behavior the mechanism produces, or misjudges how a capacity scales.

Part 1

Close the loop

State what a behavior is, and how to compare two models of it fairly.

1A behavior belongs to the closed loop

A response curve describes one experiment. A behavior is what the closed vector field on the totals does.

The course now turns from building tools to explaining behaviors. The first eight lectures estimated scales, wrote reactions, analyzed dynamics and noise, reduced fast processes and weighed equilibrium states. They ended with binding and catalysis as the model class. From here each lecture starts from something a cell does, and asks what the model class lets us explain about it.

Lecture 8 handed over the loop that every example closes. A binding layer at equilibrium allocates each total among free species and complexes. The allocation sets the activities, and the activities set the catalytic rates. Catalysis and removal then change the totals, and the totals change the allocation. For a produced protein X, the production law vprod(q;b)v_\mathrm{prod}(q;b) is read off the binding layer, and the accumulation line is

q˙X=vprod(q;b)−δ qX.\dot q_X = v_\mathrm{prod}(q;b) - \delta\, q_X .(1)

Here qq collects the totals, bb collects the binding constants and activities, and δ\delta is the removal frequency shared by every X-containing state. If only free X is removed, the loss term is −δX-\delta X instead, and binding protects X. Growth dilution removes every state alike, at −λqX-\lambda q_X.

Circuit biology usually replaces the binding layer by one effective rate law. Production is written as a Hill function of one regulator concentration uu:

vprod=β (u/K)n1+(u/K)n(activation),vprod=β1+(u/K)n(repression).v_\mathrm{prod}=\beta\,\frac{(u/K)^n}{1+(u/K)^n}\quad\text{(activation)},\qquad v_\mathrm{prod}=\frac{\beta}{1+(u/K)^n}\quad\text{(repression)}.(2)

The maximal rate is β\beta, the half-maximal input is KK, and the Hill coefficient nn sets the steepness. Lecture 8 derived laws of this form as limits of binding-catalysis models. Each limit holds under stated conditions, for example a regulator in excess of its binding sites.

The question is what the shortcut loses once the loop closes. An effective rate law can be fitted to a measured response, and the binding-catalysis model gives the same response at the fitted condition. The two can still predict different steady states, different numbers of attractors, different adaptation and different rhythms. We test five behaviors that cells compute with. 2A fair comparison changes one total

Derive the shortcut from the full model, match the two where they must agree, and only then change what a cell changes.

A fair comparison has five steps, and the last one extends Lecture 8's pipeline. Lecture 8 read a response off a binding layer in five steps: model, experiment, candidates, regime sequence, response. Here the response becomes a production term, and the comparison ends by closing the loop.

  1. Model. Write the binding reactions, the totals, the activities and the removal variant. Name the observable.
  2. Limit. Derive the effective rate law as a limit of the model, and name the assumptions that make the limit exact.
  3. Match. Choose parameters so that both models give the same response at one reference condition.
  4. Move a total. Change what a cell changes: an input total rather than a free input, a regulator total, a number of binding sites, a turnover rate.
  5. Close the loop. Run the total dynamics and read the behavior: steady states, attractors, adaptation error or a cycle.

Steps 2 and 3 keep the comparison honest. An effective rate law is not wrong. It is the right answer to a narrower question, posed at the condition where its assumptions hold. A comparison that skipped the derivation could blame the law for a parameter choice. A comparison that skipped the matching could report a difference that is only a shift of scale.

Step 4 is where the two models begin to part. An effective rate law takes one concentration as its input, usually a free one. A cell controls totals: it makes and removes molecules, and binding decides how many of them are free. Lecture 8 showed that one binding pair has different laws in different regimes of its totals. Whenever a total moves the system across a regime, the effective rate law fitted in one regime stops describing the other.

Step 5 is where a difference in response becomes a difference in behavior. Steepness, a threshold or a shared pool changes the vector field. Only the closed dynamics say whether that changes the number of attractors, the return after a step or the existence of a cycle. A steep response or a count of binding states is never evidence of multistability by itself.

Before calling two models different, derive one as a limit of the other, match them at a reference condition, change one total, and read the behavior from the closed loop.

Part 2

Responses that compute

Two responses that a Hill curve usually summarizes: the sign of regulation and a threshold. Each is matched in a free input, then moved in a total. Every number in Parts 2 and 3 is a model prediction at stated illustrative parameters, unless a box marks it as measured.

3Activator and repressor part when the inducer is a total

Two circuits turn a gene on when an inducer appears. Matched in free inducer, they compute different functions of the inducer total.

An inducer can turn a gene on in two ways. It can activate an activator, which then loads the promoter. Or it can inactivate a repressor, which then frees the promoter. Both circuits are routinely summarized by the same increasing Hill curve, once their basal level, range, midpoint and steepness are matched.1 This section asks whether they compute the same thing.

1 · Model. Both circuits read one promoter D and make one output protein G. In the activator circuit, the inducer I binds the activator A, and only the complex CAIC_{AI} binds the promoter, as CDAIC_{DAI}. In the repressor circuit, the repressor R binds the promoter as CDRC_{DR}, and the inducer binds the repressor as CRIC_{RI}, which cannot bind the promoter. All four binding steps are elementary:

Two conditions for the threshold

The threshold needs two conditions: tight inducer binding and an inducer total that the cell controls. Take tightness first. At qR/KDR=100q_R/K_{DR}=100, the repressor's nHn_H in total inducer is 25.7 at KRI=0.01KDRK_{RI}=0.01K_{DR}, 6.1 at 0.1KDR0.1K_{DR} and 1.9 at KDRK_{DR}. So the repressor must bind its inducer at least ten times more tightly than its operator. Then the input. A metabolite, a second messenger, a protein anti-repressor or a cell-free extract has a total set inside the system. A membrane-permeant inducer in a large medium clamps the free inducer, and then the effective rate law (5) is the right model.

4One binding layer makes a threshold at a total

The textbook switch needs two saturated enzymes. One tight binding layer also makes a threshold if the input is a total, and the threshold sits at another total.

Ultrasensitivity means a response steeper than the Michaelis-Menten law, and two measures are used. The Hill coefficient nHn_H of §3 summarizes the whole rise from 10% to 90%. The local order dln⁡(output)/dln⁡(input)d\ln(\text{output})/d\ln(\text{input}) is the slope on log axes at one point, the gain of Lecture 8's regime tables. A threshold is a claim about the local order, because a hard saturation corner can raise nHn_H without one.

The canonical switch is a modification cycle whose two enzymes are both saturated. A kinase E converts a substrate X into its modified form Y, and a phosphatase F converts it back. Each enzyme binds its substrate in an elementary step and acts in a composite catalytic step, with co-substrates held fixed:

E+X⇌k−Ek+ECEX,CEX⇝E+Y  (rate law kECEX),F+Y⇌k−Fk+FCFY,CFY⇝F+X  (rate law kFCFY).\begin{aligned}&E+X\xrightleftharpoons[k_{-E}]{k_{+E}}C_{EX},\qquad C_{EX}\rightsquigarrow E+Y\ \ (\text{rate law }k_EC_{EX}),\\ &F+Y\xrightleftharpoons[k_{-F}]{k_{+F}}C_{FY},\qquad C_{FY}\rightsquigarrow F+X\ \ (\text{rate law }k_FC_{FY}).\end{aligned}(9)

The input is the ratio of the two enzyme totals, r=qE/qFr=q_E/q_F, with equal catalytic constants kE=kFk_E=k_F. The readout is the modified fraction of the substrate total, y=qY/qSy=q_Y/q_S, counting free and enzyme-bound Y. Both enzymes have the same dissociation constant K, which is also their Michaelis constant when catalysis is slow against unbinding.

Saturation sets the steepness. Goldbeter and Koshland took both enzymes in trace and computed the response in closed form.6 Their ratio of the inputs giving 90% and 10% modification is RV=81(Kˉ+0.1)2/(Kˉ+0.9)2R_V=81(\bar K+0.1)^2/(\bar K+0.9)^2, with Kˉ=K/qS\bar K=K/q_S. nH=ln⁡81ln⁡RV≈0.25 qSK.n_H=\frac{\ln 81}{\ln R_V}\approx 0.25\,\frac{q_S}{K}.(10)

So qS=100Kq_S=100K makes a switch whose rise from 10% to 90% spans an 18% change of the enzyme ratio, while qS=Kq_S=K gives only nH=1.33n_H=1.33 (model predictions, Figure 4, left).

Part 3

Closed loops that hold, adapt and cycle

Three behaviors that exist only in the closed dynamics: several stable states, a return after a step, and a sustained rhythm. Each is read from fixed points, step responses or a cycle, never from a response curve alone.

5Shared binding partners multiply the stable states

A Hill toggle holds two states, and N genes with Hill repression hold at most N + 1. One shared pool of binding partners holds combinatorially many, because one dimerization equilibrium makes both the activator and the inhibitor.

A stable state of a gene circuit can store a memory or a cell type. The question is how many stable states N mutually inhibiting transcription factors can hold, and what sets that number. A count of binding states or a steep response does not answer it. Only the stable fixed points of the closed total dynamics do.

1 · Model. Mutual inhibition is written here in two binding-catalysis forms, one for two genes and one for N.

Adding factors separates the Hill network from MultiFate. In the Hill product form a stable state has at most one raised node, because two nodes at the same raised level push each other apart, one up and one down. The removal variant decides how the factors couple. If only free monomers are removed, each factor's equation depends on its own free monomer alone, and the factors decouple. The contrast is not that binding beats Hill functions. A Hill network of N self-activating switches with weak cross-repression also reaches 2N2^N states. The point is narrower. In MultiFate one dimerization equilibrium supplies both the steepness of self-activation and a graded cross-inhibition by sharing, so a combinatorial repertoire needs neither promoter cooperativity nor designed cross-repression.

Count the stable states of a mutual-inhibition circuit from its closed loop. A symmetric two-gene loop needs a loop gain above 1 per node, from cooperativity or from a promoter pool larger than K. N Hill genes hold at most N + 1 states, while a shared partner pool holds the binomial sum (22).

6Two implementations of one feedforward loop fail at opposite ends

An incoherent feedforward loop cancels its input with a ratio. Degradation and repression compute the same ratio, but each needs its own binding regime, and the input pushes them out of it at opposite ends.

Adaptation is a return to the old output after a sustained change of input. The incoherent feedforward loop adapts without feedback. The input drives the output X directly, and it also drives a controller Y that opposes X. Y can oppose X in two ways: as an enzyme that degrades X, or as a repressor of the gene that makes X.

The test is a tenfold input step. Hold the input at u0u_0 until steady state, then step it to 10u010u_0. The steady-state error of the step is ε=qX∗(10u0)/qX∗(u0)−1\varepsilon=q_X^*(10u_0)/q_X^*(u_0)-1, and an input range where ∣ε∣≤0.1|\varepsilon|\le 0.1 is the adaptive window.

If X is also diluted, at the controller's rate γ\gamma, the best error of a tenfold step is 19%, and no input adapts within 10% (Figure 10, dashed). A 10% window then needs a catalytic step about 3500 times faster than the dilution of X (model predictions). The repression design has no such lower bound, because its output is removed at first order anyway.

Saturating the degrader trades robustness for range

Zero-order kinetics does not rescue this loop. A heavier degrader load widens the window, because free X grows while the complex does not, and as ρ→1\rho\to 1 the degrader saturates. But the set point (27) then depends steeply on the load: dln⁡X∗/dln⁡ρ=1/(1−ρ)d\ln X^*/d\ln\rho=1/(1-\rho), which is 1.05 at ρ=0.05\rho=0.05, 2 at 0.5 and 10 at 0.9. With the degrader fully saturated, the removal flux kcatqYk_\mathrm{cat}q_Y no longer depends on X, and a steady state exists only as ρ→1\rho\to 1. For ρ≥1\rho\ge 1 production outruns the degrader's capacity, and X accumulates without bound.

5 · Close the loop. The step responses of the closed dynamics show both failures (Figure 12). At low input, u=0.01→0.1u=0.01\to 0.1, the degradation design pulses 5.9-fold and returns to within 8.5%, while the repression design rises about tenfold and stays there, an error of 824%. At high input, u=10→100u=10\to 100, the roles swap. The repression design pulses 4.9-fold and returns to within 8.1%. The degradation design's total output settles at an error of 814%, while its free X still returns. The effective rate laws see neither failure: the Michaelis-Menten law adapts at every input, and the Hill law shows the leak at low input but not the titration.

A heavy promoter turns the repression failure into a switch. At qG=10Kdq_G=10K_d the high-input step starts on Lecture 8's titration ray, where the repressor and promoter totals match. The output then over-adapts and settles at 0.41 of its old level (model prediction). The Hill law, with its promoter in trace, cannot show this.

The readout matters as much as the regime. In the degradation design free X adapts exactly at every input, and only the total fails. Which pool counts is a biological question. The total counts when bound X still acts or is still measured, as for a fluorescent fusion, or for a target mRNA that is still translated while bound, until it is cleaved. The effective rate law cannot pose this question, because it has no complex.

Estimate meets measurement

Engineered circuits sit where the regimes put them. Segall-Shapiro, Sontag and Voigt tuned a monomeric TALE repressor to stay in excess at the lowest gene dosage, and obtained nearly constant expression across plasmids from 3 to 100 copies per cell, at the price of output strength (pp. 353 to 354).13 Bleris and colleagues saw a miRNA-based loop in mammalian cells lose adaptation at high copy number, which they attributed to saturation of the RNA-induced silencing complex (p. 5).14 Du and colleagues' miRNA modules compensate total mRNA only while the regulator stays below its dissociation constant (Data S1, p. 16).15 No source used here says which design a natural circuit uses.

Both loops forget the input only inside one regime. Lecture 10 asks what a loop must remember to forget it everywhere.

Place each implementation of a feedforward loop on its binding pair's regime map before trusting its effective rate law. A lightly loaded degrader adapts while it stays free and below its dissociation constant, and a repressor while it exceeds both its dissociation constant and its promoter total. The input moves the controller total, so the two designs fail at opposite ends.

7A finite promoter pool lets monomeric repressors oscillate

A ring of three repressors can oscillate only when each link amplifies enough. A Hill law caps the amplification at its Hill coefficient, so a Hill ring of monomeric repressors cannot oscillate. A finite promoter pool supplies the amplification by titration.

The repressilator is a ring of three genes, each repressing the next. Its behavior is a sustained rhythm, a stable cycle of the closed loop. The question is whether three monomeric repressors can sustain one. Monomeric here means one binding unit per site, with no cooperativity at the promoter.

A symmetric ring of three loses its steady state to a rotating mode when the loop gain per node exceeds 2. Linearize a symmetric ring with uniform removal at its steady state. Each node relaxes at rate 1, in units of the removal rate, and is pushed by its upstream neighbour with slope −H-H. What was known, and what this analysis adds

Loinger and Biham already found oscillation in deterministic rate equations for a monomeric repressor on one promoter site, once bound repressor is degraded, as cell division does (Sec. Together they make the oscillation analyzable, and so generalizable. A simulation alone cannot say why the ring oscillates.

Read the contrasts

Five contrasts of three kinds. Each kind names a check to run before an effective rate law is trusted with a behavior.

8Three ways a shortcut falls short

An effective rate law can hide a difference between mechanisms, forbid a behavior a mechanism produces, or misjudge how a capacity scales. Moving a total and closing the loop exposes each.

Every contrast in this lecture began from agreement. Each effective rate law was derived as a limit of its binding-catalysis model and matched to it, and it held where it was derived. The contrasts appeared only when a total moved, and they became behaviors only when the loop closed. A Hill law is not wrong. It answers a narrower question than the one a cell poses.

The five contrasts are of three kinds, and each kind comes with a check. Take them in turn.

A hidden difference means two mechanisms share one effective rate law. The activator and the repressor share one induction curve in free inducer (§3). Degradation and repression share one ratio of input to controller (§6). The law is right for each mechanism where it was matched, and so it cannot tell them apart. The check is to ask which other mechanism the law also describes, then move a total that the two treat differently.

A forbidden behavior means the law's form rules out what the mechanism allows. A Hill law caps the log-slope at its coefficient. So a two-site receptor in free ligand cannot pass nH=2n_H=2 (§4), and a Hill ring of monomeric repressors cannot reach the loop gain of 2 it needs (§7). Titration of a total is not capped by any coefficient. The check is to compare the gain a behavior needs with the gain the law can supply.

A misjudged scaling means the law describes a small circuit and misjudges how its capacity grows. A Hill toggle and MultiFate-2 are both multistable switches. Yet N Hill genes hold at most N + 1 states, while a shared partner pool holds hundreds by N = 12 (§5). The check is to count the stable states as a function of size, from the closed loop.

The removal variant decided three of the five outcomes. Uniform dilution made the steady state read the inducer total in §3, let partner competition set MultiFate's count in §5, and let the monomer ring oscillate in §7. Removing free species only protected the bound state each time, and it changed the answer each time: one shared steady state in §3, decoupled factors in §5 and no oscillation in §7.

The habit to keep is the five steps of §2. Write the circuit as binding and catalysis, with its totals, its observable and its removal variant. Derive the effective rate law as a limit, and match the two. Then move a total the way a cell does, close the loop, and read the behavior from the dynamics.

Each behavior returns later in the course, in one of three lectures. Adaptation returns in Lecture 10. Both feedforward loops of §6 forget the input only inside one regime, and Lecture 10 asks what a loop must remember to forget it everywhere, which leads to integral feedback. Regime geometry returns in Lecture 11. Every threshold and failure here sat on a regime boundary of one binding pair, the relaxation cycle of §7 is a walk through the regimes of three promoter pools, and Lecture 11 generalizes these regime maps into reaction-order geometry. Computation returns in Lecture 12. The repressor's comparison of two totals, the titration threshold and the choice among stable states are its primitives.

Before trusting an effective rate law with a behavior, run three checks. Does another mechanism share the law? Does the law cap a gain the behavior needs? Does its count of states scale like the mechanism's? Answer each by moving a total and closing the loop.

Beyond the lecture clock

Questions to develop further

Open threads for extension essays and later lectures. They are not walked through in the room.

  1. Noise. Multistability with noise becomes multimodality, and switching between states sets how long a cell type lasts. How do MultiFate's stable states fare in a stochastic model at realistic copy numbers, and does uniform dilution lengthen or shorten their lifetimes? Lectures 5 and 6 supply the tools.
  2. Other oscillators. Relaxation oscillators, circadian clocks, the cell cycle and bacterial replication initiation all keep time. Which of them run on titration of a finite pool, and does each need bound partners to be diluted, as the ring of §7 does?
  3. Natural circuits. Which induced repressors bind their inducer at least ten times more tightly than their operator, as the threshold of §3 requires? Which natural feedforward loops degrade their output and which repress it? No source used here settles either question.
  4. Protected repressors. The ring of §7 stops oscillating when bound repressor is spared from removal. How far are DNA-bound repressors protected from active degradation in cells, and how much of the window survives a protease that acts on free repressor only?
  5. Combined arms. A cell could use degradation and repression together to widen the adaptive range of §6. Where does the combination still fail?
