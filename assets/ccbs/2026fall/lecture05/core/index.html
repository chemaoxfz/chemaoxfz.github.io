Stochastic dynamics: the master equation and Gillespie

Under a femtoliter, regularity is a lucky guess. The chemical master equation, exact sampling, and the two moments that answer most questions.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · lecture 5 · core page · 2026-09-15 (Tue) 10:40–12:15 · students present, lecturer-backed

Molecules are counted, not measured, and at cellular copy numbers the counting is the physics rather than an error bar on it. This lecture builds the chemical master equation as the exact description of a reaction network, shows that it is a Markov chain on the counting lattice, and gives the algorithm that samples it exactly. Then it asks how much of the distribution you actually need, and finds that two moments usually suffice.

Part 1

The counting regime

When randomness is a property of the design, not a measurement error.

1Why noise becomes physics

At ~1 molecule per femtoliter, the mean-field picture loses its guarantee — and the Repressilator's fluctuations stop being a curiosity.

Start from the numbers of lectures 1 and 2: a 1 µM regulator over a 1 fL volume is order one molecule per cell. When a cell has a handful of copies, every event — transcription, binding, degradation — is a discrete jump, and averages over a cell population hide the single-cell truth that gene expression is bursty and noisy.1 The lecture's opening example is the Repressilator (Elowitz & Leibler): an engineered oscillator whose single-cell trajectories show substantial fluctuations — the deterministic equations predict oscillation; the stochastic system does not stay on that trajectory, and the fluctuations are not error, they are the system.

2The chemical master equation

The state is a probability distribution; the master equation moves probability between counts.

Let pn(t) be the probability of n copies. For the birth–death process (production rate k, degradation rate μ per molecule) the master equation is the account book of probability flow:2

dpn/dt = k(n+1)pn+1 − knpn + μpn−1 − μpn (1)

Each term: probability leaving state n (rightward/leftward), and entering it from the neighbors. General form: a linear ODE on an infinite-dimensional space — the deterministic ODE is recovered as the mean and becomes exact only in the large-volume limit; the distribution is the full object. There is no escaping the discreteness here: states are integers, and "n = 0" is a special state (it can absorb probability but not produce it) — the seed of extinction, below.

Part 2

Sampling

Exact realizations of the master equation: the Gillespie algorithm, its correctness and its cost.

3The Gillespie algorithm

Two random draws, one exact trajectory: when does the next reaction occur, and which reaction is it?

At state x, each reaction i has a propensity ai(x) (its rate law, in molecules-per-time units). The total propensity a0 = Σai; the inter-event time is exponential,3

τ ~ Exp(a0),   reaction j chosen with probability aj/a0 (2)

Advance time by τ, apply j, repeat. The derivation walks through all of it: the probability that no reaction happens in dt, the exponential waiting time for each reaction, and the competition-weighted selection. The essential virtue: the algorithm is exact — it samples the master equation's distribution with no approximation, which is why it is the reference method to check everything else (moment closure, tau-leaping, ODE limits) against.

4Exactness, speed, and what it costs

Exactness comes at a price — one event at a time. That's the lesson, and the reason approximations exist.

The exact algorithm is event-by-event, so a cell at 10⁵ molecules and ms timescales samples 10⁶+ events per second — expensive. Approximations, in order of faithfulness: tau-leaping (bundle many events per step), moment closure (part 3: track the first moments, close them approximately), and the LNA (part 3's systematic Gaussian approximation). For the course, the practise rule is: Gillespie when counts are small (regulators); ODEs when they're large (metabolites); moment equations when all you need is the noise level.

Part 3

Chains and moments

The master equation is a Markov chain. Stationary distributions, first passages, and the two moments that answer 'how noisy is this cell?'

5Chains, transition matrices, stationary distributions

A CTMC is a master equation; a discrete chain is a transition matrix; the stationary distribution solves the balance equations.

Markov chains are the same jump-process mathematics as the CME of part 1, read differently. In continuous time (CTMC) the master equation dp/dt = Qp is the same object as equation (1) above; in discrete time the transition matrix πPt+1 = πPt repeats. The stationary distribution solves the balance equations, and the finest version is detailed balance (1) per pair of states: πiqij = πjqji. The lecture's summary slide covers exactly this line: CTMC master equation → steady state → detailed balance → hitting & first-passage times.3

6Hitting times and first passages

How long before a chain gets somewhere? One linear system: Q^j τ^j = 0 with boundary conditions, and often a closed form.

The first-passage problem: the expected time to first reach a set of states solves a linear system, Q(j)τ(j) = 0 with boundary values, and the notes carry the general recurrence plus the worked rotational example (the qij/|qii| structure and the "wait" annotation: the expected time stays put — a standard random-walk result) and the finite-state projection remark for solving the CME by chain methods.1 Compute: small systems by hand (two or three states), bigger ones by sparse linear algebra. And note the two face of this in the course: the same computation, run on a gene-state chain, gives switching/first-expression times — the bridge from the chain picture to the stochastic events of part 1.

7Mean = variance: Poisson universality

For a birth–death process in steady state the distribution is Poisson: mean equals variance, CV = 1/√λ.

Solve the steady state of (1) for the crude birth–death process: the distribution is Poisson with rate λ = μ/k.3

P{X = k} = λke−λ/k!,   ⟨X⟩ = Var(X) = λ,   CV = 1/√λ (3)

Two consequences to keep. First, the Poisson reference: if the measured variance exceeds the mean, the process is not Poisson — something (bursting, feedback, noise propagation) is at work; this is precisely the Fano factor F = Var/⟨X⟩ and its departure from 1. Second, the size of noise in biology's currency: CV ~ 1/√λ — noise goes down as copy number goes up, which is itself a design constraint: cells that need precision buy copies.

8Linear noise analysis

Linearity in the noise: the covariance of a linear-noise system obeys a Lyapunov equation, Mη + ηMᵀ = D.

The general machinery: write the mean-field ODE, add small deterministic noise; the propagator matrix M comes from the Jacobian of the deterministic system, the noise matrix D from the net stoichiometric fluxes, and the steady-state covariance η solves the Lyapunov equation2

Mη + ηMᵀ = D (4)

This is the workhorse: any CRN with a stable fixed point gets a systematic noise level, without simulation. (The 2025 notes develop the index-heavy version and end mid-example — treat the margins: the classic pitfalls are cross-correlation terms and the sign conventions on the stoichiometric matrix; the homework in this block is exactly to carry (4) through one example twice: by hand and by simulation.)
