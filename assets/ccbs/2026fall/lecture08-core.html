The cell as a biomachine

Binding converts controlled totals into molecular states. The activities of those states determine how the totals change.

Lecture 8 · core · 95-minute session · Fall 2026

We can now build the molecular response functions that the rest of the course will use. Lecture 7 established when a binding subnetwork can be described by equilibrium weights. Here we use that result to determine the states present at a given total concentration, then let their activities produce the dynamics. The complete example will predict how an enzyme's substrate inventory changes when supply increases.

The exposition derives the construction and solves all three inherited binding problems. The lineage traces its conceptual origins. The extension applies the same construction to an ion-coupled transporter.

1The experimental knob changes a total

An equilibrium occupancy becomes a prediction only after we know the available free ligand. Lecture 7's two-state result uses a free concentration. Expressing or supplying a molecule sets its amount across free and bound forms. If binding takes a substantial share of that amount, the free concentration must be calculated from the inventory.

A constituent total counts every modeled state containing that constituent. Consider the elementary binding pair A+B⇌koffkonCABA+B\xrightleftharpoons[k_{\mathrm{off}}]{k_{\mathrm{on}}}C_{AB}. Bare A and B denote free concentrations, CABC_{AB} denotes the complex concentration, and the controllable totals are qA=A+CABq_A=A+C_{AB} and qB=B+CABq_B=B+C_{AB}. Each complex uses one unit from each pool.

The active state determines the readout. A catalyst may act while bound, giving a flux proportional to CABC_{AB}. An activator sequestered by B may act only while free, giving an output that depends on A. Before calculating a regulatory curve, specify which state the experiment reads.

Write the total of each constituent and identify the active state before treating a supplied concentration as a regulatory input.

2One binding pair can be solved without an infinite reservoir

The equilibrium equation and two inventories determine the complex. Define the dissociation constant Kd=koff/konK_d=k_{\mathrm{off}}/k_{\mathrm{on}}, which has concentration units. Equilibrium gives KdCAB=ABK_dC_{AB}=AB. Replacing the free concentrations using the two totals yields

KdCAB=(qA−CAB)(qB−CAB).K_dC_{AB}=(q_A-C_{AB})(q_B-C_{AB}).

The physical bounds select one root of the quadratic. The complex must satisfy 0≤CAB≤min⁡(qA,qB)0\le C_{AB}\le\min(q_A,q_B). With T=qA+qB+KdT=q_A+q_B+K_d, the acceptable root is

CAB=T−T2−4qAqB2=2qAqBT+T2−4qAqB.C_{AB}=\frac{T-\sqrt{T^2-4q_Aq_B}}{2}=\frac{2q_Aq_B}{T+\sqrt{T^2-4q_Aq_B}}.

The other root requires more complex than the pools contain. Reject it using the smaller-total bound, then recover free A and B from their inventories. Both total amounts matter whenever binding appreciably depletes either partner.

The rationalized expression is useful for numerical calculation. In weak binding, the first form subtracts nearly equal numbers. The second form evaluates the same physical root without that cancellation.

The equilibrium constant carries a specific kinetic assumption. The enzyme complex balance from Lecture 2 instead gives KM=(koff+kcat)/konK_M=(k_{\mathrm{off}}+k_{\mathrm{cat}})/k_{\mathrm{on}} when catalytic escape contributes to complex removal. Our KdK_d belongs to binding equilibrium. The two agree approximately when catalytic escape is negligible compared with dissociation.1

Use the total bounds to select the physical binding root, and distinguish an equilibrium dissociation constant from a catalytic complex-balance constant.

3One pair gives saturation, a bottleneck, or a threshold

Saturation follows when binding barely depletes the input partner. If qA≪Kdq_A\ll K_d, then CAB/B=A/Kd≤qA/Kd≪1C_{AB}/B=A/K_d\le q_A/K_d\ll1. Thus B≈qBB\approx q_B throughout the input range, giving

CAB≈qAqBKd+qB.C_{AB}\approx q_A\frac{q_B}{K_d+q_B}.

The non-saturating end of this curve is effectively mass action. For qB≪Kdq_B\ll K_d as well, CAB≈qAqB/KdC_{AB}\approx q_Aq_B/K_d. If the complex acts at frequency kcatk_{\mathrm{cat}}, the flux is approximately (kcat/Kd)qAqB(k_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_d)q_Aq_B. This is the same effective-rate interpretation developed in Lecture 2, with the binding-equilibrium constant appropriate to the present reduction.

Tight binding creates a smaller-pool bottleneck when the complex is active. As KdK_d becomes small, the complex approaches min⁡(qA,qB)\min(q_A,q_B). Adding more of the already abundant partner then does little. The limiting constituent sets the amount of active complex.

The same tight binding creates a threshold when free A is active. Accounting gives A≈max⁡(qA−qB,0)A\approx\max(q_A-q_B,0). Below the matching point, B sequesters added A. Above it, additional A remains free. This is the molecular-titration mechanism of ultrasensitive regulation.2

Finite affinity rounds the threshold on a calculable concentration scale. At equal totals qA=qB=qq_A=q_B=q, the free species satisfy A=BA=B and A2=Kd(q−A)A^2=K_d(q-A). For tight binding, this gives A≈KdqA\approx\sqrt{K_dq}. A finite-affinity binding pair therefore approximates a rectifier with a rounded neighborhood near equivalence.

A one-site reporter can be sharp

Let a trace reporter read free A through h=A/(KR+A)h=A/(K_R+A). At half response, A=KRA=K_R, so qA,1/2=KR+qBKR/(Kd+KR)q_{A,1/2}=K_R+q_BK_R/(K_d+K_R). With qB=10q_B=10, KR=1K_R=1, and Kd=0.01K_d=0.01, all in micromolar, the midpoint is 10.901 µM.

The local midpoint Hill slope measures the change in the response logit per fractional change in total activator. Differentiating the inventory at A=KRA=K_R gives

nH=dlog⁡[h/(1−h)]dlog⁡qA∣h=1/2=qA,1/2KR[1+qBKd/(Kd+KR)2]≈9.93.n_H=\left.\frac{d\log[h/(1-h)]}{d\log q_A}\right|_{h=1/2}=\frac{q_{A,1/2}}{K_R\left[1+q_BK_d/(K_d+K_R)^2\right]}\approx9.93.

The sharpness comes from sequestration before the one-site readout. A synthetic yeast experiment tested this mechanism using a promoter with one activator binding site.3

Choose the readout and compare pool sizes with the binding scale to predict saturation, a bottleneck, or a rounded free-species threshold.

4Binding regulates activity by changing which states are present

Occupancy alone does not specify a production rate. Suppose a gene exchanges between free GG and bound CGRC_{GR}, with total qGq_G. Let the two states produce transcript T at effective frequencies k0k_0 and k1k_1. Their composite transcription processes and rate law are

G⇝G+T,CGR⇝CGR+T,vtx=k0G+k1CGR.G\rightsquigarrow G+T,\qquad C_{GR}\rightsquigarrow C_{GR}+T,\qquad v_{\mathrm{tx}}=k_0G+k_1C_{GR}.

A binding change regulates the output according to the relative activities of the states. If the bound fraction is θ=CGR/qG\theta=C_{GR}/q_G, then vtx/qG=k0(1−θ)+k1θv_{\mathrm{tx}}/q_G=k_0(1-\theta)+k_1\theta. Increasing binding activates when k1>k0k_1>k_0, represses when k1<k0k_1<k_0, and has no effect on this output when the two activities match.

The same construction extends to many conformations and complexes. Each state has an equilibrium weight and an activity for each modeled process. The total activity is the sum vα=∑jkαjxjv_\alpha=\sum_j k_{\alpha j}x_j. Allosteric ensembles and equilibrium promoter models supply important examples, with their own kinetic conditions for relating occupancy to output.45

Compute regulation by weighting each molecular state's abundance by its activity, so affinity and catalytic effect remain distinct model inputs.

5Compose modules through their shared molecular pools

Adding a binding partner changes the amount of signal available to an existing module. Let repressor R bind either a gene G or a sponge T, whose total is qspongeq_{\mathrm{sponge}}. The gene responds to free R, while both complexes draw from total repressor. With dissociation constants KGRK_{GR} and KRTK_{RT}, the three balances give

qR=R+qGRKGR+R+qspongeRKRT+R,GqG=KGRKGR+R.q_R=R+\frac{q_GR}{K_{GR}+R}+\frac{q_{\mathrm{sponge}}R}{K_{RT}+R},\qquad \frac{G}{q_G}=\frac{K_{GR}}{K_{GR}+R}.

The shared balance determines the whole response without fitting a new repression curve. Its right side increases strictly with R, so one scalar solve gives the free signal for any three totals. At half repression, R=KGRR=K_{GR}, which immediately gives

qR,1/2=KGR+qG2+qspongeKGRKRT+KGR.q_{R,1/2}=K_{GR}+\frac{q_G}{2}+\frac{q_{\mathrm{sponge}}K_{GR}}{K_{RT}+K_{GR}}.

A tight sponge shifts the threshold by approximately its own total amount. When KRT≪KGRK_{RT}\ll K_{GR}, nearly every sponge molecule is occupied at the repression midpoint. The added repressor required is therefore approximately qspongeq_{\mathrm{sponge}}. Below the threshold, the exact balance automatically prevents a negative available-repressor concentration.

Fast binding can also load a changing upstream signal. As a signal changes, downstream complexes must fill or empty. This consumes part of the change in the total and slows the free signal under appropriate production and removal laws. Retroactivity makes this effect explicit in interconnected biochemical systems.6 The exposition derives that dynamic load and solves both competitive and series binding.

Connect binding modules by combining their inventories, then solve for the shared free species before evaluating either module's activity.

6The binding map closes a general dynamical model

The shared-pool calculation can now be placed inside Lecture 6's exact total dynamics. Let x collect all species, L count their binding constituents, and q=Lxq=Lx. If Γbind\Gamma_{\mathrm{bind}} and Γcat\Gamma_{\mathrm{cat}} are the binding and remaining reaction stoichiometries, then LΓbind=0L\Gamma_{\mathrm{bind}}=0 removes binding fluxes and gives

q˙=LΓcatvcat(x)+Lr(x,t)−λq.\dot q=L\Gamma_{\mathrm{cat}}v_{\mathrm{cat}}(x)+Lr(x,t)-\lambda q.

The boundary terms retain their physical readouts after projection. Here r contains species-specific supply and removal rates, and λ is a uniform growth-dilution rate. Uniform dilution acts on the whole total. Export of only a free species must still use that free concentration.

A compatible ideal binding layer supplies one state for each positive set of totals. Assume a well-mixed ideal solution, positive compatible binding constants, a complete constituent inventory, and one explicit free species for each independent constituent. The binding equations and totals then determine a unique positive state xeq(q;b)x^{\mathrm{eq}}(q;b), where b collects the binding weights. The exposition proves this by minimizing a strictly convex potential, in the setting of balanced reaction-network theory.7

State activities turn that equilibrium map into a closed total equation. Collect the linear state-activity coefficients in KcatK_{\mathrm{cat}}, and use Γ^\widehat\Gamma for the corresponding projected changes of totals. With rqr_q denoting the projected boundary and dilution terms,

q˙=Γ^Kcatxeq(q;b)+rq.\boxed{\dot q=\widehat\Gamma K_{\mathrm{cat}}x^{\mathrm{eq}}(q;b)+r_q.}

The algebraic state moves as the totals evolve. A complex does not remain constant merely because its binding equation has been replaced by an equilibrium relation. The exact projection precedes the approximation. Fast tracking of the compatible equilibrium map is the additional assumption that makes the reduced dynamics accurate.

The equilibrium license from Lecture 7

The driven four-state promoter, Hopfield proofreading loop, and driven adaptive-sensing examples cannot be absorbed wholesale into this equilibrium layer. Their circulating steps must remain in the model or receive a stationary, nonequilibrium reduction. Slow catalytic conversion may itself be reversible.

Build a closed total model by combining exact constituent projection with a justified equilibrium map and explicit state activities.

7Build an enzyme with supply, reverse conversion, and export

An enzyme that binds product shares its finite pool between two competing forms. Use five species: free enzyme E, free substrate S, free product P, substrate complex CESC_{ES}, and product complex CEPC_{EP}. Resolve conversion between the two bound forms as one reversible chemical step. Supply adds S at rate u and export removes free P at rate koutPk_{\mathrm{out}}P.

Two changing totals describe the reduced dynamics when enzyme amount is fixed. Define qE=E+CES+CEPq_E=E+C_{ES}+C_{EP}, qS=S+CESq_S=S+C_{ES}, and qP=P+CEPq_P=P+C_{EP}. Forward and reverse catalytic frequencies are kfk_f and krk_r, so

J=kfCES−krCEP,q˙E=0,q˙S=u−J,q˙P=J−koutP.J=k_fC_{ES}-k_rC_{EP},\qquad \dot q_E=0,\quad\dot q_S=u-J,\quad\dot q_P=J-k_{\mathrm{out}}P.

The binding reconstruction is one monotone scalar equation. Let KSK_S and KPK_P be the substrate and product dissociation constants. Equilibrium and their respective totals give

E+qSEKS+E+qPEKP+E=qE,S=qSKSKS+E,P=qPKPKP+E.E+\frac{q_SE}{K_S+E}+\frac{q_PE}{K_P+E}=q_E,\qquad S=\frac{q_SK_S}{K_S+E},\qquad P=\frac{q_PK_P}{K_P+E}.

Each time step now has a definite physical calculation. Solve for E between zero and qEq_E, reconstruct S, P and the two complexes, calculate J, then advance the totals. Neither substrate nor product is assumed to be an infinite reservoir. This is the shared-pool construction of §5 used inside a complete dynamical model.

Reduce the open reversible enzyme to two changing totals and one scalar binding solve while retaining product competition and selective export.

8Predict the inventory required after supply increases

Steady operation fixes the outgoing free product before it fixes any complex. Both changing totals must stop changing, so J=u=koutPJ=u=k_{\mathrm{out}}P. Thus P=u/koutP=u/k_{\mathrm{out}}. Binding gives CEP=EP/KPC_{EP}=EP/K_P, and the net catalytic balance gives CES=u/kf+(kr/kf)EP/KPC_{ES}=u/k_f+(k_r/k_f)EP/K_P.

The finite enzyme inventory then determines free enzyme and substrate. Substitute those two complexes into qE=E+CES+CEPq_E=E+C_{ES}+C_{EP} to obtain

E=qE−u/kf1+(1+kr/kf)P/KP,S=KS(ukfE+krPkfKP).E=\frac{q_E-u/k_f}{1+(1+k_r/k_f)P/K_P},\qquad S=K_S\left(\frac{u}{k_fE}+\frac{k_rP}{k_fK_P}\right).

A finite steady state requires supply below the enzyme's forward capacity. The numerator requires u<kfqEu<k_fq_E. As supply approaches that ceiling, the free enzyme available for substrate becomes small and the required substrate inventory grows. Slower export increases product occupancy and reverse flux, also increasing the substrate needed for the same net throughput.

Set qE=KS=KP=1 μMq_E=K_S=K_P=1\,\mu\mathrm M, kf=1 s−1k_f=1\,\mathrm s^{-1}, kr=0.2 s−1k_r=0.2\,\mathrm s^{-1}, and kout=0.3 s−1k_{\mathrm{out}}=0.3\,\mathrm s^{-1}. The reduction can be checked without changing its equilibrium affinities. Accelerate both association and dissociation together while keeping all slow rates fixed. For the stated 60 s experiment, increasing both binding speeds tenfold reduces the maximum total error against the full five-species dynamics from about 0.034 to 0.0034 µM. Accelerating only one binding pair leaves a persistent error if both pairs are eliminated. The research calculation and exposition give the full comparison.

Use the total balances and reconstructed complexes to predict throughput, free concentrations, and total inventories after a supply or export change.

9Opposed catalytic activities can amplify a small imbalance

Two binding-catalysis arms can redistribute a conserved target sharply. Let enzyme E convert X to Y and enzyme F convert Y to X. Define qX=X+CEXq_X=X+C_{EX} and qY=Y+CFYq_Y=Y+C_{FY}, with qtarget=qX+qYq_{\mathrm{target}}=q_X+q_Y fixed. If C(qa,qb,K)C(q_a,q_b,K) denotes the exact pair function from §2, the reduced cycle is

q˙Y=kEC(qE,qtarget−qY,KE)−kFC(qF,qY,KF).\dot q_Y=k_EC(q_E,q_{\mathrm{target}}-q_Y,K_E)-k_FC(q_F,q_Y,K_F).

Saturation makes the balance sensitive to the two catalytic capacities. When both enzymes are saturated over most of the target range, their fluxes are close to VE=kEqEV_E=k_Eq_E and VF=kFqFV_F=k_Fq_F. A small capacity imbalance moves target toward one form until one arm loses saturation. This is zero-order ultrasensitivity.8

This sharp response has one stable steady state in the stated single-cycle model. Increasing qYq_Y decreases the forward flux and increases the reverse flux, so their difference crosses zero once with negative slope. Multiple stable states require a suitable dynamical feedback structure. Binding nonlinearity helps shape that structure, even though the binding equilibrium at any fixed set of totals remains unique.

The maintained chemical drive belongs in the model interpretation. In a phosphorylation-dephosphorylation cycle, nucleotide and phosphate reservoirs support the opposed reactions. Phosphatase action is a different chemical process from reversing kinase chemistry. The effective one-way activities here assume those reservoirs and negligible reverse activities on the modeled timescale.

Diagnose zero-order amplification from opposed saturated fluxes, and assess multistability separately from the steepness of the response curve.

10Binding constrains which total-concentration orders are possible

A local logarithmic slope quantifies how strongly a total regulates an active state. Define ρC,qA=∂log⁡CAB/∂log⁡qA\rho_{C,q_A}=\partial\log C_{AB}/\partial\log q_A, with the other total and affinity held fixed. A value near one means a one-percent increase in the input gives about a one-percent increase in complex. A value near zero means that input has little local effect.

The exact pair equation gives both orders without solving the quadratic again. Differentiate KdCAB=ABK_dC_{AB}=AB together with the two total balances. With D=Kd+A+BD=K_d+A+B, the result is

D dCAB=B dqA+A dqB,ρC,qA=Kd+BD,ρC,qB=Kd+AD.D\,dC_{AB}=B\,dq_A+A\,dq_B,\qquad \rho_{C,q_A}=\frac{K_d+B}{D},\quad\rho_{C,q_B}=\frac{K_d+A}{D}.

The two orders form a positive-weight mixture of three limiting behaviors. Rearranging gives

(ρC,qA,ρC,qB)=AD(0,1)+BD(1,0)+KdD(1,1).(\rho_{C,q_A},\rho_{C,q_B})=\frac{A}{D}(0,1)+\frac{B}{D}(1,0)+\frac{K_d}{D}(1,1).

The geometry describes all operating points of this mechanism. Each complex order lies between zero and one, while their sum lies between one and two. The free-A readout from §3 obeys different bounds and can be ultrasensitive, so it is not constrained by the complex's triangle. If a single complex catalyzes a flux at constant frequency, its flux has the same total orders as that complex.

These derivatives connect molecular architecture to dynamics. The logarithmic responses enter the ordinary Jacobian after multiplication by the appropriate flux and pool scales. Lecture 11 generalizes this one-pair geometry to larger binding networks. Lecture 9 first uses the response functions themselves to build dynamical motifs.

Differentiate binding with respect to the controlled totals to obtain structural limits on local regulation, then use the correct readout's orders in dynamical analysis.

11The construction becomes a model for cellular functions

We now have a procedure that reaches an intervention prediction. Account for the molecular constituents, reconstruct the compatible binding states at their totals, evaluate the states' activities, and evolve the projected totals. The open enzyme shows every step. The sponge and modification cycle show how the same construction creates regulatory behavior through shared pools.

The remaining course asks what functions these models can realize. Lecture 9 connects the responses into multistable and oscillatory motifs. Lecture 11 studies the set of total orders that an architecture can achieve. Lecture 12 turns the finite-affinity threshold into molecular computational units and asks how well they perform. The transporter extension demonstrates a different physical output, with cargo and ion currents reconstructed from explicit molecular states.

A larger cell model still needs evidence for its molecular architecture. Within a chosen state list, binding weights and state activities have distinct roles. Discovering the relevant interactions, spatial variables and kinetic states remains part of model building. Lecture 16 returns to that inference problem when considering the cell as a whole.

What binding regulates

Catalytic stoichiometry determines which totals an activity changes. Binding determines how much of each active state exists at those totals. Together they turn molecular interactions into a calculable dynamical model.

Use the total-state-activity construction as the molecular foundation for testing a proposed cellular control or computation mechanism.
