The cell as a biomachine

Three tools for biochemical regulation: build the binding-catalysis model, identify the dominant pools, and derive the response.

Lecture 8 · core · 95-minute session · Fall 2026

Lecture 7 ended with an enzyme that binds substrate or product. When binding equilibrates rapidly, their free concentrations determine how the enzyme is allocated. But experiments often control total amounts. Binding changes the free concentrations themselves. How do we solve that allocation problem, and how does its solution explain regulation?

We will follow one binding pair all the way from its molecular states to its regulatory responses. Then we will add a competitor, a dimer and a second regulator. The same three tools will explain each construction: count constituents, identify the dominant contributions, and read the activity of the resulting states.

The exposition develops the algebra and numerical methods. The lineage traces the ideas behind this construction. The extension applies it to a transporter with a physical current.

1How cells select chemical activity

A chemist can change the conditions around a reaction. Temperature, solvent, pressure, concentration and catalysts are all possible controls. A living cell must coordinate many reactions in a shared environment while remaining viable. It needs ways to change selected activities without changing every reaction in the same way.

Molecular states provide selective control. A substrate-bound enzyme can catalyze a conversion that free enzyme cannot. A repressor-bound promoter can produce less transcript than its unbound state. A ligand can redistribute a receptor between conformations with different activities. In each case, the cell changes a process by changing which molecular states are present.

Binding-catalysis models turn this observation into a reusable construction. The binding part allocates constituents among molecular states. The catalytic part assigns activities to those states. “Catalysis” here includes an effective production process such as transcription when its internal steps are not resolved. Its rate law must still be specified.

This construction retains the information needed to connect modules. Two regulatory curves may look independent when measured separately, yet draw from the same protein pool when combined. An explicit state list records that shared material. State activities then determine how redistribution changes the output.

Our first tool uses the equilibrium reduction already justified in Lectures 6 and 7. We assume that the selected binding subsystem relaxes rapidly and has compatible equilibrium constants. Driven transitions or slow conformations remain explicit when those assumptions fail. Within this scope, we can specify the equilibrium allocation without listing every microscopic route by which a complex forms.

Model biochemical regulation by separating molecular allocation from the activities of the allocated states.

Tool 1 · Construct the binding-catalysis model

2Count the constituents in every state

Start with the reversible enzyme from Lecture 7. Its free species are enzyme E, substrate S and product P. Its complexes are CESC_{ES} and CEPC_{EP}. Binding and catalytic conversion are resolved as

E+S⇌kS,−kS,+CES,E+P⇌kP,−kP,+CEP,CES⇌kcat,−kcat,+CEP.E+S\xrightleftharpoons[k_{S,-}]{k_{S,+}}C_{ES},\qquad E+P\xrightleftharpoons[k_{P,-}]{k_{P,+}}C_{EP},\qquad C_{ES}\xrightleftharpoons[k_{\mathrm{cat},-}]{k_{\mathrm{cat},+}}C_{EP}.

Binding preserves three inventories. Each complex contains one enzyme, and each contains either one substrate or one product. Thus

qE=E+CES+CEP,qS=S+CES,qP=P+CEP.q_E=E+C_{ES}+C_{EP},\qquad q_S=S+C_{ES},\qquad q_P=P+C_{EP}.

Collect these counts in the composition matrix LL. With the species order and total order shown below, each column describes one species and each row counts one constituent:

x=(E,S,P,CES,CEP)T,q=(qE,qS,qP)T,q=Lx.x=(E,S,P,C_{ES},C_{EP})^{\mathsf T},\qquad q=(q_E,q_S,q_P)^{\mathsf T},\qquad q=Lx.

The graph scales directly to larger binding systems. For dd reference constituents and rr additional states, order the n=d+rn=d+r species with the free reference species first. Then

L=[ Id∣L(c) ],qi=xi(a)+∑j=1rLij(c)xj(c).L=[\,I_d\mid L^{(c)}\,],\qquad q_i=x_i^{(a)}+\sum_{j=1}^r L^{(c)}_{ij}x_j^{(c)}.

The superscripts distinguish reference species from additional states. An entry Lij(c)L^{(c)}_{ij} counts copies of constituent i in state j. A dimer therefore contributes twice to its protein total. The corresponding graph edge has weight two.

Here “atomic” means a reference constituent of the binding model. It need not be a chemical element or an indivisible molecule. Substrate and product are separate constituents for binding accounting because binding preserves their identities. Catalytic conversion changes those identities, so it can change their totals.

Two precise versions of atomic accounting

An isomer-atomic matrix has the displayed identity block, nonnegative integer entries in L(c)L^{(c)}, and no zero species column. It permits repeated columns. A faithful-atomic matrix additionally has distinct columns, so distinct species have distinct compositions. Allowing repeats retains conformations or other isomers as distinct states.2

The chosen totals must also contain all independent conservation laws of the binding subsystem. If an actual reaction network has additional conserved pools, include them or restrict the model accordingly. A complex list cannot erase a kinetic obstruction to reaching the proposed equilibrium.

The matrix is a binding conservation law because binding only rearranges these constituents. If Γbind\Gamma_{\mathrm{bind}} contains the binding reaction columns, then LΓbind=0L\Gamma_{\mathrm{bind}}=0. The same matrix need not annihilate catalytic or boundary reactions. This distinction lets the totals become the slow variables.

Specify the states and their constituent counts. The resulting matrix both draws the composition graph and defines the totals preserved by binding.

3Describe binding equilibrium in useful coordinates

For ideal mass-action binding, the complex list determines the form of the equilibrium equations. Their numerical values require binding affinities. For each additional state, define its association coefficient bj>0b_j>0 by

xj(c)=bj∏i=1d(xi(a))Lij(c).x_j^{(c)}=b_j\prod_{i=1}^d\bigl(x_i^{(a)}\bigr)^{L^{(c)}_{ij}}.

For the enzyme, these relations are CES=bESESC_{ES}=b_{ES}ES and CEP=bEPEPC_{EP}=b_{EP}EP. For a dimer they give CRR=bRRR2C_{RR}=b_{RR}R^2. Thus the same composition columns supply the exponents needed to compute every state from the free reference species.

A binary association coefficient has inverse concentration units. Its reciprocal is the dissociation constant. For the enzyme pair,

Kd,S=1bES=kS,−kS,+,Kd,SCES=ES,[Kd,S]=M.K_{d,S}=\frac{1}{b_{ES}}=\frac{k_{S,-}}{k_{S,+}},\qquad K_{d,S}C_{ES}=ES,\qquad [K_{d,S}]=\mathrm{M}.

The same unit holds for dimerization, with the convention Kdim=R2/CRRK_{\mathrm{dim}}=R^2/C_{RR}. For a state containing sj=∑iLij(c)s_j=\sum_iL^{(c)}_{ij} constituents, however, [bj]=M1−sj[b_j]=\mathrm{M}^{1-s_j}. Its reciprocal has concentration units only when sj=2s_j=2. An isomer ratio such as E∗/EE^\ast/E is dimensionless.

These equations define the equilibrium manifold: the set of positive molecular states compatible with the fixed binding affinities. A manifold here is simply the curve or surface of allowed states as the free concentrations vary. Fixing the totals selects an allocation on that surface.

The matrix NN records these equilibrium relations in logarithmic coordinates. Choose a common reference concentration c∘c^\circ and define xˉ=x/c∘\bar x=x/c^\circ. Define dimensionless association coefficients bˉj=bj(c∘)sj−1\bar b_j=b_j(c^\circ)^{s_j-1}. The association equations become

Naalog⁡xˉ=log⁡bˉ,Naa=[ −(L(c))T∣Ir ].N^{\mathrm{aa}}\log\bar x=\log\bar b,\qquad N^{\mathrm{aa}}=[\,-(L^{(c)})^{\mathsf T}\mid I_r\,].

This is a canonical choice of affinity coordinates. The superscript aa labels association affinity. Each row compares one state's concentration with the product of its free constituents. No microscopic assembly route has to be selected to write it. A multi-constituent row expresses an equilibrium relation, not necessarily a single many-body reaction.

The matrices LL and NaaN^{\mathrm{aa}} are a Gale pair. This means that they have complementary full row ranks and satisfy NaaLT=0N^{\mathrm{aa}}L^{\mathsf T}=0. Constituent counts and binding relations describe complementary information: what binding preserves and how equilibrium redistributes it.

For one binding pair, this complementarity can be checked directly. In the order x=(E,S,CES)Tx=(E,S,C_{ES})^{\mathsf T},

L=(101011),Naa=(−1,−1,1),NaaLT=(0,0).L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad N^{\mathrm{aa}}=(-1,-1,1),\qquad N^{\mathrm{aa}}L^{\mathsf T}=(0,0).

The negative entries remove one free enzyme and one free substrate. The positive entry adds their complex. Each constituent count cancels, while the logarithmic relation fixes the complex-to-free-species ratio.

Changing affinity coordinates leaves the physical surface unchanged

Any full-rank binding-coordinate matrix NN with NLT=0NL^{\mathsf T}=0 can be split as N=[N1∣N2]N=[N_1\mid N_2]. Orthogonality gives N1=−N2(L(c))TN_1=-N_2(L^{(c)})^{\mathsf T}. Since N has rank r, the square matrix N2N_2 is invertible. Therefore

N=N2Naa,(L(c))T=−N2−1N1,log⁡k=N2log⁡bˉ.N=N_2N^{\mathrm{aa}},\qquad (L^{(c)})^{\mathsf T}=-N_2^{-1}N_1,\qquad \log k=N_2\log\bar b.

The equation Nlog⁡xˉ=log⁡kN\log\bar x=\log k describes the same physical states. More generally, replacing N by UNUN requires replacing log⁡k\log k by Ulog⁡kU\log k for an invertible U. For one binary pair, reversing the row changes the association coordinate c∘/Kdc^\circ/K_d into the dissociation coordinate Kd/c∘K_d/c^\circ. Changing N while keeping its numerical right side fixed would usually change the model.

Equilibrium therefore removes the need to specify every binding pathway, once the state list and compatible affinities are known. The composition matrix specifies the architecture. The coefficients b specify where its equilibrium surface lies. Microscopic rates are still needed to test whether that surface is reached quickly enough for the reduction.

Use L for constituent accounting and a Gale-complement N for affinity coordinates. Transform the affinities with N to preserve the same equilibrium states.

4Build the binding-catalysis DAE

The totals already remove binding flux by exact accounting. Let Γcat\Gamma_{\mathrm{cat}} contain the catalytic reaction columns, v their fluxes, and rqr_q the contribution of boundary processes to the totals. Before making a fast-equilibrium approximation, the full rate equation gives

q˙=Lx˙=LΓcatv+rq=Γqv+rq,Γq=LΓcat.\dot q=L\dot x=L\Gamma_{\mathrm{cat}}v+r_q=\Gamma_qv+r_q,\qquad \Gamma_q=L\Gamma_{\mathrm{cat}}.

The missing information is the molecular allocation needed to evaluate v. Fast binding supplies it through the equilibrium equations. State-specific catalytic frequencies supply the output law. These pieces form our first tool:

The binding-catalysis differential-algebraic equations

q=Lx,Nlog⁡xˉ=log⁡k,v=Kcatx,q˙=Γqv+rq.\begin{aligned} q&=Lx, & N\log\bar x&=\log k,\\ v&=K^{\mathrm{cat}}x, & \dot q&=\Gamma_qv+r_q. \end{aligned}

The algebraic equations reconstruct the molecular states at the current totals. Call this state xeq(q;b)x^{\mathrm{eq}}(q;b). The differential equations advance those totals using catalytic fluxes and boundary processes. The entries of KcatK^{\mathrm{cat}} are activities per unit time. Thus x and q have concentration units, while v and rqr_q have concentration per time units.

For the enzyme, keep forward and reverse catalytic fluxes as separate nonnegative channels. Using the species order in Figure 2,

v=(v+v−)=(000kcat,+00000kcat,−)⏟Kcatx,Γq=(00−111−1).v=\begin{pmatrix}v_+\\v_-\end{pmatrix} =\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&k_{\mathrm{cat},+}&0\\0&0&0&0&k_{\mathrm{cat},-}\end{pmatrix}}_{K^{\mathrm{cat}}}x,\qquad \Gamma_q=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Supply adds substrate total and a free-product drain removes product total. Their unresolved boundary processes are ∅⇝S\varnothing\rightsquigarrow S and P⇝∅P\rightsquigarrow\varnothing, with rates vinv_{\mathrm{in}} and koutPk_{\mathrm{out}}P. Hence

q˙E=0,q˙S=vin−(v+−v−),q˙P=(v+−v−)−koutP.\dot q_E=0,\qquad \dot q_S=v_{\mathrm{in}}-(v_+-v_-),\qquad \dot q_P=(v_+-v_-)-k_{\mathrm{out}}P.

The binding solve reduces to one equation for free enzyme. Substitute S=qSKd,S/(Kd,S+E)S=q_SK_{d,S}/(K_{d,S}+E) and the corresponding expression for P into the enzyme inventory:

E+qSEKd,S+E+qPEKd,P+E=qE.E+\frac{q_SE}{K_{d,S}+E}+\frac{q_PE}{K_{d,P}+E}=q_E.

The left side increases strictly with E. At zero it is zero, and at E=qEE=q_E it is at least qEq_E. There is one physical root. Recover the free ligands and complexes, then compute both catalytic fluxes. No free concentration has been replaced by a total.

A unique positive allocation also holds for the ideal atomic binding class above with positive totals and affinities. The exposition derives it from a strictly convex potential. This is uniqueness of the fast binding state at specified totals. The slow equations may still have several steady states or other dynamical behaviors.

Which approximation has been made?

The projection q=Lxq=Lx is exact. Replacing binding dynamics by equilibrium requires relaxation faster than the changes in totals and parameters, with compatible equilibrium constants. Replacing free species by their totals is a further approximation that we have not yet made.

The equilibrium constant is Kd=koff/konK_d=k_{\mathrm{off}}/k_{\mathrm{on}}. The catalytic quasi-steady calculation of Lecture 6 instead gives KM=(koff+kcat)/konK_M=(k_{\mathrm{off}}+k_{\mathrm{cat}})/k_{\mathrm{on}} for its simple irreversible enzyme. Both have concentration units, but only the first is a binding-equilibrium constant. They approach each other when catalytic escape is small compared with dissociation.

The binding-catalysis DAE combines exact totals, a justified fast equilibrium allocation and state-specific activities that drive slow change.

Tool 2 · Find the dominant contributions and simplify

5Ask which species carries each total

The exact binding solve gives an allocation. To understand its behavior, ask which species carries most of each pool. We return to one binding pair:

E+S⇌koffkonCES,qE=E+CES,qS=S+CES,KdCES=ES.E+S\xrightleftharpoons[k_{\mathrm{off}}]{k_{\mathrm{on}}}C_{ES},\qquad q_E=E+C_{ES},\qquad q_S=S+C_{ES},\qquad K_dC_{ES}=ES.

The enzyme total can be mostly free or mostly bound. The substrate total has the same two possibilities. These choices give four allocation patterns. We will develop the one-binding poster’s argument step by step.3

A dominance regime is an allocation pattern with enough separation to neglect the smaller contributions. “The largest term” identifies a region. “Much larger than the omitted terms” justifies an approximation. The distinction matters near a boundary where two terms are comparable.

The method has three steps. First, choose the dominant contribution in every total. Second, solve the simplified totals together with the binding equations. Third, substitute the result into the proposed inequalities. The last step gives the conditions under which the approximation is self-consistent.

For a larger complex, compare constituent contributions rather than raw species concentrations. A dimer contributes 2CRR2C_{RR} to the protein pool. A state containing three copies contributes three times its concentration. The entries of L tell us exactly which terms to compare.

Select the dominant terms, solve the reduced equations, then check that the discarded contributions are small.

6Work out the four one-binding patterns

The first three patterns give simple formulas in the two totals. We will derive their validity conditions as we go. All comparisons below concern the same one-binding system, with fixed positive KdK_d.

A. Both totals are carried by free species

Assume E≫CESE\gg C_{ES} and S≫CESS\gg C_{ES}. Then E≈qEE\approx q_E and S≈qSS\approx q_S, so the binding equation gives CES≈qEqS/KdC_{ES}\approx q_Eq_S/K_d. Checking the discarded fractions gives

CESE≈qSKd≪1,CESS≈qEKd≪1.\frac{C_{ES}}{E}\approx\frac{q_S}{K_d}\ll1,\qquad \frac{C_{ES}}{S}\approx\frac{q_E}{K_d}\ll1.

This is the dilute, weak-occupancy regime: both totals are small compared with the dissociation constant.

B. Free enzyme carries its pool, but substrate is bound

Assume E≫CESE\gg C_{ES} and CES≫SC_{ES}\gg S. Now E≈qEE\approx q_E, CES≈qSC_{ES}\approx q_S and S≈KdqS/qES\approx K_dq_S/q_E. The checks are

CESE≈qSqE≪1,SCES≈KdqE≪1.\frac{C_{ES}}{E}\approx\frac{q_S}{q_E}\ll1,\qquad \frac{S}{C_{ES}}\approx\frac{K_d}{q_E}\ll1.

Enzyme must exceed both substrate total and the binding scale. Almost every substrate molecule is bound, while most enzyme remains available.

C. Bound enzyme carries its pool, but substrate remains free

Exchange enzyme and substrate in the previous calculation. Then CES≈qEC_{ES}\approx q_E, S≈qSS\approx q_S and E≈KdqE/qSE\approx K_dq_E/q_S. The required conditions are qS≫qEq_S\gg q_E and qS≫Kdq_S\gg K_d. The enzyme is saturated, and extra substrate mostly remains free.

D. The complex carries both totals

Assume CES≫E,SC_{ES}\gg E,S. Both leading inventories now say qE≈qS≈CESq_E\approx q_S\approx C_{ES}. Those equations locate the regime near matched totals, but they lose the small free pools. To recover the free species, retain the exact difference

qE−qS=E−S.q_E-q_S=E-S.

At exactly matched totals qE=qS=Qq_E=q_S=Q, this gives E=SE=S. The binding equation then becomes E2=Kd(Q−E)E^2=K_d(Q-E). For Q≫KdQ\gg K_d,

E=S≈KdQ,CES≈Q−KdQ.E=S\approx\sqrt{K_dQ},\qquad C_{ES}\approx Q-\sqrt{K_dQ}.

For nearly matched totals, define Q=(qE+qS)/2Q=(q_E+q_S)/2. Strong complex dominance requires Kd≪QK_d\ll Q and ∣qE−qS∣≪Q|q_E-q_S|\ll Q.

A small free pool remains even when nearly all material is complexed. A mismatch in totals comparable with KdQ\sqrt{K_dQ} already changes that residual allocation substantially. This scale will explain the rounded corner of a titration response.

To draw the boundaries in totals, use the exact inventories. On E=CESE=C_{ES}, binding gives S=KdS=K_d, hence qS=Kd+qE/2q_S=K_d+q_E/2. On S=CESS=C_{ES}, the corresponding boundary is qE=Kd+qS/2q_E=K_d+q_S/2. These two equations produce the next map without fitting any response curve.

The exact solution covers the boundaries

Use the exact root when competing contributions are comparable. Substituting the two totals into the binding equation gives KdCES=(qE−CES)(qS−CES)K_dC_{ES}=(q_E-C_{ES})(q_S-C_{ES}). The physical root is

CES=2qEqST+T2−4qEqS,T=qE+qS+Kd.C_{ES}=\frac{2q_Eq_S}{T+\sqrt{T^2-4q_Eq_S}},\qquad T=q_E+q_S+K_d.

This root lies between zero and the smaller total. Recover E=qE−CESE=q_E-C_{ES} and S=qS−CESS=q_S-C_{ES}. The rationalized form avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers in weak binding. All one-binding response curves below use the exact physical allocation.

One binding equation supports four allocation patterns. Their validity follows from the inventories, and the small residual pools control the transition near matching totals.

Tool 3 · Turn allocation into a regulatory response

7Specify the regulatory experiment

A regulatory response requires an input, fixed parameters and an output. Divide the totals into an input block qIq_I that the experiment varies and a parameter block qPq_P that it holds fixed. Keep the affinities b and state activities fixed during that sweep. The resulting map is

qI ⟼ xeq(qI,qP;b) ⟼ v=Kcatxeq(qI,qP;b).q_I\ \longmapsto\ x^{\mathrm{eq}}(q_I,q_P;b)\ \longmapsto\ v=K^{\mathrm{cat}}x^{\mathrm{eq}}(q_I,q_P;b).

Here the subscripts I and P label experimental roles: inputs and parameters. They do not name chemical species. In the enzyme equations, by contrast, qPq_P denotes product total. We will name the held-fixed physical totals explicitly in each example to avoid relying on this shorthand.

The readout decides whether binding activates or represses. Suppose a molecular machine has free state E and bound state CESC_{ES}, with effective production frequencies k0k_0 and k1k_1. The output is

v=k0E+k1CES=k0qE+(k1−k0)CES.v=k_0E+k_1C_{ES}=k_0q_E+(k_1-k_0)C_{ES}.

At fixed machine total, increasing occupancy activates when k1>k0k_1>k_0, represses when k1<k0k_1<k_0, and leaves this output unchanged when the frequencies agree. An enzyme, a receptor and a promoter can therefore share a binding calculation while having different outputs. The activity assignment is part of the mechanism.

The regime map then predicts where the response changes character. Sweep total substrate qSq_S while holding qEq_E and KdK_d fixed. A horizontal line in Figure 8 follows that experiment. Reading either the complex or the free species along that line gives the four curves in the next section.

Declare the varied total, the held-fixed quantities and the state activity before interpreting a curve as bioregulation.

8Read four responses from one binding pair

Two parameter regimes give four archetypal responses because there are two possible active states. First take qE≪Kdq_E\ll K_d. Then CES/S=E/Kd≤qE/Kd≪1C_{ES}/S=E/K_d\le q_E/K_d\ll1, so substrate total is dominated by free substrate throughout the sweep. Keeping the full enzyme inventory gives

CES≈qEqSKd+qS,E≈qEKdKd+qS.C_{ES}\approx q_E\frac{q_S}{K_d+q_S},\qquad E\approx q_E\frac{K_d}{K_d+q_S}.

A complex-active machine shows gradual saturation. A free-active machine shows gradual repression. Both curves pass from pattern A toward pattern C as substrate rises. The same binding affinity sets the concentration scale because substrate remains effectively undepleted.

Next take qE≫Kdq_E\gg K_d. Tight binding uses nearly all of the less abundant partner. Away from the narrow rounded corner,

CES≈min⁡(qE,qS),E≈max⁡(qE−qS,0).C_{ES}\approx\min(q_E,q_S),\qquad E\approx\max(q_E-q_S,0).

A complex-active machine grows almost linearly with substrate until enzyme becomes limiting. A free-active machine loses activity as substrate sequesters its available enzyme. The sweep passes from a mostly free-enzyme region through the matched-pool region into enzyme saturation.

The free-species curve also gives a rising threshold if we change the experiment. Hold the sequestering substrate total fixed and increase enzyme total. Then E≈max⁡(qE−qS,0)E\approx\max(q_E-q_S,0): little free enzyme appears until the sequestering pool fills. This molecular-titration mechanism can generate a sharp response without a large binding stoichiometry.4

Finite affinity rounds every such threshold. At matching totals the residual free amount is of order KdqE\sqrt{K_dq_E}, as derived in §6. The sharpness is therefore relative: the transition becomes narrow compared with qEq_E when Kd/qEK_d/q_E is small. There is no discontinuity at positive affinity.

The low-occupancy end recovers the effective one-step rate from Lecture 2. If the complex alone acts, and both totals are small compared with KdK_d, then v≈(kcat/Kd)qEqSv\approx(k_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_d)q_Eq_S. The coefficient kcat/Kdk_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_d has units M−1s−1\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}. It is the substrate slope per enzyme in this equilibrium reduction. The catalytic quasi-steady reduction uses kcat/KMk_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_M under its own assumptions.

9Add competition through a shared pool

A competitor regulates output by taking enzyme into another state. Add an inhibitor I that binds E in a complex CEIC_{EI}. The two elementary binding pairs are

E+S⇌kS,−kS,+CES,E+I⇌kI,−kI,+CEI.E+S\xrightleftharpoons[k_{S,-}]{k_{S,+}}C_{ES},\qquad E+I\xrightleftharpoons[k_{I,-}]{k_{I,+}}C_{EI}.

Only CESC_{ES} generates the chosen output, at rate v=kcatCESv=k_{\mathrm{cat}}C_{ES}. The fast equations and the enzyme total are

Kd,SCES=ES,Kd,ICEI=EI,qE=E+CES+CEI.K_{d,S}C_{ES}=ES,\qquad K_{d,I}C_{EI}=EI,\qquad q_E=E+C_{ES}+C_{EI}.

First suppose free S and I are maintained by large reservoirs. Substituting the two binding equations into the enzyme total gives

CES=qES/Kd,S1+S/Kd,S+I/Kd,I=qESKd,S(1+I/Kd,I)+S.C_{ES}=q_E\frac{S/K_{d,S}}{1+S/K_{d,S}+I/K_{d,I}} =q_E\frac{S}{K_{d,S}(1+I/K_{d,I})+S}.

The competitor raises the effective substrate scale to Kd,S(1+I/Kd,I)K_{d,S}(1+I/K_{d,I}). The microscopic substrate affinity is unchanged. The added term counts an additional enzyme state that competes for the same pool.

Dominance makes strong inhibition transparent. If I/Kd,I≫1+S/Kd,SI/K_{d,I}\gg1+S/K_{d,S}, then qE≈CEIq_E\approx C_{EI} and

CES≈qEKd,IKd,SSI.C_{ES}\approx q_E\frac{K_{d,I}}{K_{d,S}}\frac{S}{I}.

Most enzyme is inhibitor-bound. The small active fraction is set by the ratio of the two weighted ligand concentrations. Product inhibition in Lecture 6 has the same allocation mechanism when product-bound enzyme dominates the enzyme pool. If reverse catalysis is present, its flux must also be included in the net output.

Finite ligand supplies require two more inventories. Add qS=S+CESq_S=S+C_{ES} and qI=I+CEIq_I=I+C_{EI}. Eliminating free ligands gives

E+qSEKd,S+E+qIEKd,I+E=qE.E+\frac{q_SE}{K_{d,S}+E}+\frac{q_IE}{K_{d,I}+E}=q_E.

This is the same monotone scalar solve used for the reversible enzyme. It automatically accounts for inhibitor depletion. If free I is maintained but substrate is finite, the effective affinity above can instead be used in the one-pair root, with the unoccupied enzyme pool understood to include free and inhibitor-bound enzyme.

Worked branch: a sponge changes the input seen by a promoter

A second binding partner can compete for the regulator itself. Let free repressor R bind gene G or sponge T. Define KGR=GR/CGRK_{GR}=GR/C_{GR} and KRT=RT/CRTK_{RT}=RT/C_{RT}. Only free G produces. At fixed gene and sponge totals, the repressor balance and output are

qR=R+qGRKGR+R+qTRKRT+R,vk0qG=GqG=KGRKGR+R.q_R=R+\frac{q_GR}{K_{GR}+R}+\frac{q_TR}{K_{RT}+R},\qquad \frac{v}{k_0q_G}=\frac{G}{q_G}=\frac{K_{GR}}{K_{GR}+R}.

At half repression, R=KGRR=K_{GR}. Substitution gives the exact total-input midpoint

qR,1/2=KGR+qG2+qTKGRKRT+KGR.q_{R,1/2}=K_{GR}+\frac{q_G}{2}+\frac{q_TK_{GR}}{K_{RT}+K_{GR}}.

If KRT≪KGRK_{RT}\ll K_{GR}, nearly all sponge is occupied at this midpoint. The sponge therefore shifts the threshold by approximately its total concentration. This conclusion follows from which species carries the sponge pool.

Compose binding mechanisms through the totals they share. Dominance then identifies whether a competitor removes active machine or absorbs its regulator.

10Dimerization changes both binding and storage

A dimerizing repressor changes the state list before it changes the response law. Let two free repressor molecules bind as CRRC_{RR}, and let that dimer bind gene G as CGRRC_{GRR}. At the chosen resolution, only free G produces protein through the composite process G⇝G+RG\rightsquigarrow G+R, with rate v=k0Gv=k_0G.

2R⇌kdim,−kdim,+CRR,G+CRR⇌kprom,−kprom,+CGRR.2R\xrightleftharpoons[k_{\mathrm{dim},-}]{k_{\mathrm{dim},+}}C_{RR},\qquad G+C_{RR}\xrightleftharpoons[k_{\mathrm{prom},-}]{k_{\mathrm{prom},+}}C_{GRR}.

Both dissociation constants have concentration units under the definitions

CRR=R2Kdim,CGRR=GCRRKprom,qG=G+CGRR,qR=R+2CRR+2CGRR.C_{RR}=\frac{R^2}{K_{\mathrm{dim}}},\qquad C_{GRR}=\frac{GC_{RR}}{K_{\mathrm{prom}}},\qquad q_G=G+C_{GRR},\qquad q_R=R+2C_{RR}+2C_{GRR}.

The gene's free fraction follows exactly as a function of free monomer:

GqG=11+R2/(KdimKprom).\frac{G}{q_G}=\frac{1}{1+R^2/(K_{\mathrm{dim}}K_{\mathrm{prom}})}.

To express the response in total repressor, identify what carries that total. If 2CRR+2CGRR≪R2C_{RR}+2C_{GRR}\ll R, then qR≈Rq_R\approx R. Defining KH=KdimKpromK_H=\sqrt{K_{\mathrm{dim}}K_{\mathrm{prom}}} gives the degree-two Hill form

GqG≈11+(qR/KH)2.\frac{G}{q_G}\approx\frac{1}{1+(q_R/K_H)^2}.

This approximation needs small dimer storage as well as small promoter-bound storage. Near the midpoint, sufficient conditions are 2KH/Kdim≪12K_H/K_{\mathrm{dim}}\ll1 and 2qG≪KH2q_G\ll K_H. A squared free-monomer dependence alone does not establish a squared total-protein dependence.

A different law appears when free dimers carry most of the repressor total. If 2CRR≫R,2CGRR2C_{RR}\gg R,2C_{GRR}, then qR≈2R2/Kdimq_R\approx2R^2/K_{\mathrm{dim}}. Substitution into the same gene fraction gives

GqG≈11+qR/(2Kprom).\frac{G}{q_G}\approx\frac{1}{1+q_R/(2K_{\mathrm{prom}})}.

The response is now first order in total repressor. Around its midpoint, the required conditions are 2Kprom≫KH2K_{\mathrm{prom}}\gg K_H and qG≪Kpromq_G\ll K_{\mathrm{prom}}. The molecular mechanism is unchanged. The species that stores the input has changed.

A binding stoichiometry determines the free-species dependence. The dominant storage form determines how that dependence appears in a total-input experiment.

11Derive combined regulation from joint states

Two regulators require a list of joint promoter states. Let free regulators R1,R2R_1,R_2 bind gene G, forming CGR1C_{GR_1}, CGR2C_{GR_2} and, when allowed, CGR1R2C_{GR_1R_2}. Define their equilibrium weights relative to free G by

w0=1,w1=R1K1,w2=R2K2,w12=ωR1R2K1K2.w_0=1,\qquad w_1=\frac{R_1}{K_1},\qquad w_2=\frac{R_2}{K_2},\qquad w_{12}=\omega\frac{R_1R_2}{K_1K_2}.

The positive dimensionless factor ω describes the binding interaction in the double state. Independent binding gives ω=1\omega=1. The promoter total is qG=GZq_G=GZ, where Z=w0+w1+w2+w12Z=w_0+w_1+w_2+w_{12}. Thus each state's concentration is its weight times qG/Zq_G/Z. This is the state-counting construction from Lecture 7, now applied to combined regulation.5

Assign production frequencies a0,a1,a2,a12a_0,a_1,a_2,a_{12} to those states. Their unresolved production processes are composite. The activity-weighted rate is

vout=qGa0+a1R1/K1+a2R2/K2+a12ωR1R2/(K1K2)1+R1/K1+R2/K2+ωR1R2/(K1K2).v_{\mathrm{out}}=q_G\frac{a_0+a_1R_1/K_1+a_2R_2/K_2+a_{12}\omega R_1R_2/(K_1K_2)} {1+R_1/K_1+R_2/K_2+\omega R_1R_2/(K_1K_2)}.

Dominance explains the high-input behavior. Independent OR remains active when the double state dominates. If that double state exists but is inactive, its growing occupancy instead suppresses output. With mutual exclusion, occupancy remains among the two active single states, so the output saturates.

Testing the mixed response therefore adds information that isolated measurements cannot supply. A two-input dose matrix probes the joint states. An occupancy measurement can further distinguish a binding change from an activity change. The response surface alone need not identify a unique mechanism.

For finite regulators, close their inventories before evaluating this output law. In the four-state model the additional balances are

qR1=R1+qGw1+w12Z,qR2=R2+qGw2+w12Z.q_{R_1}=R_1+q_G\frac{w_1+w_{12}}{Z},\qquad q_{R_2}=R_2+q_G\frac{w_2+w_{12}}{Z}.

These equations determine the two free regulator concentrations together. Additional targets add bound terms to the same totals. The binding-catalysis construction thereby supplies a composition procedure: enlarge the state list, update the shared inventories, solve the allocation, and evaluate the activities.

Reading a promoter at low copy number

For one promoter in maintained regulator reservoirs, normalized weights are probabilities of discrete promoter states. Their weighted activity is a mean production rate. Lecture 6's fast-switching limit justifies using it for slow dynamics. A finite-pool stochastic model instead needs the conditional stationary distribution at the relevant molecule counts. A deterministic concentration root is not generally its exact mean.

Derive combined regulation from allowed joint states and their activities. Dominant occupancy explains the response, while shared totals determine which inputs those states actually see.

12Close the dynamics with production and removal

The regulatory map becomes a dynamical model when we specify how inventories change. For a produced protein X, let vprod(q;b)v_{\mathrm{prod}}(q;b) be the rate obtained from the binding allocation and state activities. If every X-containing state undergoes uniform removal at frequency δ, the total balance is

q˙X=vprod(q;b)−δqX.\dot q_X=v_{\mathrm{prod}}(q;b)-\delta q_X.

A free-protein-only removal process gives −δX-\delta X instead. Binding then regulates the substrate of removal as well as the production machinery. Uniform growth dilution gives −λqX-\lambda q_X, from Lecture 3's conversion between counts and concentrations. These boundary laws must use the same constituent accounting as the binding model.

We now have three tools with distinct jobs. The DAE specifies how molecular allocation and slow change fit together. Dominance identifies useful approximations and their validity conditions. The input-output construction translates those allocations into activation, repression, thresholds and combined regulation.

Lecture 9 will connect these mechanisms into dynamical circuits. Once a protein regulates its own production or another regulator, which steady states exist, and are they stable? Unique fast allocation does not answer that question. It supplies the vector field on which the dynamical tools act.

Lecture 11 will develop a systematic geometry of the response regimes we have drawn here. Lecture 12 will use the resulting response shapes for molecular computation. The physical state list and the conservation laws remain the common starting point.

Use the three tools to derive a molecularly specified vector field. Its steady states and dynamics are the next questions to solve.

After class: one extra state creates a response window

A nonmonotone response can follow from sequential binding to one promoter. Suppose free regulator R binds first as CGRC_{GR} and then as CGRRC_{GRR}, with stepwise dissociation constants K1K_1 and K2K_2. Their relative weights are 1,R/K1,R2/(K1K2)1,R/K_1,R^2/(K_1K_2). Only the single-bound state produces, at frequency a.

vaqG=CGRqG=R/K11+R/K1+R2/(K1K2).\frac{v}{aq_G}=\frac{C_{GR}}{q_G} =\frac{R/K_1}{1+R/K_1+R^2/(K_1K_2)}.

Three dominance regimes explain the shape. At low R, the empty state dominates. At intermediate R, the active single state can dominate. At high R, the inactive double state dominates. A broad response window requires K1≪R≪K2K_1\ll R\ll K_2, which is possible when K1≪K2K_1\ll K_2.
