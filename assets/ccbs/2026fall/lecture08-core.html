The cell as a biomachine

Biology's model class, and three tools for using it: the binding-catalysis model, leading-order dominance, and regulation read from the regime map.

Lecture 8 · core · 95-minute session · Fall 2026

Seven lectures have built tools for analyzing a model once it is written down. Lectures 1 to 5 supplied scales, reactions, networks, dynamics and stochastic counts. Lecture 6 supplied exact totals and a first differential-algebraic model, and promised that this lecture would develop it systematically. Lecture 7 supplied equilibrium: path independence, states and their weights, and the clock order tbind≪tobs≪tenvt_{\mathrm{bind}}\ll t_{\mathrm{obs}}\ll t_{\mathrm{env}}.

These tools leave a prior question open. Which models of a cell should we write down at all? Part 1 asks whether biology has a model class, as mechanics and circuits do, and narrows the answer with two observations about cells. Parts 2 to 4 are the three tools that the answer supplies: the model itself, a way to simplify it at leading order, and a way to read regulation from it.

The exposition develops the algebra and the uniqueness proof. The lineage traces where these ideas came from. The extension applies them to a transporter that carries a physical current.

Part 1

A model class for biology

Mechanics and circuits each have one form that every system of the field fits. Does biology have one? Two observations about cells narrow the answer.

1Does biology have a model class?

Mechanics has a model class. Newton's laws, or one Lagrangian form, contain every mechanical system, with slots for its masses and forces. Circuits have one too: Kirchhoff's laws for the connections, plus one element law per component, such as Ohm's law. A model class is one form that every system of a field fits, with slots for its parts. Does biology have one?

Biochemistry is the candidate core, because it is universal. Every cell runs on biochemical reactions. Thermal, electrical, mechanical and optical effects enter at the cell's boundary and act on biochemical states: a voltage opens a channel, heat unfolds a protein, light isomerizes a pigment. A model class for biology should therefore be a class of biochemical models, with interfaces at the boundary.

Chemical reaction networks, the class of Lecture 3, contain every cell. They fail two tests. The class is too large: it admits steps no cell runs, such as ten molecules meeting in one collision while one of them vanishes, with stoichiometry and reaction order chosen freely. It is also too detailed. An elementary network lists every intermediate and every rate constant, while we want effective rate laws for the dynamics we observe.

The opposite end fails too. A Hill function adopted as a law is simple, but it assumes that the ligand is in excess and that only one molecule's state matters. Detailed biophysical models of promoters and allosteric proteins usually make the same assumption. Many mechanisms fall outside it: combinatorial BMP receptor signaling, the competing dimers of MultiFate, negative cooperativity, sequestration in antithetic control, limiting pools in growth laws, and a substrate carried on a protein. The research essay Bioregulation beyond effective rate laws works through each one.

Top-down modeling wants a class large enough to contain the cell's mechanisms. Bottom-up analysis wants every member of the class to be analyzable and interpretable. The thesis of this lecture is that the two meet at binding-catalysis systems. Two observations about cells, in §2 and §3, cut the network class down to it.

Judge a proposed model class by two tests. It must contain the cell's mechanisms, and every member must be analyzable. Reaction networks fail the second test, and effective laws fail the first.

2Cells are not chemists

A chemist controls a reaction through its conditions: temperature, solvent, pressure, concentrations and catalysts. A cell cannot. It must keep thousands of reactions running in one compartment that stays viable. Its main control is which catalyst is present, and in which state. Cellular chemistry is therefore enzymatic reactions plus the regulation of enzyme activity.

Lecture 7's reversible enzyme shows the pattern in its smallest form. With Lecture 7's rate-constant names, its three reversible steps are

E+S⇌k−Sk+SCES,CES⇌kcat−kcat+CEP,CEP⇌k+Pk−PE+P.E+S\xrightleftharpoons[k_{-S}]{k_{+S}}C_{ES},\qquad C_{ES}\xrightleftharpoons[k_{\mathrm{cat}}^{-}]{k_{\mathrm{cat}}^{+}}C_{EP},\qquad C_{EP}\xrightleftharpoons[k_{+P}]{k_{-P}}E+P.

The first and third steps are binding. Binding changes no total. Count the totals of enzyme, substrate and product:

qE=E+CES+CEP,qS=S+CES,qP=P+CEP.q_E=E+C_{ES}+C_{EP},\qquad q_S=S+C_{ES},\qquad q_P=P+C_{EP}.

Binding S to E moves one substrate from S into CESC_{ES}, and qSq_S does not change. The middle step is catalysis. It turns a bound substrate into a bound product, so it lowers qSq_S by one and raises qPq_P by one.

At the level of totals only two changes remain:

qS⇝qP,qP⇝qS.q_S\rightsquigarrow q_P,\qquad q_P\rightsquigarrow q_S.

These are the total dynamics that Lecture 6 wrote and Lecture 7 evolved. Regulation acts on the enzyme. It moves the enzyme among states with different activities: free, substrate-bound and product-bound. Metabolism, signaling and gene expression share this pattern. A kinase is an enzyme whose substrate is a protein. A promoter with its polymerase is the catalyst of transcription, and bound regulators set its state.

The arrows carry this distinction. Binding and catalytic steps are elementary, so they are straight arrows with rate constants. The changes of totals are composite: each lumps binding, catalysis and release. They are squiggly arrows and carry no rate constant. Their rate laws are written separately, and §4 writes them.

Sort any step of a cell's chemistry by what it changes. Binding moves constituents between states, catalysis changes totals, and regulation moves a catalyst between states of different activity.

3Separated clocks put binding at equilibrium

Order the clocks. The binding time tbindt_{\mathrm{bind}} is how long a binding step takes to relax. The catalysis time tcatt_{\mathrm{cat}} is how long catalysis takes to change the totals. It matches the observation time tobst_{\mathrm{obs}} of Lecture 7, because catalysis is what we watch. The mutation time tmutt_{\mathrm{mut}} is how long a parameter takes to change. The claim is

tbind≪tcat≈tobs≪tmut.t_{\mathrm{bind}}\ll t_{\mathrm{cat}}\approx t_{\mathrm{obs}}\ll t_{\mathrm{mut}}.

Lecture 2's ledger supplies the rungs. A diffusive encounter takes about 70 ns, and a conformational change about 1 ms. One enzyme turnover takes about 0.1 s, an expression step tens of seconds, and an mRNA half-life about 5 min. A division takes 40 min. From a conformational change to one turnover is already a factor of 100.

The mutation clock is reached from inheritance, not from the ledger. An affinity or a catalytic constant changes only when a changed gene sequence is inherited and spreads. That takes at least many divisions, far longer than anything we observe.

Three consequences follow, one from each part of the claim.

  1. Binding relaxes before catalysis moves the totals. Binding can be solved at fixed totals. This is Lecture 6's fast-slow reduction.
  2. Binding couples to no fuel. Its stationary state is therefore thermodynamic equilibrium, with detailed balance and negligible dissipation (Lecture 7).
  3. Affinities and catalytic constants are fixed parameters while we observe. This is Lecture 7's argument about the environment clock tenvt_{\mathrm{env}}, applied to the mutation clock.

The classification is then operational, and in a sense tautological. Every fast, undriven, reversible step belongs to the binding layer. This includes conformational changes and other changes of state, not only association. Every driven, slow or chemistry-changing step belongs to catalysis. A step that fails the clock test moves to catalysis, rather than being forced into equilibrium.

Classify a step by two tests: its clock and its driving. Fast and undriven means binding at equilibrium. Everything else is catalysis. The constants of both are fixed parameters.

Part 2

Tool 1: the binding-catalysis model

Binding and catalysis are defined, and binding sits at equilibrium. The model can be written down before any example. The examples then read it line by line.

4Two observations give a four-line model

Start from Lecture 3's rate equation, x˙=Γv(x)\dot x=\Gamma v(x) (Lecture 3 §3). Every elementary reaction appears in it with its own rate constant. Now apply the second observation. Binding is fast and undriven, so at every slow time it sits at equilibrium. Its differential equations become algebraic ones. Fix one canonical concentration unit, such as 1 μM1\,\mu\mathrm M, and measure every concentration in it. Every concentration is then a pure number, and its logarithm is defined. No bar or other mark is carried afterwards. Equilibrium gives one monomial relation per complex. Section 6 derives them and collects them as Nlog⁡x=log⁡kN\log x=\log k.

Catalysis is performed by one species per step, the enzyme state that carries it. Its flux is first order in that species, v=Kcatxv=K^{\mathrm{cat}}x. Row j of KcatK^{\mathrm{cat}} has one nonzero entry: the catalytic constant of step j, in the column of the species that performs it.

The binding-catalysis model

q=LxconservationNlog⁡x=log⁡kequilibriumv=Kcatxactivityq˙=Sv+Sextvextaccumulation\begin{aligned} q&=Lx &&\text{conservation}\\ N\log x&=\log k &&\text{equilibrium}\\ v&=K^{\mathrm{cat}}x &&\text{activity}\\ \dot q&=Sv+S^{\mathrm{ext}}v^{\mathrm{ext}} &&\text{accumulation} \end{aligned}

The first two lines are the binding layer. At given totals they fix every species, x=xeq(q;k)x=x^{\mathrm{eq}}(q;k). The last two lines are the catalysis layer. They read fluxes from those species and integrate them into new totals. The concentrations x and q are pure numbers in the canonical unit. The fluxes are concentrations per unit time, and the entries of KcatK^{\mathrm{cat}} are frequencies.

Each line has one job. Conservation defines the totals. Equilibrium shares each total among the species that contain it. Activity reads a flux from each active species. Accumulation integrates the fluxes into new totals, which the next binding solve shares out again.

The four lines parallel a circuit's laws. Conservation is Kirchhoff's current law. Equilibrium says that a potential exists, as Kirchhoff's voltage law does, and this is Lecture 7's path independence. Activity is an element law, like Ohm's law. Accumulation integrates a flux, like a capacitor charging.

The two descriptions differ in what they integrate and what they ask us to specify.

The rate equation is stiff because its two layers run on clocks two or more orders of magnitude apart. An integrator must take steps as short as the binding time, yet follow the totals over catalytic times. The binding-catalysis model removes the fast layer by solving it.

Two uses of S

The matrix SS is not the substrate SS. The matrix always multiplies a flux vector, as in SvSv, and the substrate never does. The course writes Γ\Gamma for stoichiometry on species, and S=LΓcatS=L\Gamma_{\mathrm{cat}} only for catalysis on totals.

Write any cell model in four lines: conservation, equilibrium, activity and accumulation. Integrate only the totals, and solve the binding layer at each slow time.

5The complex list specifies the binding layer

The first line needs the complex list. Take d atomic species and r complexes, and order the n=d+rn=d+r species with the atomic species first. Each complex has a composition: how many copies of each atomic species it contains. Collect the compositions in the counting matrix

L=[ Id∣L(c) ],qi=xi(a)+∑j=1rLij(c)xj(c).L=[\,I_d\mid L^{(c)}\,],\qquad q_i=x_i^{(a)}+\sum_{j=1}^{r}L^{(c)}_{ij}x_j^{(c)}.

Here x(a)x^{(a)} are the free atomic species and x(c)x^{(c)} the complexes. The entry Lij(c)L^{(c)}_{ij} counts copies of atomic species i in complex j. "Atomic" means a reference constituent of the binding model, not a chemical element. An enzyme, a substrate and a protein monomer are all atomic here.

The same data is a graph. Draw d square nodes for the totals and n circle nodes for the species. Wherever total i counts species j, draw an edge of weight LijL_{ij}. This bipartite composition graph has L as its bi-adjacency matrix: rows are squares, and columns are circles.

Three instances show what the columns can say. The enzyme has three totals and two complexes (Figure 7). A dimer uses two copies of its monomer, so qR=R+2CRRq_R=R+2C_{RR} and L=(1  2)L=(1\ \ 2): the edge has weight two. An isomer E∗E^\ast keeps its composition while changing its state, so qE=E+E∗q_E=E+E^\ast and L=(1  1)L=(1\ \ 1): two species share a column.

Five properties of the counting matrix
  1. Binding conserves every total. LΓbind=0L\Gamma_{\mathrm{bind}}=0, so the totals are the slow variables. They change only through catalysis and external fluxes.
  2. Columns are compositions. Column j lists the atomic content of species j.
  3. Repeated columns are a choice. An isomer-atomic L has the identity block, nonnegative integer entries and no zero column, and it allows repeated columns. A faithful-atomic L forbids them, so distinct species have distinct compositions. Repeats keep isomers as distinct states.2
  4. The rows must be complete. The totals must include every independent conservation law of the binding layer. A missing total leaves a conserved quantity unaccounted for.
  5. Edges mean "contains", not "reacts". The graph records composition. It says nothing about which binding steps occur.

Specify a binding layer by its complex list. Write L column by column from each complex's composition, and draw the composition graph from L.

Part 3

Tool 2: leading-order dominance

Newton's law states a model, and calculus analyzes it. The binding-catalysis model needs its own analysis tool, and dominance regimes supply it.

7Dominance regimes are the model's calculus

Newton's law states a model. Calculus and the conservation laws of energy and momentum analyze it. The binding-catalysis model needs its own analysis tool, for bioregulation in which binding regulates catalysis. That tool is the dominance regime.

Each total qiq_i is a sum of positive terms LijxjL_{ij}x_j, one for each species that contains it. Over most of parameter space one term holds almost all of the total. That species is the total's winner. A choice of one winner per total is a regime, and a regime is named by its winner tuple. For one pair, (qE,qS)≈(E,S)(q_E,q_S)\approx(E,S) is the regime (E,S)(E,S): free enzyme wins the enzyme total, and free substrate wins the substrate total. Letters never name regimes, because letters already name species.

On the composition graph a regime is an orientation. Draw an arrow from each winner into its total. Point every other edge of that total out of it, toward a species that the total outweighs. A choice of winners is realizable exactly when this orientation is acyclic. A directed cycle would make a species outweigh itself.

A regime is regular when no species wins two totals. In general, the winners' columns of L must be independent. Otherwise the regime is singular.

Each regular regime has a leading-order formula. Keep only the winner in each conservation equation, keep every equilibrium relation, and solve. Every species becomes a monomial in the totals and affinities. On log axes a monomial is a straight line, and its exponents are the regime's gains.

Then check. Each discarded term must be smaller than its total's winner. Substituting the formulas turns each condition into a linear inequality in log coordinates. So each regime holds on a polyhedral region of log parameter space, bounded by straight lines.

Inside its region the formula is correct within a bounded factor. A sum of n positive terms lies between its largest term and n times it, so replacing a total by its winner errs by less than a factor n. Deep inside the region the other terms are negligible, and the formula becomes exact.

Analyze a binding-catalysis model by its regimes in three steps. Select one winner per total. Solve for monomial laws. Check the discarded terms to find the region where each law holds.

Optional: the exact solution and the exact boundaries

Substituting both totals into the binding relation gives KdCES=(qE−CES)(qS−CES)K_dC_{ES}=(q_E-C_{ES})(q_S-C_{ES}), a quadratic. Its physical root is

CES=2qEqST+T2−4qEqS,T=qE+qS+Kd.C_{ES}=\frac{2q_Eq_S}{T+\sqrt{T^2-4q_Eq_S}},\qquad T=q_E+q_S+K_d.

This root lies between zero and the smaller total. The rationalized form avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers when binding is weak. The exact curves of §10 and the explorer use it.

The exact equal-contribution curves follow from the same relations. E=CESE=C_{ES} means S=KdS=K_d, hence qS=Kd+qE/2q_S=K_d+q_E/2. S=CESS=C_{ES} means qE=Kd+qS/2q_E=K_d+q_S/2. Each curve lies within a factor of two of the leading-order rays. Between the two curves the complex outweighs both free species. On log axes that wedge has constant width, so at leading order it collapses to the singular ray.

Read the regime of any binding pair from its totals. If both totals are below KdK_d, the free species win. Otherwise the smaller total is bound. In every case CES≈min⁡(qE,qS,qEqS/Kd)C_{ES}\approx\min(q_E,q_S,q_Eq_S/K_d).

Part 4

Tool 3: regulation read from the regime map

A response is a path across the regime map. Name the input, the held quantities and the active state. The regimes that the path crosses then give the response at leading order.

9Declare the regulatory experiment

A response needs an input, fixed quantities and an output. The definition makes each of them explicit.

A regulatory experiment

Split the totals into a varied input block qIq_{\mathrm I} and a held block qPq_{\mathrm P}. Hold the affinities b and the activities KcatK^{\mathrm{cat}} fixed. The response is the map

qI ⟼ xeq(qI,qP;b) ⟼ v=Kcatxeq(qI,qP;b).q_{\mathrm I}\ \longmapsto\ x^{\mathrm{eq}}(q_{\mathrm I},q_{\mathrm P};b)\ \longmapsto\ v=K^{\mathrm{cat}}x^{\mathrm{eq}}(q_{\mathrm I},q_{\mathrm P};b).

Upright subscripts label roles: input and parameter. Italic subscripts name species, so qPq_P is product total, and qIq_I in §11 is inhibitor total.

The activity assignment sets the sign of the response. Suppose a molecular machine has a free state E and a bound state CESC_{ES}, with activities k0k_0 and k1k_1. Its output is

v=k0E+k1CES=k0qE+(k1−k0) CES.v=k_0E+k_1C_{ES}=k_0q_E+(k_1-k_0)\,C_{ES}.

At fixed qEq_E, occupancy activates when k1>k0k_1>k_0, represses when k1<k0k_1<k_0, and does nothing when they agree. An enzyme, a receptor and a promoter can share one binding calculation and still differ in output. The activity assignment is part of the mechanism.

A response plot must therefore name three things: the swept total, the held quantities and the active state. Sweep qSq_S at fixed qEq_E and KdK_d, and the experiment is a horizontal line across Figure 12.

Before reading a curve as regulation, declare the swept total, the held quantities and the active state. The same binding pair activates, represses or does nothing, depending on the last.

10Four responses along two paths

Two horizontal cuts through the totals map give four responses at leading order: two paths, times two readouts. Each response is read regime by regime.

Take the weak cut qE=0.01Kdq_E=0.01K_d. It starts in (E,S)(E,S) and crosses into (CES,S)(C_{ES},S) at qS=Kdq_S=K_d. Read the complex: CES/qE≈qS/KdC_{ES}/q_E\approx q_S/K_d, then CES/qE≈1C_{ES}/q_E\approx1. This is complex saturation, the Michaelis-Menten shape at leading order. Read the free enzyme: E/qE≈1E/q_E\approx1, then E/qE≈Kd/qSE/q_E\approx K_d/q_S. This is graded repression.

Take the tight cut qE=100Kdq_E=100K_d. It starts in (E,CES)(E,C_{ES}) and crosses into (CES,S)(C_{ES},S) through the singular ray at qS=qEq_S=q_E. Read the complex: CES/qE≈qS/qEC_{ES}/q_E\approx q_S/q_E, then 1. This is a limiting-pool bottleneck: every substrate is bound until the enzyme runs out. Read the free enzyme: E/qE≈1E/q_E\approx1, then Kd/qSK_d/q_S. It drops a hundredfold at the ray, where it passes through Kd/qE=0.1\sqrt{K_d/q_E}=0.1. This is a titration switch.

These are the two kinds of path across one binding edge. A graded path breaks at qS=Kdq_S=K_d, and it occurs when qE<Kdq_E<K_d. A switch breaks at qS=qEq_S=q_E, and it occurs when qE>Kdq_E>K_d.

Each piece is a power law of slope 1, 0 or −1 on log axes. The slope is the regime's gain, the logarithmic derivative of the output with respect to the input. A change of regime is a change of integer gain.

The low-occupancy end recovers the effective one-step rate law of Lecture 2. In (E,S)(E,S), with the complex active at kcatk_{\mathrm{cat}}, the flux is v=kcatCES≈(kcat/Kd) qEqSv=k_{\mathrm{cat}}C_{ES}\approx(k_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_d)\,q_Eq_S. The coefficient kcat/Kdk_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_d has units M−1s−1\mathrm M^{-1}\mathrm s^{-1}. It is the second-order constant of the whole enzyme, in this equilibrium reduction.

Swapping the input gives the rising threshold of molecular titration. Hold a sequestering total qS≫Kdq_S\gg K_d fixed and sweep qEq_E. While qE<qSq_E<q_S the path lies in (CES,S)(C_{ES},S), and free enzyme stays tiny, E≈KdqE/qSE\approx K_dq_E/q_S. Past qE=qSq_E=q_S it enters (E,CES)(E,C_{ES}), and E≈qEE\approx q_E. Little free enzyme appears until the sequestering pool fills, and then all extra enzyme is free. This threshold needs no cooperativity.4

11A competitor adds a candidate for the enzyme total

Add an inhibitor I that binds the enzyme in a complex CEIC_{EI}. With Lecture 7's naming, the two binding pairs are

E+S⇌k−Sk+SCES,E+I⇌k−Ik+ICEI.E+S\xrightleftharpoons[k_{-S}]{k_{+S}}C_{ES},\qquad E+I\xrightleftharpoons[k_{-I}]{k_{+I}}C_{EI}.

Only CESC_{ES} acts, so v=kcatCESv=k_{\mathrm{cat}}C_{ES}. Binding gives Kd,SCES=ESK_{d,S}C_{ES}=ES and Kd,ICEI=EIK_{d,I}C_{EI}=EI, and the enzyme total is qE=E+CES+CEIq_E=E+C_{ES}+C_{EI}.

Hold both ligands in excess, so S wins qSq_S and I wins qIq_I. Then only the enzyme total can change winner. Its candidates are E, CESC_{ES} and CEIC_{EI}, with CES/E=S/Kd,SC_{ES}/E=S/K_{d,S} and CEI/E=I/Kd,IC_{EI}/E=I/K_{d,I}.

In the log coordinates (S/Kd,S,I/Kd,I)(S/K_{d,S},I/K_{d,I}) the map has three regular regimes: (E,S,I)(E,S,I), (CES,S,I)(C_{ES},S,I) and (CEI,S,I)(C_{EI},S,I). Three rays from (1,1)(1,1) bound them: S=Kd,SS=K_{d,S}, I=Kd,II=K_{d,I} and S/Kd,S=I/Kd,IS/K_{d,S}=I/K_{d,I}. The one-pair geometry returns, now with one total and three candidates. The leading-order laws are

CES≈{qE S/Kd,Sin (E,S,I),qEin (CES,S,I),qE Kd,IKd,S SIin (CEI,S,I).C_{ES}\approx\begin{cases}q_E\,S/K_{d,S} & \text{in }(E,S,I),\\ q_E & \text{in }(C_{ES},S,I),\\ q_E\,\dfrac{K_{d,I}}{K_{d,S}}\,\dfrac{S}{I} & \text{in }(C_{EI},S,I).\end{cases}

The saturation boundary moves. Without inhibitor the complex takes over at S=Kd,SS=K_{d,S}. With inhibitor, the complex must beat both E and CEIC_{EI}, so saturation begins at

S=Kd,Smax⁡(1, IKd,I).S=K_{d,S}\max\Bigl(1,\ \frac{I}{K_{d,I}}\Bigr).

This is the leading-order form of the reservoir formula Kd,S(1+I/Kd,I)K_{d,S}(1+I/K_{d,I}). The exact allocation is CES=qES/(Kd,S(1+I/Kd,I)+S)C_{ES}=q_ES/\bigl(K_{d,S}(1+I/K_{d,I})+S\bigr). The substrate affinity itself is unchanged. The competitor adds a candidate for the enzyme total, and that moves the boundary. Composing two binding reactions tunes the effective KdK_d.

Strong inhibition is a regime statement. Where CEIC_{EI} wins, the catalytic flux falls as 1/I1/I. Product inhibition in Lecture 6 is the same mechanism, with product-bound enzyme winning the enzyme total.

Optional: finite ligands, where the enzyme can titrate the inhibitor

With finite ligands, add the totals qS=S+CESq_S=S+C_{ES} and qI=I+CEIq_I=I+C_{EI}. Eliminating the free ligands gives the same monotone scalar equation as the reversible enzyme:

E+qSEKd,S+E+qIEKd,I+E=qE.E+\frac{q_SE}{K_{d,S}+E}+\frac{q_IE}{K_{d,I}+E}=q_E.

Now the inhibitor total can change winner too. A tight inhibitor, Kd,I≪qEK_{d,I}\ll q_E, is mostly bound while qI<qEq_I<q_E: CEIC_{EI} wins qIq_I, and the enzyme titrates the inhibitor. Each inhibitor then takes one enzyme from the pool that substrate can reach. Once qIq_I exceeds qEq_E, CEIC_{EI} wins the enzyme total, and substrate binding collapses.

Treat a competitor as one more candidate for a shared total. It moves the saturation boundary by the factor max⁡(1,I/Kd,I)\max(1,I/K_{d,I}), and where it wins, the output falls as 1/I1/I.

12Joint states decide how regulators combine

Two regulators R1R_1 and R2R_2 bind a promoter G. The joint promoter states are G, CGR1C_{GR_1}, CGR2C_{GR_2} and, when allowed, CGR1R2C_{GR_1R_2}. Relative to free G their equilibrium weights are

w0=1,w1=R1K1,w2=R2K2,w12=ωR1R2K1K2.w_0=1,\qquad w_1=\frac{R_1}{K_1},\qquad w_2=\frac{R_2}{K_2},\qquad w_{12}=\omega\frac{R_1R_2}{K_1K_2}.

The positive factor ω describes the interaction of the two bound regulators, and independent binding has ω=1\omega=1. The promoter total is qG=GZq_G=GZ with Z=∑jwjZ=\sum_jw_j, so each state holds wjqG/Zw_jq_G/Z. This is Lecture 7's state counting, applied to combined regulation.5

Assign each state an activity aja_j. The production processes are composite, so their rate law is given separately:

vout=qG ∑jajwj∑jwj.v_{\mathrm{out}}=q_G\,\frac{\sum_ja_jw_j}{\sum_jw_j}.

Now read the promoter through its winners. With the regulators in excess, only the promoter total can change winner. Its candidates are the allowed joint states. At leading order the output is the activity of the winning state, vout≈awinqGv_{\mathrm{out}}\approx a_{\mathrm{win}}q_G. So the regime map in the log coordinates (R1/K1,R2/K2)(R_1/K_1,R_2/K_2) is a truth table.

The architecture fixes the map. Independent sites give four quadrants, bounded by R1=K1R_1=K_1 and R2=K2R_2=K_2, like the one-pair free-species map. Exclusive binding removes the doubly bound state. That leaves three regimes, won by G, CGR1C_{GR_1} and CGR2C_{GR_2}, and a diagonal ray where the two single states tie.

Assigning activities derives the familiar rules. For independent sites the weights factor, Z=(1+R1/K1)(1+R2/K2)Z=(1+R_1/K_1)(1+R_2/K_2), and the exact outputs are compact.

A two-input dose matrix therefore adds information that isolated measurements cannot supply. An occupancy measurement can further separate a binding change from an activity change.

For finite regulators, close their totals before evaluating the output:

qR1=R1+qG w1+w12Z,qR2=R2+qG w2+w12Z.q_{R_1}=R_1+q_G\,\frac{w_1+w_{12}}{Z},\qquad q_{R_2}=R_2+q_G\,\frac{w_2+w_{12}}{Z}.

These equations determine the two free regulators together. More targets add bound terms to the same totals. The composition procedure is general: enlarge the complex list, update the shared totals, solve the binding layer, and read the activities.

Reading a promoter at low copy number

For one promoter in maintained regulator reservoirs, normalized weights are probabilities of discrete promoter states. Their weighted activity is a mean production rate. Lecture 6's fast-switching limit justifies using it for slow dynamics. A finite-pool stochastic model instead needs the conditional stationary distribution at the relevant molecule counts. A deterministic concentration root is not generally its exact mean.

Derive combined regulation from joint states. List the allowed states, find which one wins each region of the input plane, and read off its activity. A truth table is a regime map.

Coda: close the dynamics

The regulatory map becomes a dynamical model once the totals change. For a produced protein X, let vprod(q;b)v_{\mathrm{prod}}(q;b) be its production rate, read from the binding layer and the activities. If every X-containing state is removed at the same frequency δ, the accumulation line for X reads

q˙X=vprod(q;b)−δqX.\dot q_X=v_{\mathrm{prod}}(q;b)-\delta q_X.

If only free X is removed, the loss term is −δX-\delta X instead, and binding then protects X. Uniform growth dilution gives −λqX-\lambda q_X, from Lecture 3's conversion between counts and concentrations. Each loss law must use the same totals as the binding layer.

The model class is now established. It carries as many biological constraints and as much reduction as possible without losing generality, and every member stays analyzable. This closes the course's first phase.

Lecture 9 opens the second phase. It closes these production laws with addition and removal, and asks which steady states exist, whether they are stable, and when a circuit oscillates. Unique fast allocation does not answer that. It supplies the vector field on which Lecture 9's tools act. Lecture 11 generalizes the regime maps, whose boundaries are straight lines and whose laws are monomials, into reaction-order geometry. Lecture 12 uses the response shapes as computing elements.

Close a binding-catalysis model with production and loss on the same totals. The result is the vector field that Lecture 9 analyzes.

A1The winner of the repressor total sets the Hill degree

A dimerizing repressor changes the complex list before it changes the response. Two free repressor monomers bind as CRRC_{RR}, and the dimer binds gene G as CGRRC_{GRR}. Only free G produces, through the composite process G⇝G+XG\rightsquigarrow G+X with rate law v=k0Gv=k_0G.

GqG=11+R2/(KdimKprom).\frac{G}{q_G}=\frac{1}{1+R^2/(K_{\mathrm{dim}}K_{\mathrm{prom}})}.

A2A sponge shifts the input a promoter sees

A second binding partner can compete for the regulator itself. Free repressor R binds gene G or sponge T:

G+R⇌k−Gk+GCGR,T+R⇌k−Tk+TCRT,KGR=GRCGR,KRT=RTCRT.G+R\xrightleftharpoons[k_{-G}]{k_{+G}}C_{GR},\qquad T+R\xrightleftharpoons[k_{-T}]{k_{+T}}C_{RT},\qquad K_{GR}=\frac{GR}{C_{GR}},\qquad K_{RT}=\frac{RT}{C_{RT}}.

GqG=KGRKGR+R.\frac{G}{q_G}=\frac{K_{GR}}{K_{GR}+R}.

Read it through winners. Keep the promoter in trace, so CGRC_{GR} never wins qRq_R. Then qTq_T and qRq_R form the one-pair map of §8, with T in the enzyme's role and R in the substrate's. A3One extra state creates a response window

A regulator R binds a promoter twice in sequence, first as CGRC_{GR} and then as CGRRC_{GRR}, with stepwise dissociation constants K1K_1 and K2K_2. The state weights are 1, R/K1R/K_1 and R2/(K1K2)R^2/(K_1K_2). Only the singly bound state produces, at activity a:

va qG=CGRqG=R/K11+R/K1+R2/(K1K2).\frac{v}{a\,q_G}=\frac{C_{GR}}{q_G}=\frac{R/K_1}{1+R/K_1+R^2/(K_1K_2)}.

With the regulator in excess, only the promoter total changes winner. Its candidates are G, CGRC_{GR} and CGRRC_{GRR}. When K1<K2K_1<K_2 they win in turn. G wins below R=K1R=K_1, where the output is about R/K1R/K_1. CGRC_{GR} wins between K1K_1 and K2K_2, where the output is about 1. CGRRC_{GRR} wins above K2K_2, where the output is about K2/RK_2/R. When K1>K2K_1>K_2 the singly bound state never wins. G and CGRRC_{GRR} tie at R=K1K2R=\sqrt{K_1K_2}, and the peak falls to about K2/K1\sqrt{K_2/K_1} at leading order. For the swapped values of Figure 21 that is 0.1. The exact peak is 0.048, within the factor-of-three bound for three terms.
