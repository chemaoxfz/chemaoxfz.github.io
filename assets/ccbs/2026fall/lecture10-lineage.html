How separate questions became one account of adaptation

An amplifier designer, a physicist, control theorists, biochemists, chemotaxis modellers, network searchers and synthetic biologists each answered a different question. This page follows what each answer meant when it was asked, and what it came to mean once the answers were joined.

CCBS · Lecture 10 · lineage · 12 October 2026

Robust adaptation joins three questions that were first asked separately. Can feedback reduce sensitivity to changing parts? Can a disturbed output return exactly? Can chemistry implement the required memory? The history below follows how answers to these questions were combined, and why their assumptions still matter.

The core develops the mechanism. The exposition derives a method for checking an adaptation claim. Here the organizing evidence is what each source asked, measured or proved.

The sources below did not set out to build one account. Each asked its own question, with its own kind of evidence, and several of them changed meaning when a later source joined them to another. The page is ordered by those questions, not by date, and each section keeps three things apart: what the source claimed, how it was later read, and how the course now builds on it.

Lecture 9's lineage already tells how Black came to his amplifier, from drifting telephone repeaters to his recollection of a ferry crossing. This page recalls that story in two sentences and starts from the factor his amplifier made famous.

1Each piece of today's account of adaptation answered its own question

The organizing question is which questions, and which kinds of evidence, produced the pieces now used to explain robust adaptation. A second question follows: what did each piece come to mean once it was joined to the others? The table answers both in one line per piece, and the sections below give each answer its evidence.

Five kinds of evidence appear, and the page keeps them apart. A theorem holds exactly under its hypotheses. A model calculation shows what a stated model does at stated parameters. A sampled model ensemble shows how often a property survives random parameter changes inside one model. A choice among fitted candidate models ranks the candidates that were fitted, and only those. A measurement shows what cells or circuits did under one protocol, within its precision.

Three changes of meaning carry the page. The internal model principle went from a necessity theorem about designed regulators to a guide for reading networks. The two sources of ultrasensitivity that Lecture 9 taught, a saturated modification cycle and a sequestering pair, were both later read as memories. And Black's question about an amplifier came back in biology as a question about which constants a set point may depend on.

I · Two engineering questions, and the theorem that asked both

2Black's feedback bought insensitivity to the amplifier's own parts

Black's problem was a gain that drifted with its tubes. He built amplifiers with about 40 dB more gain than needed and fed the output back on the input "so as to throw away the excess gain" (pp. 1–2).1 Lecture 9's lineage tells the rest of that story. What this page needs is the factor.

Black's own algebra already joined two results. He wrote the output as signal plus noise plus distortion, with the noise and distortion "assumed to be generated within the amplifier" and the distortion "a function only of the signal output" (p. 3). With feedback, all three are divided by one factor at a fixed input (eq. (2), p. 5). Compared at the same signal output, with the same amplifier and its input raised, the internally generated noise and distortion are smaller by that factor. The amplifier's own variations are stabilized by it, while those of the feedback circuit are "neither appreciably improved nor degraded" (p. 6). In the limit of a loop gain far above 1, the amplification stops depending on the amplifier and is set by the feedback circuit (p. 3).1

Bode later gave the two ideas separate names. The usual conception of feedback, he wrote, "includes two distinct ideas": a loop return, which he called the return difference, and "a reduction in the effects of variations in the tube characteristics", which he called the sensitivity (p. 47).2 In normal circuits, he added, the two are related by simple mathematical laws, so that "feedback" can name both (p. 47). His sensitivity is a ratio of percentage changes, so an amplifier with 40 dB of feedback has sensitivity 100 for its forward elements (pp. 52–53). Åström and Murray's textbook calls the reciprocal of Bode's quantity the sensitivity function, and reads it as the factor between a relative error in the process and the relative error of the closed loop (eqs. (12.2) and (13.23), pp. 12-3 and 13-17).3

A static construction shows the connection, with Black's quantities under the course's names. Call the amplifier's gain without feedback GampG_\mathrm{amp} and the fraction of the output fed back ρ\rho, so the loop gain is H=GampρH=G_\mathrm{amp}\rho, the same loop gain as in the core. Add a disturbance χout\chi_\mathrm{out} at the amplifier's output and a disturbance χin\chi_\mathrm{in} to the command VinV_\mathrm{in} before the comparison. This is the course's explanation, not Black's derivation:

One factor answers both questions. The closed-loop gain's relative sensitivity to GampG_\mathrm{amp} is 1/(1+H)1/(1+H), and to ρ\rho it is −H/(1+H)-H/(1+H), so as HH grows the dependence moves from the amplifier to the feedback path. A share needs a stated baseline, and the page uses two. On Black's basis, the same amplifier with its input raised and the noise voltages fixed, both disturbances keep 1/(1+H)1/(1+H) of their shares. Figure 1(b) uses baseline (B): an amplifier without feedback whose gain equals GclG_\mathrm{cl}, driven by the same VinV_\mathrm{in} and carrying the same noise voltages. So the first result holds against both baselines used here, and the second depends on the baseline. Neither holds on every basis. With the same amplifier at the same input, feedback lowers the signal and the disturbance added at the output together, so their ratio does not change. These are comparisons inside the course's model, not a claim that two physical amplifier designs generate the same internal noise.

The factor works at the scale of Black's measurement. Forty decibels of feedback means 1+H=1001+H=100. A change of 0.7 dB in an amplifier's own gain then becomes about 0.007 dB in the closed loop, 0.0067 dB exactly for that finite change. The 0.7 dB was measured on a conventional amplifier of comparable size, not on the open loop of Black's feedback amplifier, so the arithmetic illustrates the factor at the scale of his measurement and does not predict it.

Measured

Black's feedback amplifiers changed gain by less than 0.01 dB when the plate voltage went from 240 to 260 volts. An amplifier of conventional design and comparable size changed by about 0.7 dB (p. 2).1

Before calling a feedback loop robust, name the parameter, the disturbance's entry point and the baseline. The factor 1/(1+H)1/(1+H) shrinks the amplifier's own variations, and against baseline (B) it shrinks a disturbance added at the output but not one added to the command.

3Exact return was a different question, answered by a part that integrates

Black's factor shrinks an error without removing it, and an older paper had already separated the two. Maxwell opened his 1868 paper on governors by defining a governor as the part of a machine that keeps its velocity "nearly uniform", and then split regulators into two kinds (transcription p. 1).4 A historian of control reads the opening the same way: Maxwell begins by "carefully distinguishing between regulators which give offset, which, following Siemens, he terms moderators, and governors which have no offset" (p. 68).5 The archived copy of Maxwell's paper is a modern typeset transcription that does not mark the original page numbers, so this page cites the transcription's pages.

A moderator reduces the error and keeps it. In a moderator, "the resistance is increased by a quantity depending on the velocity", so an increase of driving power still increases the velocity, "though a much smaller increase than would be produced without the moderator" (transcription p. 1). His equation (1) puts the final velocity above the normal velocity by the change of load divided by the moderator's force constant (transcription p. 4). Following Siemens, he would not call this a governor.

A governor returns exactly, because a separate piece accumulates the error. If the centrifugal part "sets in motion a contrivance which continually increases the resistance as long as the velocity is above its normal value", the governor brings the velocity "to the same normal value whatever variation (within the working limits of the machine)" is made in the load (transcription p. 1). For an idealized piece with no resistance of its own, integrating its equation shows its momentum equal to the force constant times the accumulated excess distance, so when the piece rests even the machine's position is restored (transcription p. 4).

Jenkin's governor keeps the speed exact but not the position. Its moving piece meets a viscous resistance, and then "the position of the machine is affected by that of the governor, but the final velocity is constant" (eq. (8), transcription p. 5). The box writes this governor as a state model from his equations (6) and (10).

These are the course's names for Maxwell's quantities. The machine's speed above its normal value is ω\omega, and the governor's moving piece has displacement φ\varphi and velocity vgv_\mathrm{g}. The moments of inertia of the machine and of the piece are JJ and JgJ_\mathrm{g}. The force per unit speed deviation is κ\kappa, the moderator's force constant, which slows the machine and drives the piece. The piece's viscous coefficient is cc, and the brake's resistance per unit displacement is ss. A step dd changes the driving force minus the resistance, so a heavier load makes dd negative.

Maxwell then asked a separate question: does the motion settle? For Jenkin's governor he derived a condition, his (14), on the piece's viscous resistance against the brake's strength. In the box's names it reads (κ/J+c/Jg) c/Jg>s/Jg(\kappa/J+c/J_\mathrm{g})\,c/J_\mathrm{g}>s/J_\mathrm{g}. If it fails, "there will be a dancing motion of the governor", so the weight is hung in a viscous liquid to damp it (transcription p. 6).4

A governor establishes that accumulated error can remove an offset, but it does not establish when such a mechanism is necessary. Maxwell analyzed a chosen device and tested whether it settled. The next question reverses that reasoning: what must every regulator contain if exact return is to survive changes of the plant?

Tell a moderator from a governor by asking whether the corrective action depends on the present error or on its accumulated history, and test stability separately in either case.

4The internal model principle asked for exact regulation that survives changes of the plant

Francis and Wonham asked a converse question. Others had given procedures for designing regulators whose stability and regulation survive small changes of parameters, among them Davison, Staats and Pearson, and Wonham. Francis and Wonham asked instead: "What controller structure is necessary for structural stability?" (p. 457).6 Their paper summarizes and extends their own 1975 results, so it is neither the first appearance of these results nor the invention of integral control.

Structural stability joins Black's question to Maxwell's. A regulator synthesis is structurally stable when closed-loop stability and output regulation both survive the specified small changes of parameters (p. 457). Maxwell's governor contributes exact return. Black contributes the question of insensitivity to parameters, which his amplifier answered only in part: at finite loop gain its sensitivity stays 1/(1+H)1/(1+H). Structural stability asks for both at once.

The answer is an internal model of the signals, not of the plant. Under readability of the regulated variable and a stated technical condition, a structurally stable synthesis needs a compensator that contains an internal model of the exogenous signal generator, controllable by the regulated variable and observable by the actuator (Theorem 2, p. 460). For constant signals that model is a mode at zero frequency, an integrator. The internal model supplies closed-loop zeros that cancel the unstable poles of the exogenous signals (p. 460).

The theorem carries its scope in its own words. "A smaller class of parameter variations in general requires less reduplication in the internal model" (p. 460). For constant signals, uncertain plant parameters leave no offset, but a slight perturbation of the internal model itself leaves "a small steady state offset error", because "the accuracy of regulation depends on the fidelity of the internal model" (p. 461). The weakly nonlinear extension is local, for constant signals and a smooth plant designed on its linearization (pp. 462, 464). The conclusion calls perfect asymptotic rejection an idealization (p. 464).6

The internal model idea already had a biological echo. Francis and Wonham credit plausibility arguments for an internal model to Kelley and to Conant and Ashby, and note that internal models had a role in theories of visual perception and of brain function (p. 460). Sontag later wrote that the principle "originates in the biological cybernetics literature", and that it is "not a theorem but rather a 'mold' for many possible theorems" (p. 120).7 The core's §3 states the classroom form with its four hypotheses and sets Sontag's theorem beside it.

State the internal model principle with its hypotheses, and keep uncertainty in the plant apart from changes to the reference machinery: for constant signals the first leaves no offset, while the second can.

II · Biology asked the questions again, with molecules

5Goldbeter and Koshland read a saturated modification cycle as a switch

Goldbeter and Koshland asked how a pathway could respond sharply without cooperative enzymes. Cooperativity and a ligand acting at several steps were known answers. They examined a protein that one enzyme modifies and another demodifies, and asked what happens "in the absence of allosteric cooperativity and multiple inputs" (p. 6840).8 In the core's names, the target is YY, its modified form YPY_P, and the two enzymes EE and FF, with capacities kEqEk_Eq_E and kFqFk_Fq_F (their V1V_1 and V2V_2).

Their answer was a steady-state switch. When both converter enzymes are saturated, the transition from unmodified to modified protein becomes far more abrupt (p. 6841). Their response coefficient, the ratio of capacity ratios between 90% and 10% modification, is 81 for a Michaelis-Menten response and falls toward 1 as both enzymes saturate (Eq. 10, p. 6841). With both Michaelis constants at a hundredth of the target total, they compare the steepness to "a Hill coefficient greater than 13". Their Eq. 10 gives about 26 (Figure 3a).

Three ingredients and one rate explain it. The kinetics must be at least partly zero order, the target's total must be conserved, and the system must reach a steady state rather than an equilibrium (pp. 6843–6844). When both enzymes are saturated, the rate of modification is the difference of the two capacities, kEqE−kFqFk_Eq_E-k_Fq_F, whatever the amounts of the two forms, and it continues until one form falls below saturation (p. 6844). That rate is the fact a later reading would use differently.

They also asked what enzyme complexes do. With enzyme totals at 10% of the target or less, the switch barely changes. Larger totals damp it, and how much depends on whether the target stays active while bound, so the readout may count bound forms (Fig. 3 and p. 6842). Figure 3(b) reconstructs that calculation and adds the course's question: what a readout sees if it counts the bound modified form.

Adaptation appears in the paper only as a reason to want sensitivity. The authors name "an adaptive sensory system in which one pathway must be turned on and another pathway turned off" as a place where zero-order ultrasensitivity could supply the needed sensitivity (p. 6844). They add that "the data are not yet available" to say the device is used in cells (p. 6844). Their curves are model calculations.

Read zero-order ultrasensitivity as a statement about a steady state: two saturated enzymes, a conserved target, and a capacity ratio that crosses 1.

6Chemotaxis turned the precision of adaptation into a question about parameters

Barkai and Leibler asked Black's question of a cell. They addressed "the sensitivity of the networks to variations in their biochemical parameters" (p. 913).9 Bacterial chemotaxis was their case: after a step of attractant, the steady tumbling frequency returns to its old value. Earlier models, they wrote, "assumed that the biochemical parameters are fine-tuned" to keep that return (p. 913). Their examples include a 1986 model of exact sensory adaptation by Segel, Goldbeter, Devreotes and Knox (their ref. 17, p. 917).

Their answer was a structure, not a tuning. Adaptation is robust when the methylating enzyme CheR works at saturation and the demethylating enzyme CheB acts only on active receptors, so that "the rate of reverse modification is determined by the system's output" (Fig. 1b legend, p. 913; Box 1, p. 914). They describe it as "a feed-back system" in which the activity is "continuously compared to a reference" (pp. 915–916). They do not use the word integral.

Their evidence was a sampled model ensemble. Starting from a perfectly adaptive reference model, they changed every biochemical parameter at random by a factor of two. Over 80% of 6,157 such altered models stayed within 15% of perfect adaptation, while the adaptation time varied widely (p. 915, Fig. 3). They also predicted which property would move: "the steady-state activity itself, which is not a robust property of the network, depends on the enzyme concentrations" (p. 916).

They proposed the experiment that would test it. Vary the expression of the chemotaxis enzymes, then measure the precision of adaptation, the adaptation time and the tumbling frequency (p. 915).9

Four objects stay distinct here. The pathway, in which CheA also phosphorylates CheB. Barkai and Leibler's reduced module, their eq. (1), written for the modified-enzyme concentration and the activity (p. 916). Yi and colleagues' weighted methylation coordinate (§8). And the course's teaching model, which adopts the activity-only rate form for the mean methylation (core §5).

Ask whether a claim of robustness to parameters was made on a sampled model ensemble or measured in cells, and which property it predicts will move.

7Protein titrations measured which property was robust

Alon and colleagues turned the model's claim into a measurement. To tell the fine-tuned scenario from the robust one, they systematically varied the concentrations of the chemotaxis proteins and measured the response (p. 169).10 Their observable was the tumbling frequency of a population, before and after a saturating step of attractant.

The precision held and the reference moved. As CheR varied, the adaptation time and the steady tumbling frequency changed, while the precision of adaptation stayed within experimental error (p. 169). The measured return is consistent with exact adaptation within experimental uncertainty. Finite data do not establish mathematical exactness.

Measured

Wild-type cells adapted with a precision of 0.98 ± 0.05. As CheR varied between about 0.4 and 6 times its wild-type level, the adaptation time changed from 23 ± 2 min to about 1 min, and the steady tumbling frequency changed about threefold (p. 169).10

A mutant removed one candidate mechanism, within that test. A form of CheB lacking the domain that CheA phosphorylates still adapted, which "seems to exclude CheB phosphorylation as the mechanism of exact adaptation" (p. 170). The test shows that phosphorylation was not required in that assay. It does not exclude phosphorylation from physiological regulation.

The course reads the same split. The core's teaching model reproduces it: cutting CheR slows the return and lowers the adapted activity, and the return stays exact (core §5). The core's §9 names the split: an adaptive property kept across parameter changes, and a reference value that moves.

Design a robustness test as Alon and colleagues did: vary one component's level, then measure both the property claimed robust and a property expected to move.

8Integral control turned the internal model principle into a way to read networks

Yi, Huang, Simon and Doyle read the chemotaxis model as a controller. They proposed that the robustness of perfect adaptation "is the result of this system possessing the property of integral feedback control", and showed that integral control is "structurally inherent" in Barkai and Leibler's model (p. 4649).11 They traced the robustness theory behind that reading to electrical network design, citing Bode's book (p. 4649). Black's line of work met chemotaxis there.

Their necessity argument was linear. They argued that "integral control in some form is necessary" (p. 4649). The proof in their appendix assumes a linearization about an equilibrium whose matrix has "all its eigenvalues in the open left half of the complex plane", and for a nonlinear system concludes only that "the linearization must have integral control". They called the result "a special case of the internal model principle" (p. 4653).

Then they turned the theorem into a way of reading. The necessity "is important to biologists, because they must reverse engineer systems 'designed' by evolution" (p. 4652). They proposed "to catalog the types of basic biochemical networks that can implement integral control" (p. 4652). A theorem about what a designed regulator must contain became a guide to what an evolved network might contain.

Sontag rebuilt the theorem for that use. Citing Yi and colleagues, he wrote that the principle "could help guide experimentalists and modelers" and "help distinguish among mathematical models which do or do not contain internal models" (p. 120).7 For biology he wanted a theorem without a split into plant and controller and without the robustness assumption. He also named how biology changes the objective: control design would "not even notice disturbances", while in biology "signal detection is often an objective, to be followed by a return to default values" (p. 120).

Sontag's theorem is nonlinear, and it trades robustness for detection. It treats a smooth system with one input and one output, affine in the input (p. 122). It assumes adaptation in a strong sense: for every input in the class and every initial state, the solution exists for all time and stays bounded, and the output tends to zero (p. 122). It assumes signal detection, a uniform relative degree: the lower derivatives of the output do not depend on the input's present value, and one derivative does, through a factor that vanishes nowhere (Assumption 1, p. 123). It adds two technical conditions on certain vector fields, completeness and commutation, which hold automatically for linear systems and are "quite strong" for nonlinear ones (Assumptions 2 and 3, p. 123; Remark 3.2, p. 125). And the inputs must come from a Poisson-stable exosystem, whose trajectories keep returning near their start (pp. 123–124). Under these hypotheses the system contains an output-driven internal model of its inputs (Theorem 1, p. 124).7

The course keeps the two theorems apart and looks for coordinates. The core's §1 bookkeeping is stated for an output that responds to the step, which is Sontag's setting rather than a design that never notices. Its §3 and optional A1 set the hypotheses side by side, and its §9 finds a incoherent feedforward loop's integral as a coordinate that no molecule carries. Optional A1 writes that coordinate in Sontag's output-driven form and says that this alone does not check every hypothesis of his global theorem.

Use an internal model theorem as a reading guide only after naming which hypotheses the network meets, and look for the integral as a coordinate, which need not be one molecule.

9A search from function to topology found the switch working as an integrator

Ma and colleagues asked a new kind of question. Instead of explaining one pathway, they asked "What are all network topologies that are capable of robust adaptation?" (p. 761).12 Their kinetic model is part of the question: each of three nodes has a fixed total and an active and inactive form, links are Michaelis-Menten conversions, regulations of one sign add, and basal enzymes supply a missing direction (pp. 762, 772).

Their counts carry their criteria. They searched the 16,038 three-node topologies with a path from input to output, each with 10,000 sampled parameter sets. A sampled circuit counted as adapting when its sensitivity exceeded 1 and its precision exceeded 10. For their usual 20% input step the final output then lies within 2% of its start, not exactly at it (p. 762). The 395 robust topologies are those that adapted in more than 10 of their 10,000 samples, and every one contains a negative feedback loop with a buffer node or an incoherent feedforward loop with a proportioner node (pp. 766–767).

Here the switch's saturation became an integrator in print. When the two enzymes acting on the buffer node are saturated, "the buffer node B integrates the difference between the output activity C and its input-independent steady-state value", and the motif "implements integral control" (p. 764). Precision then follows the flatness of the buffer's nullcline, which zero-order ultrasensitivity supplies, and here they cite Goldbeter and Koshland's 1984 paper (pp. 765–766). The rate that Goldbeter and Koshland used to explain a steep steady state is here proportional to the output's error, and the buffer's level is its integral.

The same constant sets both readings. Figure 4 puts them on one axis. As the saturating enzymes' Michaelis constant falls, the switch's equivalent Hill coefficient rises (Eq. 10 of 1981), and the buffer circuit's precision rises in proportion to its inverse.

The feedforward class came with an open question. The proportioner node tracks the input when its activation is saturated and its inactivation linear (p. 766). In their models the feedforward class adapted better, yet "there have so far not been clear cases where IFFLP is implemented in any biological systems to achieve good adaptation" (p. 771).12 The core's §5 uses the feedback class, and its §6 builds the two-node reduction that Jeynes-Smith and Araujo discuss, which is not a design printed by Ma and colleagues.

Read a topology count with its model and criteria beside it: 395 is a count under one enzymatic model, one sampling rule and a 2% tolerance, not a census of biology.

10A molecular feedforward adapter was identified by choosing between fitted models

Takeda and colleagues took up the gap that Ma and colleagues named. They began from it: "no clear examples of biological networks that use the incoherent feed-forward strategy have been identified" (Introduction, paragraph 1).13 The archived full text is the PubMed Central author manuscript, which carries no page numbers, so this section cites section and paragraph.

They measured near-perfect adaptation of activated Ras through a reporter's location. Cells of the amoeba Dictyostelium received sudden uniform steps of the chemoattractant cAMP in a microfluidic device. The reporter, the Ras-binding domain of human Raf1 fused to GFP (RBD-GFP), preferentially reports activated RasG, while inactive RasG already sits along the plasma membrane (Results, paragraph 1). RBD-GFP moved from the cytosol to the membrane when it bound activated Ras. Its cytosolic signal fell as membrane recruitment rose, then returned (Results, paragraph 2). The measurement is the reporter's location, and Ras activity is inferred from it.

Measured

The normalized cytosolic reporter returned to its prestimulus level within 35 s for cAMP steps from 10−210^{-2} to 10310^{3} nM (Results, paragraph 3).13

Their evidence was a choice between fitted models. They fitted the two simple topologies that can adapt perfectly, an incoherent feedforward loop and an integral-feedback loop with RasGAP as the buffer, to the same 21 data points, with nine free parameters each. "The integral control mechanism was incompatible with our experimental results", because its adaptation kinetics depended strongly on the size of the step (Results, paragraph 9). This is the kind of evidence that §1 called a choice among fitted candidate models: it ranks the candidates that were fitted.

The scope travels with the result. Further integral-control variants were tested in the supplement, which this page has not read. The authors keep open that the adapting module sits between the receptors and RasGEF (Discussion, paragraph 2). A drug that blocks actin left adaptation intact, which removes one feedback route but does not test a load on the output (Results, paragraph 6). The core lists this case among its questions to develop further and asks which of its §10 experiments would settle the wiring.

Treat a model-selection result as a ranking of the candidates that were fitted, and ask which perturbation would separate the ones that remain.

11Writing out the complexes reopened a question the switch had already raised

Jeynes-Smith and Araujo asked whether the switch-based integrator survives its own chemistry. Ma and colleagues had used Michaelis-Menten kinetics, which leave the enzyme-substrate complexes out (p. 2).14 Jeynes-Smith and Araujo asked whether "the prevalent use of simplified models" had given "a false hope for ultrasensitivity-driven RPA mechanisms" (p. 4). They wrote every complex out and sampled parameter sets.

They stated the join between switch and integrator directly. Embedded in a negative feedback loop, "the narrow transition zone for the ultrasensitive switch is converted into a narrow tolerance around an RPA setpoint" (Fig. 1 legend, p. 2). Here RPA is robust perfect adaptation.

With every complex written out, the free output did not track the set point in the cases they studied. Their free active output A∗A^* "never tracks the estimated setpoint", nor any other nontrivial set point, at any rate constants or abundances they examined (p. 5). Where they found tracking over a range of inputs, it was by C3C_3, the complex of A∗A^* bound to the unmodified opposer protein BB, while free A∗A^* fell below their tolerance. They therefore named the pool A∗+C3A^*+C_3 as the adapting variable, and noted that it "corresponds to the location of the ultrasensitive switch in the Goldbeter–Koshland model" (p. 5).14

The question of which form counts is as old as the switch. Goldbeter and Koshland had already shown that appreciable complexes damp the switch, and that the right readout depends on whether bound target stays active (p. 6842; Figure 3b).8 The question did not begin in 2023. What changed is the job: in 1981 the readout decided how sharp a switch looks, and in 2023 it decided which quantity adapts.

The course separates reference tracking from input invariance. Reference tracking asks whether a quantity sits at the set point. Input invariance asks whether it returns to the same value whatever the input. The core's §6 writes the totals, chooses one joint regime, and lets the exact flux balance pin C3C_3 near the set point σ\sigma in that regime. At its illustrative parameter set, the pool A∗+C3A^*+C_3 stays within 5% of σ\sigma over a wide range of inputs, while the full modified total, which also counts the complex that holds the memory, leaves that band. Free A∗A^* there varies by at most 5%, so it is nearly input invariant, yet it sits 99.6% below σ\sigma, so it does not track. The exposition's §3 and §4 derive the joint regime and the flux balance.

Before naming the adapting quantity, ask which complexes the readout counts, and test reference tracking and input invariance separately.

III · Where a chemical memory can live, and how to build one

12Positivity turned "does it integrate?" into "where can the integral live?"

Xiao and Doyle asked where a reaction network can keep an integral. In electrical or computer systems integral variables are built from common elements, they wrote, but their construction "is nontrivial for biomolecular reaction networks" (PDF p. 3).15 This section cites the author manuscript's PDF pages.

The obstacle is positivity. A removal must vanish when the species is absent. An elementary mass-action channel that removes a species has it as a reactant, so the species' amount is a factor in the channel's rate. Higher-order channels and effective nonlinear removal laws are allowed, provided they vanish at zero. Xiao and Doyle wrote the mass-action form with the species as a factor of its removal term (PDF p. 3). A single species then cannot follow an error of either sign once it reaches zero, and this "makes it impossible to directly implement integral variables as concentrations of chemical species in many cases" (PDF p. 4).

They named three ways around it. A constrained integral variable, such as a population that grows or shrinks in proportion to itself. A change of coordinates, in which the integral is a combination of species rather than one species. And an approximation, such as saturated enzymes (PDF pp. 4–5). The integral of a incoherent feedforward loop is such a coordinate, which they called "a feedforward system that has a feedback interpretation" (PDF pp. 4, 7).

The course uses the three routes as a checklist. The core's §4 lists zero-order removal, a difference of two species and multiplicative growth, each with its limit, and its §9 finds the incoherent feedforward loop's coordinate. The exposition's §5 checks that a pinned value can be held with nonnegative amounts and reached.

Ask where a proposed integral can live with nonnegative amounts: a saturated removal, a difference of two species, or the logarithm of a species that grows in proportion to itself.

13Concentration robustness was also found from network structure alone

Shinar and Feinberg asked which structures fix a concentration, with no controller in view. They sought "structural attributes that impart concentration robustness to any mass-action network possessing them" (p. 1389).16 Their examples were two bacterial modules in which approximate robustness had been measured, the osmoregulation system EnvZ-OmpR and the isocitrate dehydrogenase kinase-phosphatase system (p. 1389). No error, controller or integral appears in their question.

Absolute concentration robustness is a property of steady states. A network has it for a species when that species takes the same value in every positive steady state the network admits, at fixed rate constants, so the species is protected even against large changes in the total supply of the network's components (p. 1389). The property says nothing by itself about whether that steady state is reached, which one is reached, or how a load on the species is handled.

Later work placed it among the adaptive properties, with care. Xiao and Doyle wrote that it "is a special case" of perfect adaptation, because perfect adaptation may reject disturbances other than the total (PDF p. 5).15 The exposition's §9 audits Shinar and Feinberg's toy module and, for that network, constructs an exact identity in a logarithm. It derives the threshold total, the return and the stability separately, because the theorem does not supply them.

Read absolute concentration robustness as a statement about steady states at fixed rate constants, and derive return, reachability and stability separately before calling a network adaptive.

14Sequestration was designed as an exact comparator for noisy cells

Briat, Gupta and Khammash asked what integral feedback means when reactions are noisy. Negative feedback was understood for deterministic networks, but "the effect of noise on their regulatory function is not understood" (p. 15).17 Engineered circuits with perfect adaptation were rare, and they called for simple synthetic circuits with integral feedback (p. 17).

Their comparator was a pair that annihilates one to one. In the concentration version of their eq. 1, a reference species Z1Z_1 is made at a constant rate μ\mu and a sensing species Z2Z_2 at the rate θX\theta X, in proportion to the output X, and the two annihilate each other (p. 19). Annihilation need not destroy the pair, since an inert dimer of high affinity would do, and they offered a sigma factor and its anti-sigma factor in E. coli as "a hypothetical, concrete example" (p. 17).

The mean count difference integrates the mean error. Count the molecules in a fixed volume, as Lecture 5 does: NXN_X, NZ1N_{Z_1} and NZ2N_{Z_2}, with Ω\Omega the count-to-concentration factor, so that NX/ΩN_X/\Omega is the concentration of X. The reference then fires with propensity Ωμ\Omega\mu, and sensing with θNX\theta N_X. Annihilation removes one of each controller, so it leaves NZ1−NZ2N_{Z_1}-N_{Z_2} unchanged on every path. A reference event raises the difference by one and a sensing event lowers it by one, so the difference is not a conserved inventory. A path of the difference is constant between events and jumps at event times. It is not a solution of the ordinary differential equation for the concentrations, the core's equation (7). Briat and colleagues state the consequence for the mean: "the mean difference between the two controller species is proportional to the integral of the mean tracking error" (eq. 3, p. 20). In Lecture 5's angle brackets, with finite means, the identity is exact:

ddt⟨NZ1−NZ2⟩=Ωμ−θ⟨NX⟩.\frac{d}{dt}\big\langle N_{Z_1}-N_{Z_2}\big\rangle=\Omega\mu-\theta\langle N_X\rangle.

Their conclusion keeps its conditions. Tracking needs a closed loop that is ergodic, with bounded first and second moments (p. 19). Under those conditions their Theorem 1 gives tracking of the mean, whatever the initial conditions and the other parameters: the stationary mean count of X is Ωμ/θ\Omega\mu/\theta, the set point μ/θ\mu/\theta as a mean concentration (p. 20). Their Theorem 2 gives conditions on the open-loop network under which the closed loop is ergodic (p. 21). A stationary distribution is not a claim that each noisy trajectory converges. They also found that noise can stabilize a loop whose deterministic version is unstable (p. 18).17

The course defines its totals before using their difference. In the deterministic model with reversible binding, the core's §7 writes qZ1=Z1+CZ1Z2q_{Z_1}=Z_1+C_{Z_1Z_2} and qZ2=Z2+CZ1Z2q_{Z_2}=Z_2+C_{Z_1Z_2}, then shows that their difference changes at μ−θX\mu-\theta X at every binding allocation. It tests reachability, dilution and stability separately, and applies the stochastic theorem in its optional A4 to the annihilation form only.

Read a designed integrator by its identity and its hypotheses separately: name the difference that integrates, then treat ergodicity, reachability and growth as separate claims.

15A sequestration switch became a controller that adapts nearly but not exactly in growing cells

The pair that built the controller had first been used in this setting to build a switch. Chen and Arkin put the Bacillus subtilis sigma factor SigW and its cognate anti-sigma factor RsiW into E. coli. SigW activated its own promoter, arabinose controlled the anti-sigma factor, and a fluorescent reporter read the SigW-responsive promoter. They found that "ultrasensitivity from sequestration combined with positive feedback is sufficient to build a bistable switch" (article p. 1; Fig. 2, p. 2).18 Their experiment tested switching and hysteresis, not adaptation.

Aoki and colleagues reused the same pair as a comparator. They reused the B. subtilis pair SigW and RsiW from that earlier report, citing Chen and Arkin (p. 535).19 In their circuit SigW drives the controlled protein AraC and the reporter sfGFP, and AraC closes the loop by regulating the anti-sigma factor's production (p. 536). The reporter is meant to track the controlled protein, and the two are different molecules (Figure 5).

The same sequestration did a different job because the loop closed differently. Closed on SigW itself with positive feedback, sequestration gave a threshold and two stable states. Closed through the controlled protein, the same one-to-one removal compared a reference with a measured output. The pair follows the modification cycle of §9: a source of ultrasensitivity read again as a memory.

Measured

After a protease was induced, the closed loop showed "virtually no change in fluorescence", while the open loop lost more than 40% in most conditions tested. The adaptation was limited to an approximately threefold range of output (p. 536).19

In growing cells the adaptation is near-perfect, not exact. Their theory assumed negligible degradation of the two controller proteins, and they note that "in living cells, these assumptions are not satisfied" (p. 535). The core's §7 shows why. Growth dilutes free and bound controllers alike, so the complex cancels from the difference of the totals and leaves a leak, −λ(qZ1−qZ2)=−λ(Z1−Z2)-\lambda(q_{Z_1}-q_{Z_2})=-\lambda(Z_1-Z_2), where λ\lambda is the growth rate. The leak generally leaves a steady error.

Expect a molecular pair to change its job with its circuit: the same sequestration gives a threshold inside positive feedback and a comparator inside an integral loop.

IV · What the joined record establishes

16Black's question returned as which constants the set point may depend on

Black's amplifier made its gain depend on the feedback path in the high-gain limit. At finite loop gain its sensitivity to the amplifier's gain is 1/(1+H)1/(1+H). As HH grows, the closed-loop gain stops depending on GampG_\mathrm{amp} and depends on ρ\rho instead (p. 3).1 The value the loop holds is set by the part the designer controls.

Francis and Wonham drew the same line between the plant and the reference machinery. Uncertain plant parameters leave no offset, while a perturbed internal model leaves a small one (p. 461).6

The set point of Briat and colleagues' controller depends on two constants. Under their conditions, Briat and colleagues' mean output tracks the set point μ/θ\mu/\theta, as a mean concentration, whatever the initial conditions and the other model parameters (p. 20).17 Here μ\mu and θ\theta are the reference and sensing constants, the core's names.

Gupta and Khammash made the line a definition. A robust perfect adaptation claim "presupposes many specifications", among them "how the set point for the output species is encoded in terms of network parameters" (p. 1).20 They define maximal robust perfect adaptation as adaptation with respect to perturbations in virtually all network parameters, and note that earlier topological conditions "only guarantee insensitivity of the output set point to the designated input parameter" (pp. 1–2).

The course holds the reference constants fixed when it compares designs. The core's §9 asks "robust to what?" with μ\mu and θ\theta fixed, and finds that the incoherent feedforward loop keeps its input invariance while its set point moves with its four rate constants. The exposition's §7 tests input invariance, a fixed reference, a load and growth dilution as separate changes.

Before calling a set point robust, list the constants it depends on, and hold the reference constants fixed when comparing designs.

17Each piece keeps its own question, and naming the memory joins them

The record establishes four things, each with its kind of evidence. Integral feedback removes a constant error under stated hypotheses, and an internal model is necessary under others, in linear theorems with a local nonlinear extension and in Sontag's nonlinear theorem. Chemotaxis adapts precisely across the protein levels tested, a measurement within its precision. A saturated modification cycle and a sequestering pair can each serve as a memory, shown by models and, for the pair, by a built circuit that adapts nearly. And one molecular feedforward example fits better than the feedback models it was tested against.

Five readings of the record deserve distrust. A necessity claim stated without its hypotheses. A count stated without its criteria, such as 395 without its tolerance. A sampled model ensemble read as a measurement. A choice among fitted models read as a unique wiring. And a claim of priority that the sources do not make.

Several arguments remain open. The Koshland group's 1982 paper on amplification and adaptation, which Barkai and Leibler cite for adaptation (p. 913), was not available for this page, so the page claims nothing about when the two were first discussed together.21 Takeda and colleagues' further integral-control variants sit in an unread supplement. Whether cells read free forms or pools, whether natural controllers outrun dilution, whether a given biological model meets the hypotheses of Sontag's theorem, and what a network outside those hypotheses must contain all remain open. Lecture 11 takes up the regimes in which the course's limiting laws hold.

The exposition's six audit questions each come from one of these pieces. The table traces them. It is the reverse of this page's first table: there each source led to a question, and here each question leads back to its sources.
