A cell needs almost no receptors

Lecture 2 derives the capture rate of a perfectly absorbing sphere. A cell is not one: it is a sphere with a few thousand small sticky patches on it. Redo the calculation for the real geometry and the answer barely changes, which turns out to be a statement about design rather than about arithmetic.

What an extension mini-essay is

Every week you write one of these on something from that week's lectures. It is not a summary. It takes one idea and pushes on it: derives something the lecture asserted, connects two things the lecture kept apart, tests a claim against a number, or finds where the lecture is wrong. Eight run over the term and your best six count.

This is the lecturer's example, and it does the “derives something the lecture asserted” version. It is about 1300 words with two figures, which is a reasonable target and not a requirement. What is required is that it be clearly told and well illustrated, so that a classmate who missed the lecture can follow it.

1The question the lecture left open

§7 computes the capture rate of a sphere that absorbs everything touching it. No cell does that, and the lecture does not say what the real geometry costs.

The core's central derivation puts an enzyme at the origin as a perfectly absorbing sphere and gets J=4πDa C∞J = 4\pi Da\,C_{\infty}.2 It then says, in passing, that requiring the substrate to reach a small active-site patch rather than the whole surface “removes most of the sphere” and pushes the rate down.

That is the right instinct and it is almost entirely wrong about the size of the effect. I want to know by how much, because the answer changes what the bound is for.

Take the cleanest version of the question. A bacterium of radius aa carries NN receptor patches, each of radius ss, dispersed over its surface. Everything else about the surface is inert. How does the capture rate compare with the perfectly absorbing sphere of the same size, and how many patches does a cell need before the difference stops mattering?

2Setting it up with §7's machinery

The same boundary-value problem, with a mixed boundary condition instead of an absorbing one.

The physics does not change. Outside the cell, substrate still obeys the steady diffusion equation and the flux through every shell is still constant. What changes is the boundary condition at R=aR = a: instead of C=0C = 0 everywhere on the surface, we have C=0C = 0 on the patches and zero flux everywhere else.

That mixed problem has no elementary exact solution, which is why the lecture does not do it. Berg and Purcell solved it in 1977 by an electrostatic analogy: the capture rate of an absorbing surface is proportional to its capacitance, so the problem becomes finding the capacitance of a sphere carrying NN conducting disks.1 Their equation 8 is the answer:

J=Jmax NsNs+πa,Jmax=4πDa C∞J = J_{\mathrm{max}}\,\frac{Ns}{Ns + \pi a}, \qquad J_{\mathrm{max}} = 4\pi Da\,C_{\infty}(1)

Check the two limits before trusting it. As N→∞N \to \infty the fraction goes to 1 and we recover the perfectly absorbing sphere, which is the lecture's result. As N→0N \to 0 the fraction goes to Ns/πaNs/\pi a, so J→4NDsC∞J \to 4NDsC_{\infty}, which is NN times the capture rate of a single isolated disk of radius ss. Both limits are right, so the formula interpolates between the two things it should interpolate between.

The one line worth memorising

Read (1) as a competition between two lengths. NsNs is the total patch radius summed over all patches, and πa\pi a is a length characterising the cell. When the summed patch radius exceeds the cell's own length scale, the cell behaves as though it were perfectly absorbing. Radii add, not areas. That is the whole result, and it is why the answer in §3 is so small.

3The answer, and it is absurd

Half the maximum capture rate arrives when the patches cover less than a tenth of a per cent of the cell.

Set J=Jmax/2J = J_{\mathrm{max}}/2 in (1). That happens when Ns=πaNs = \pi a, so

N1/2=πa/sN_{1/2} = \pi a / s(2)

Now put in numbers. Take Berg and Purcell's own choices, a cell of radius 1 µm and a receptor patch of radius 10 Å, which is a generous few hundred square ångströms of binding site. Then

N1/2=π×1000 nm1 nm=3142 patchesN_{1/2} = \frac{\pi \times 1000\,\mathrm{nm}}{1\,\mathrm{nm}} = \pf{3142 \text{ patches}}(3)

and the fraction of the cell's surface those patches occupy is

Nπs24πa2=Ns24a2=7.9×10−4,  one part in 1273\frac{N\pi s^{2}}{4\pi a^{2}} = \frac{Ns^{2}}{4a^{2}} = \pf{7.9\times 10^{-4}}, \,\text{ one part in } 1273(4)

Say that out loud, because it is the point of the essay. Three thousand receptors covering under a tenth of a per cent of the surface capture half as fast as a cell that were sticky everywhere. The mean spacing between patches is about 63 patch radii, so the surface is overwhelmingly empty. And the curve keeps paying: 90 per cent of the maximum needs 28 000 patches, still under one per cent coverage.

4Why: one touch is a thousand touches

The result looks impossible until you notice that a molecule which has touched the cell once is already at the cell.

The naive expectation is that a molecule arriving at a surface covered ff of the way finds a patch with probability ff, so the rate should scale as ff and one part in a thousand of coverage should buy one part in a thousand of the rate. That reasoning assumes one arrival.

It is wrong because arrivals are not independent events and there are not one of them. Berg and Purcell's equation 9 gives the probability that a molecule currently a distance ss from a sphere of radius aa hits the sphere at least once more before escaping to infinity:

P=aa+sP = \frac{a}{a+s}(5)

which for a=1 μma = 1\,\mu\mathrm{m} and s=1 nms = 1\,\mathrm{nm} is 0.999. Almost certainly it comes back. The number of return visits is then geometric with that success probability, so its mean is

nˉ=P1−P=a/s=1000\bar{n} = \frac{P}{1-P} = a/s = \pf{1000}(6)

So a molecule does not get one look at the surface, it gets about a thousand, and the patches only have to be found once in a thousand tries. That is exactly the factor by which the naive estimate is wrong, and the two cancel to give (2). The sparseness of the receptors and the redundancy of the encounters are the same number.

5The catch, and it is the interesting part

The result depends on the patches being spread out. Combine them into one patch of the same total area and the rate collapses.

I nearly stopped at §4, and the essay would have been wrong in an interesting way. Equation (1) assumes the patches are uniformly dispersed, and that assumption is doing real work.

Take the same 3142 patches and fuse them into a single absorbing disk of the same total area. Its radius is Ns\sqrt{N}s, so by the isolated-disk limit of (1) its capture rate is 4DNs C∞4D\sqrt{N}s\,C_{\infty}, which against Jmax=4πDa C∞J_{\mathrm{max}} = 4\pi Da\,C_{\infty} is

Jone patchJmax=Nsπa=1N=Jmax/56\frac{J_{\text{one patch}}}{J_{\mathrm{max}}} = \frac{\sqrt{N}s}{\pi a} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} = \pf{J_{\mathrm{max}}/56}(7)

against Jmax/2J_{\mathrm{max}}/2 for the dispersed version. Dispersal is worth a factor of 28, and it costs the cell nothing. Berg and Purcell also checked the other direction and found that placing the patches randomly rather than uniformly changes the current by no more than a few per cent once N>50N > 50.1 So the cell does not need to arrange its receptors carefully. It only needs to not put them all in one place.

6What this says about the lecture

Three things, and the third one is why this belongs on lecture 2 rather than lecture 12.

The bound in §7 is more robust than it looks. The core says that requiring the substrate to reach a small patch “removes most of the sphere”. For a cell surface that is quantitatively wrong: removing 99.9 per cent of the sphere costs a factor of two. The bound 4πDa4\pi Da survives the real geometry far better than the argument for it suggests, and that is worth knowing when you use it.

It does not rescue the enzyme case, and the difference is instructive. An enzyme's active site is one patch, not three thousand, and by (7) a single patch of radius ss on a sphere of radius aa captures at s/πas/\pi a of the maximum. For a 2 nm enzyme with a 0.5 nm site that is about 8 per cent, and the orientational requirement then costs the further orders of magnitude the core quotes. So a bacterium beats the patch problem by having many patches and an enzyme cannot, because it only has one active site. The geometry is the same and the answers differ by three decades.

And it says the receptor layer is not the bottleneck. Lecture 2's argument is that binding is fast, catalysis is slow, and control therefore lives in the fast layer. This calculation adds that a cell can put its binding sites almost anywhere, in almost any number above a few thousand, and still sit near the physical limit on how fast information can arrive at its surface. Detection is cheap. What is expensive is everything the cell does afterwards, which is the subject of the remaining fourteen lectures.

What I would do next, if this were longer

Equation (2) is a prediction and it is checkable. E. coli's chemoreceptor count has been measured. If it sits near 3000 that is a design statement and this essay ends with a discovery. If it sits far above, the receptors are being used for something other than maximising capture rate, which is also a discovery and probably a better one, since Berg and Purcell's other result in the same paper is about the accuracy of a concentration measurement rather than its speed. I did not get a full text with a receptor count in time to settle it, so I am stating the prediction and not the answer.

The one thing I got wrong on the first pass

I first wrote §3 without §5 and concluded that receptor placement does not matter. It does: the factor of 28 in (7) is the difference between a cell that disperses its receptors and one that does not. The assumption was sitting in Berg and Purcell's derivation the whole time and I had read past it. If you find yourself surprised by a result, check what the derivation assumed before you write down what it means.
