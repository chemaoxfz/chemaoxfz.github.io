From order of magnitude in biology to binding and catalysis

The cell's ledger in numbers, and then the two reactions everything in this course is built from: binding and catalysis. The diffusion-limited on-rate is derived to the last boundary condition.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · lecture 2 · core page · 2026-09-03 (Thu) 10:40–12:15 · lecturer-taught

Lecture 1 estimated the physics. This one estimates the cell, and the estimate leads straight into chemistry. Once you know how crowded a cell is and how fast a molecule diffuses across it, you can bound how fast any reaction inside it can possibly go. That bound is the anchor derivation of the course, and binding and catalysis are the two reaction types the rest of the course never leaves.

Part 1

The cell in numbers

How much stuff a cell has, how fast it mixes, and why crowding is a design constraint.

1The cell in numbers

The ledger every later lecture draws on: volumes, masses, concentrations, crowd ing — built from three estimates and one unit convention.

OoM reasoning turns inward, to the cell — the domain of the whole course. Three estimates structure everything: size, mass, count. Start with the length ladder 1: an atom is ~1 Å (10−10 m), a cell ~1 µm (10−6 m), a human ~1 m. The ladder itself is the first lesson: a bacterium is to a human what an atom is to a bacterium.

Size and volume

Two consequences of the volume in (5). First, a pellet of saturated E. coli culture is ~1 µL for ~109 cells — 1 mL of culture medium is ~10−3 cells by volume, less dense than our classroom. Second, in this volume, "one molecule per cell" is already a concentration: 1/10−15L / 6×1023 ≈ 10−9 M = 1 nM. This single conversion is the seed of lecture 5: at 1 nM, discreteness is a property of the system.

Mass per molecule: the log-average trick

A glucose/nucleotide is ~3×102 Da (size ~ (3×10²)1/3 ≈ 6 Å). An amino acid is ~102 Da — because the true range is ~60–200 Da, and the average over a log scale is √(60×200) ≈ 10². The aside is worth stopping on: orders of magnitude are the right thing to average when quantities span them.

How many proteins per cell?

  • Protein length: lower bound ~50 aa, upper bound ~5×10³ aa → geometric mean √(2×105) ≈ 300 aa ≈ 30 kDa.
  • 1 pg / 30 kDa ≈ 2×107 protein molecules per E. coli — the upper bound if the whole cell were protein. Reality: 103–104 per protein type.

Volume: the genome is small, proteins are not

The genome: 5×106 bp × 0.3 nm/bp ≈ 103 µm of DNA — long enough to need folding ≥103-fold to fit. Its volume, though, is ~1.5×10−3 µm³ ≈ 1% of the cell. Proteins: 300 aa × ~100 Å³ × 107 molecules ≈ 0.3 µm³ — 20–40% of the cell's volume. The asymmetry is the lesson: the genome is information (small volume, long length), the protein is machinery (large volume, crowded). Crowding puts a cap on how many components you can pack, and it is one of the reasons cells are not dilute solutions.1

Take-away

Three numbers do most of the work: 1 fL (the volume), 30 kDa (the typical protein), 1 µM ≈ 1/fL (the regulator scale). Everything else in this course — 10⁹ encounter rates, 10⁻³/s degradation, 1 nM switch thresholds — is these three numbers times a scaling law.

2Diffusion and the mixing of a cell

Brownian motion is a random walk: τ ≈ x²/D with D ∝ 1/r — and the mixing times that follow run the whole course.

The same random-walk logic, in biological currency. Brown (1827) watched pollen jiggle under a microscope with no apparent force. Estimate its size: visible under a 1800s microscope, not visible to the naked eye → ~10 µm (eukaryotic-cell scale). Its motion: visible jiggling over seconds (~1 µm on seconds timescale), but not significant net movement over minutes → to move one body length (10 µm) takes about an hour:

Dpollen ≈ x²/τ = (10 µm)² / 3600 s ≈ 10−2 µm²/s (11)

Now for the scaling that turns this into biology. Diffusion is a balance between the thermal kicks (∝ L², since it scales with the surface being hit) and viscous drag (F ∝ η L v), so mobility per force is v/F ∝ L−1, and Einstein's relation gives

D = μ kBT,   μ ∝ v/F ∝ L−1 ⇒ D ∝ 1/L (12)

A protein is ~1 nm (from (300 aa)1/3-ish size scalings), 104-fold smaller than pollen, so Dprotein ≈ 100 µm²/s. Then the mixing times, which the rest of the course quotes:

Table 6. τ = x²/D for a protein (D ≈ 100 µm²/s): the size scaling of mixing.
CompartmentSizeτWhat it means
E. coli1 µm0.01 swell-mixed on any biological timescale
mammalian cell10 µm1 sstill fast

The axon row is the payoff: since τ ∝ x², mass transport by diffusion dies at large distances, and the existence of axonal transport (and vesicles, and motors) is predicted by this scaling rather than assumed. That is what a null model is for.

Part 2

Reactions in a cell

What a reaction is, the two arrow vocabularies, and the two steps every enzyme takes.

3How a cell does chemistry

Cellular changes happen through chemical reactions, ultimately governed by diffusion kinetics — at constant temperature and pressure.

A cell changes its state by reactions: molecules meet, decide, and become other molecules. Three properties frame everything. First, cellular chemistry happens at roughly constant temperature and pressure, so free energies, not heats, are the currency (that is lecture 7's background). Second, the rate at which molecules can meet is set by diffusion — which is why this derivation is the course's first rate estimate.1

Third — the feature that matters most for this week — reactions in cells are catalyzed. Thermodynamically favorable reactions can sit for years on their own (spontaneous glucose degradation, peptide-bond cleavage, ATP hydrolysis), and enzymes accelerate them by factors of 1010 or more. The consequence is a clean separation of two questions, and the course keeps them apart deliberately: whether a reaction can proceed (thermodynamics, lecture 7) versus how fast it does (kinetics — this week).

4Elementary vs composite

Straight arrows are mechanisms; squiggly arrows are bookkeeping for the net change.

The third notation is the one the scribe names explicitly: the net-change form, (X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ) → (X₁ + P₁, …, Xₙ + Pₙ), written on molecule counts rather than species symbols. It is the notation that makes the accounting of lecture 3 automatic, because it registers only who lost and who gained what.

5Four biological examples

The same two arrows, four real systems — and the habit of asking which one a diagram really is.

The 2025 scribe lists four examples worth having on the wall:1

  1. Cell replication: N ⇝ 2N (or N ⇝ N+1) — composite; the mechanism is a whole cell cycle.
  2. Photon doubling in a laser: photon + excited atom → 2 photons + ground atom — elementary in the interesting direction, and one of the few systems where the "elementary" step is genuinely one event.
  3. Diffusion through a passive membrane channel: Aout → Ain — elementary as written (it is a single crossing), but the rate hides transport physics.
  4. ATPase pumping H⁺ out: H⁺in ⇝ H⁺out — composite, and energy-driven: this is exactly the kind of arrow that lecture 7 (energy) asks about.
The habit to install now

When anyone writes a rate law, ask: is that arrow elementary? If it is composite, the rate law must be derived (lecture 6's Michaelis–Menten) or measured — never assumed from the arrowhead.

6Two steps: binding, then catalysis

An enzymatic reaction is binding plus catalysis; the fastest possible rate is the association rate, which is governed by diffusion.

An enzyme and its substrate collide, bind, and then the enzyme's atoms orient the substrate into the product. Two steps, one rate ceiling. The fastest reaction rate is limited by the association step — react immediately upon association — and that association is diffusion-limited, hence the name: diffusion-limited on-rate.1 The scheme:

E + S ⇌ C ⇝ E + P (1)
What "fast" means, in the end

The diffusion limit sets kon ≈ 10⁹ M−1s−1. At 1 mM substrate, one enzyme gets ~10⁶ encounters per second. But most enzymes are far slower — bottlenecked by catalysis at 10–10² reactions per second. Keep both numbers; the gap between them is the whole subject of enzyme engineering, and the answer to most "why isn't this reaction faster?" questions.

Part 3

How fast can it be

The diffusion-limited on-rate, derived, and the units checked.

7The diffusion-limited on-rate, derived

Fick's law in spherical symmetry, two boundary conditions, one integration chain — and 10⁹ s⁻¹M⁻¹ falls out.

This is the anchor derivation. Set up: an enzyme at the origin, substrate diffusing in a steady state; the enzyme acts as an absorbing sphere of radius a. Step 1 — flux at the surface. At a point on the enzyme surface, Fick's first law:

j(a) = D ∂C/∂R |R=a,   roughly ≈ D C∞/a (2)

Step 2 — conservation gives the profile. In spherical symmetry, steady state means the flux through any sphere is the same: 4πR² j = const ≠ 0. In the steady state of Fick's second law, ∂C/∂t = D∇²C, the angular derivatives vanish and

(1/R²)·d/dR (R² dC/dR) = 0 ⇒ R² dC/dR = A ⇒ C(R) = −A/R + B (3)

Step 3 — boundary conditions. Absorbing surface: C(a) = 0; far away: C(∞) = C∞. So B = C∞, A = aC∞:

C(R) = C∞(1 − a/R) (4)

Step 4 — the gradient at the surface, the flux, the rate. ∂C/∂R|a = C∞/a, so j = D C∞/a, and the total rate is flux × area:

J = j·4πa² = 4πDa C∞ = kon C∞,   kon = 4πDa (5)

Step 5 — the number. With D ≈ 10² µm²/s for a small molecule, a ≈ 1 nm, and the unit conversion for a molecule-per-liter concentration:

kon = 4π · 10² µm²/s · 1 nm · (6×10²³/mol) · (1 L / 10¹⁵ µm³) ≈ 10⁹ s−1M−1 (6)

Note what the derivation does for you: it turns "how fast can a reaction be?" into a Newtonian boundary-value problem — a physical question, forced by the math to have one answer. If a claimed on-rate exceeds 10⁹, it is wrong, and you can say why.

8The units, checked

j = D ∂c/∂R, dimension by dimension — the way every estimate in this course should be checked.

The scribe includes the dimensional check of the flux law, and it is the template for every future estimate:1

Table 2. Dimensional verification of j = D ∂c/∂R.
QuantityDimensions

The habit generalizes: before believing a rate, check its units; before believing a scale, check its exponents. (The same table, with t ≈ x²/κ and J = 4πDaC∞, is what gives lecture 1's and this lecture's numbers legs.)
