Two rate constants and the gap between them

Molecules in a cell meet at up to 10¹⁰ M⁻¹s⁻¹ and enzymes turn over at about 10 s⁻¹. Five separate research programmes produced those two numbers and none of them owns the distance between. That distance is not an inefficiency. It is the structural fact that makes a cell controllable.

Fangzhou Xiao · Westlake University · CCBS (EST 5053), Fall 2026 · the research pass behind lecture 2 · 2026-09-01

This is the scholarship behind a ninety-five minute lecture. The lecture teaches how to count what is in a cell, how to turn a size into a diffusion coefficient, how to derive the fastest possible bimolecular rate from a boundary-value problem, and what enzymes actually do about that bound. Those four things arrived from four different centuries of work, and they are usually taught as a list of numbers. They are not a list. Put end to end they are an argument, and the argument has a conclusion that decides how the rest of the course is built.

The conclusion is this. Binding reaches equilibrium roughly ten thousand times during a single catalytic turnover. That ratio is measured, not assumed, and it is what licenses treating a cell as a fast reversible layer regulating a slow irreversible one. A second, entirely independent route reaches the same place: a metabolite pool empties in a tenth of a second and gene expression takes forty minutes, so a fast regulatory layer is forced to exist, and the only candidate on the timescale ladder is binding. Allostery was discovered in 1956. The timescales predict it.

What follows traces where each piece came from, what its authors actually claimed, and where the retelling has drifted. Several things had to be corrected in the writing. Robert Brown was not watching pollen. The famous protein count is an upper bound that assumes a cell is dry. The lac repressor's impossible speed has been over-generalised by its own literature. And the constant Michaelis and Menten actually fitted in 1913 was not the Michaelis constant.

How to read this

Part I introduces the five programmes that produced the numbers, generously, and ends on the fact none of them owns. Part II states the frame in three sections, and §10 is the crux. Part III is the payoff, including the two interactive demos. Part IV is about teaching, which is what this document is ultimately for. Part V says where the argument sits and where it breaks. Every claim resting on a paper carries a superscript, and every reference in the list was read in full unless it is marked NEEDED.

Part I

Five programmes, and the fact they leave on the table

Each of these produced half of the argument. None of them wrote down the other half.

1Counting a cell

A tradition that treats the composition of a bacterium as a measurable object, and what it is for.

There is a research programme whose product is a table. It runs from the mid-century bacterial physiologists, who weighed cultures and assayed their protein, RNA and DNA, through Bremer and Dennis's growth-rate-dependent ledger, to Milo's BioNumbers database and Phillips and Milo's Cell Biology by the Numbers. Its claim is modest and it is correct: cell biology has a numerical spine, the spine is knowable to about a factor of two, and knowing it changes which questions are worth asking.

The spine for one E. coli is short enough to memorise. A volume of one to four femtolitres.1 A wet mass near one picogram, about 70% water. Protein at 0.24 g per mL of cell, which is three to four million protein molecules in a cubic micron.2 A total metabolite pool near 300 mM, of which glutamate alone is 96 mM.3 A genome of 4.6 million base pairs, 1.6 mm long and half a percent of the volume.

The correction that made the programme interesting

The count of proteins is the place where the tradition audits itself. Divide the whole wet mass by the mass of a 33 kDa protein and you get 2×107, which is the number many courses still teach. It assumes the cell is dry and made only of protein. The measured figure is three to four million per femtolitre, about five times smaller. Milo went further and found that several proteome-wide mass-spectrometry censuses report totals three to ten times below even the correct estimate, and argued they should be renormalised.2 An estimate that catches an experiment is doing more than an estimate that agrees with one.

What the programme structurally cannot do is tell you which of its numbers matter. A table has no argument in it. The ledger says a metabolite sits at millimolar and an enzyme at micromolar, and it does not say that the ratio of those two numbers is why enzymes are saturated, which is why binding equilibrates, which is why the cell can be regulated at all. That inference needs a second programme.

2What Brown actually saw

The founding observation of the second programme, and the detail that almost every retelling gets wrong.

In June 1827 Robert Brown put pollen of Clarkia pulchella in water under a single-lens microscope and watched particles move with no visible cause. Nearly every textbook says he watched pollen grains jiggle. He did not, and he could not have.

Pearle and colleagues went back to the plant, grew it, and repeated the observation with both a modern microscope and a reconstructed single-ball lens.4 A Clarkia pollen grain is about 100 µm across. What Brown saw were organelles ejected from grains that burst under turgor: oblong amyloplasts, which store starch, at a radius near 2 µm, and spherical spherosomes, which store lipid, clustered at a radius of 0.5 µm.

The size matters because Brownian displacement falls with particle radius. Pearle et al. tabulate the mean one-dimensional displacement in one second: 0.74 µm for a particle of radius 0.5 µm, and 0.07 µm for one of radius 50 µm.4 With a lens of magnification 370 the eye cannot resolve a displacement below about 0.2 µm. So the organelles were visibly moving and the grain was visibly still, and that is exactly what Brown reported.

The move that made it science

Brown's contribution was not the observation. Others had seen the motion. His contribution was the control. He repeated it with dried and long-dead plants, with fossil wood, with ground glass, with metals, and with a fragment of the Sphinx, and got the same motion every time. That is what removed the vital explanation. It is the same move as a null model: the interesting step was not measuring the effect, it was eliminating the only mechanism anyone believed in.

3From a jiggle to a molecular constant

Einstein's 1905 paper is not about Brownian motion. It is about whether molecules exist, and it says so in its first paragraph.

Einstein's opening line is worth reading for what it assumes and what it does not:

In this paper it will be shown that according to the molecular-kinetic theory of heat, bodies of microscopically-visible size suspended in a liquid will perform movements of such magnitude that they can be easily observed in a microscope… If the movement discussed here can actually be observed … then classical thermodynamics can no longer be looked upon as applicable with precision to bodies even of dimensions distinguishable in a microscope. Einstein, 1905

He is not explaining a known phenomenon. He is predicting a measurable consequence of atoms and offering it as a decision procedure.5 The two results that follow are the ones this course uses. First, that the mean square displacement grows linearly in time rather than quadratically, so the observable is a diffusion coefficient and not a velocity. Second, the relation between that coefficient and the friction on the particle. For a sphere in a fluid of viscosity η, combining the Einstein relation with Stokes drag gives

D = kBT / (6πηa) (1)

which was obtained independently and slightly earlier by William Sutherland. The point for a biologist is not the formula, it is the direction of inference. One number, measured on something you can see, fixes a constant that applies to everything you cannot. Brown's own description of his particles is enough. A radius of 0.5 µm and a displacement of about a micron in a second give D ≈ 0.43 µm²/s, so kBT/6πη = D·a = 0.21 µm³/s against a true value of 0.245. Thirteen percent, from an 1827 observation with no numbers in it. Divide by the radius of a protein and you have the diffusion coefficient of a protein.

Perrin then closed the argument experimentally, tracking colloidal particles and extracting Avogadro's number from their statistics, which is the measurement that ended serious opposition to atomism. The programme's claim is therefore stronger than "diffusion is random walking". It is that a microscope is a thermometer for the molecular world, and it is why an order-of-magnitude estimate calibrated on something visible can be trusted about something invisible.

4What an arrow came to mean

A rate law is not a description of a reaction. It is a consequence of a claim about mechanism, and the claim is made by the shape of the arrow.

Guldberg and Waage stated the law of mass action to the Academy in Christiania in 1864, and the statement is more careful than the version usually attributed to them:

The substitution force, other conditions being equal, is directly proportional to the product of the masses provided each is raised to a particular exponent. … If the same masses of the interacting substances occur in different volumes, then the action of these masses is inversely proportional to the volume. Guldberg and Waage, 1864, in Abrash's translation

Two things are already there.6 The exponents are explicitly allowed to be arbitrary, which is the modern notion of reaction order and not the stoichiometric coefficient. And mass divided by volume is concentration, which is why the law is about concentrations rather than amounts. What is not there is any statement about when the exponents equal the stoichiometry. That took another two decades and it is the whole content of the word "elementary".

Van't Hoff's Études de dynamique chimique in 1884 is where kinetics becomes a subject with its own laws rather than a set of observations.7 The distinction that survives is between molecularity, which is how many molecules take part in one microscopic event, and order, which is what you measure. They coincide exactly when the written step is the microscopic event, and that is the definition of an elementary reaction.

Horn and Jackson closed the loop in 1972 by making mass-action kinetics a mathematical object in its own right, with a stoichiometric matrix, a reaction graph, and theorems about what such systems can and cannot do regardless of the rate constants.8 That is the formalism lecture 3 uses, and its hypotheses are exactly "every arrow is elementary". The moment a squiggly arrow enters, the theorems stop applying, which is the practical reason to keep the notations apart.

5The ceiling

How fast can two molecules in solution possibly react? The answer is a hundred years old and it is a boundary-value problem.

Smoluchowski asked it in 1916, in a paper on the coagulation of colloids, and got the answer by solving the steady diffusion equation around a perfectly absorbing sphere.9 The result is the master relation of this whole course:

kon = 4π(DA + DB)(aA + aB) (2)
Read in the original, and the textbook version is his

An earlier version of this page attributed 4πDa to Smoluchowski on the authority of two secondary sources, because the 1918 paper could not be obtained. It has now been read. The attribution holds, and it holds more completely than the hedge implied.

Every piece of the modern derivation is in it, in the same order. The absorbing boundary is stated as a deliberate substitution: the assumption that the sphere of radius R holds fast every arriving particle can, he writes, be replaced by the assumption that it acts perfectly adsorbing, so that the concentration at the surface is held at zero. He then solves the spherically symmetric diffusion equation, keeps the transient, and drops it under the condition t > R²/D, noting that in Zsigmondy's experiments the excluded initial stage lasted 10−3 to 10−4 seconds and was therefore of no practical significance. What is left is his equation (10), w = 4πDRν0. He also gives the two-body case, in which each particle diffuses and the coefficients add.

So the tidy derivation taught today is not a later tidy-up. It is his, including the caveat that most modern retellings leave out: the equivalence between the diffusive flux and the collision count holds only while the expected number of arrivals is small.

The factor of four that his R hides

Smoluchowski's R is an encounter radius, not a particle radius, and he is explicit that it comes out on the order of the sphere diameter. For two identical spheres of radius a the encounter radius is 2a and the relative diffusion coefficient is 2D, so the rate constant is 4π(2D)(2a) = 16πDa, four times what a careless reading of 4πDa gives.

Equation (2) is written with the sums for exactly this reason. Whenever you see a bare 4πDa quoted for a self-association, check which radius and which coefficient the author meant. This is the most common factor-of-four error in the literature that uses this bound.

Everything after that is refinement of the boundary condition. Collins and Kimball replaced the perfect sink with a partially absorbing surface, which is what happens when the intrinsic chemistry is not infinitely fast. Debye added the electrostatic term for charged reactants. Alberty and Hammes carried the whole apparatus into enzyme kinetics. Eigen built relaxation methods that could actually measure rates in the microsecond range and found proton transfers at 1010 to 1011 M−1s−1, at the ceiling.

Two later results matter more for biology than any of those. Berg and Purcell asked what happens when the absorbing surface is not the whole sphere but a scattering of small patches, which is the real situation for a receptor-covered cell.10 The answer is counterintuitive and it is the reason enzymes work at all: because a molecule that misses a patch does not leave, but diffuses back and tries again, a sphere carrying a few thousand receptors of nanometre size captures at close to the rate of a perfect absorber even though the receptors cover a thousandth of its area. Northrup and Erickson then measured the cost of orientation by Brownian dynamics, and found the basal protein-protein association rate to be 105 to 106 M−1s−1 once a few simultaneous contacts are demanded.11

What the programme established

There is a hard ceiling on any bimolecular rate constant in solution, it is computable from two sizes and two diffusion coefficients, and for biological molecules it is 109 to 1010 M−1s−1. Getting close to it requires either a very large target or a mechanism, and there are only two known mechanisms: pull the substrate in with a field, or change the dimension of the search.

6The floor

A century of enzyme kinetics, and a surprise about what its founding paper actually measured.

Victor Henri derived the saturating rate equation first, in 1903. Michaelis and Menten's 1913 paper on invertase is the one that stuck, because they controlled pH and did the experiment properly. Johnson and Goody translated it completely in 2011 and reanalysed the original data, and what they found is a genuine correction to the field's memory of itself.12

The original Michaelis constant was not the Michaelis constant

Michaelis and Menten did not stop at initial velocities. They fitted their full time courses to the integrated rate equation, including product inhibition, and reduced everything to a single global constant. That constant was Vmax/KM, which is kcat/KM × E0, what we now call the specificity constant.12 It is the quantity with a physical ceiling, the one section 5's programme bounds, and the one Bar-Even measures a century later. The field spent that century quoting KM instead.

Michaelis and Menten assumed the binding step was at equilibrium. Briggs and Haldane replaced that in 1925 with the steady-state assumption, which is weaker and almost always better, and the two-paragraph note in which they did it is one of the highest ratios of consequence to length in the literature.13 Section 11 returns to the difference, because the difference is exactly the timescale separation this essay is about.

The distributional question, though, waited until 2011. Bar-Even and colleagues pulled kinetic parameters for thousands of enzyme-substrate pairs out of BRENDA, filtered them to natural substrates using KEGG, and plotted the histogram.14

The medians are kcat ≈ 10 s−1 over 1942 pairs, kcat/KM ≈ 105 M−1s−1 over 1882, and KM ≈ 100 µM over 5194. Their own reading is evolutionary. Enzymes in central carbon and energy metabolism run at a median of 79 s−1 against 2.5 s−1 in secondary metabolism, a thirty-fold difference in exactly the direction selection pressure would predict. Their conclusion is that "the catalytic efficiency of some enzymes toward their natural substrates could be increased in many cases by natural or laboratory evolution".

What "perfect" meant when the word was coined

"Catalytic perfection" is not a loose phrase. It enters the literature in a specific paper with a specific definition, and the definition is narrower than the retelling. Albery and Knowles, in the last of eight back-to-back papers in 1976, define an efficiency function Ef whose value is 1 when the reaction is controlled entirely by diffusive steps.30 For the interconversion of dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate they compute 2.5×10−11 for a simple carboxylate base, and 0.6 for triosephosphate isomerase. The enzyme is more than 1010 times as efficient as the free base, and it is within a factor of two of the ceiling.

Their criterion is a statement about the entire free-energy profile, not about one rate constant.31 An enzyme is perfect once it has lowered every transition state below that of the diffusive step and raised every bound intermediate above the free-enzyme-plus-substrate state. At that point there is nothing left for selection to erode. And, in a detail almost always dropped, it is the diffusion limit for the thermodynamically less stable substrate that binds, because that is the highest barrier the system must cross.

Perfection is not a property of the enzyme alone

The sharpest thing in Albery and Knowles is easy to miss. Ef depends on the ambient substrate concentration, and they evaluate it at the measured in vivo value of 40 µM. Their Figure 10 shows why it must: at low substrate the efficiency is flat, and at high substrate it falls, because the enzyme spends its time tied up as a complex rather than waiting to be found.

So "TIM is a perfect enzyme" and "the median enzyme is five decades below the ceiling" are not measured on the same axis. The first is a whole-profile statement evaluated at a physiological concentration. The second is a comparison of kcat/KM against 4πDa. Both are true. Neither is a translation of the other, and reading Figure 4 above as "most enzymes are 105-fold less perfect than TIM" is a category error that this page previously left room for.

Where the argument went next

Albery and Knowles rank three ways an enzyme can improve, in increasing order of difficulty: uniform binding, in which all bound states shift together relative to the free states; differential binding, which requires the enzyme to discriminate between bound intermediates; and catalysis of an elementary step, which requires it to discriminate between an intermediate and a transition state. Only the last is what a chemist would call catalysis, and it is the hardest to evolve.

That ordering has become an argument about origins. Noor and colleagues take the observation that enzyme superfamilies often share a substrate-handle binding motif rather than a catalytic chemistry, and propose that many families began binding-first rather than chemistry-first.32 Uniform binding, the cheapest move on Albery and Knowles' list, is then where enzymes start rather than a footnote. Gerlt's retrospective is the readable way in to what the eight 1976 papers actually established and what has survived.33

7The fact nobody owns

Put programme five's ceiling next to programme six's floor and something falls out that neither of them states.

The specificity constant of the median enzyme is 105 M−1s−1. The Smoluchowski ceiling for a metabolite meeting an enzyme, computed from equation (2) with measured diffusion coefficients and radii, is 1.4×1010 M−1s−1. The ratio is 1.4×105.

Five orders of magnitude is a lot of room, and the literature treats it as a puzzle about enzymes. It is not only that. It is also a fact about time. If catalysis is that slow relative to encounter, then during one turnover the binding equilibrium is re-established thousands of times. The bound fraction of enzyme is therefore not a dynamical variable at all on the timescale of the flux. It is an equilibrium quantity, computable by statistical mechanics, and free to be used as a control input.

The claim of this essay

The gap between kon and kcat is not primarily a statement about how good enzymes are. It is the structural condition that separates a cell into two layers: a fast reversible one that equilibrates and therefore regulates, and a slow irreversible one that carries flux and therefore does the work. Every programme in Part I holds one end of that statement. None of them writes it down.

Part II

The frame

Two objects, one relation, and one measurement.

8Two rate constants

Define them by their formulas, then read off where each one's value comes from.

Definition

For the elementary association E + S → C, the on-rate kon is the constant in the mass-action flux v = kon[E][S]. Its units are M−1s−1, which is a volume swept per unit time per mole.

For the composite step C ⤳ E + P, the turnover number kcat is the constant in v = kcat[C]. Its units are s−1, a frequency.

The asymmetry in the definitions is the asymmetry in the physics. kon has an upper bound derivable from first principles, because getting two molecules into contact is a transport problem and transport is governed by an equation we can solve. kcat has no such bound below 106–107 s−1, because what happens after contact is a chemical rearrangement inside a protein, and no general theory predicts it from structure. So one constant is physics and the other is biology, and the whole of enzymology lives in the second.

Note also that kon is not directly comparable to kcat. One is second order and one is first order. The comparable quantity is the specificity constant kcat/KM, which is second order and which reduces to kon exactly when every encounter leads to product. That is why kcat/KM is the parameter with a ceiling, and it is why it was the right thing for Michaelis and Menten to fit in 1913.

9The master relation, dated

Where the 4π comes from, and what changes when the target is smaller than the searcher.

The derivation is four steps and it is worth doing rather than quoting. Put the enzyme at the origin as an absorbing sphere of radius a. In steady state the flux through every concentric shell is equal, so 4πR²j is a nonzero constant. The steady diffusion equation in spherical symmetry then integrates twice to C(R) = −A/R + B. The boundary conditions C(a) = 0 and C(∞) = C∞ fix the constants and give C(R) = C∞(1 − a/R). Differentiating at the surface and multiplying by the area gives J = 4πDaC∞.

Halford and Marko give a version with no calculus in it at all, and it is the one to teach.15 If targets sit at concentration c, a searcher is typically a distance r = c−1/3 from one. In a time r²/D it either hits the target, with probability a/r, or wanders off. Waiting r/a times longer makes success near certain, so the total time is (r/a)(r²/D) = 1/(Dac) and the rate constant is Dac/c = Da. The 4π is what the exact calculation adds.

The bound

For two species diffusing freely in solution and reacting on every contact, kon = 4π(DA+DB)(aA+aB). No unguided bimolecular reaction in solution exceeds it.

The factor that matters most in practice is hidden in a. It is the reactive radius, not the molecular one. When the target is a small site on a large molecule, the bound falls in proportion. Halford and Marko take a/d = 0.1 for an operator site on DNA and get 108 M−1s−1 rather than 109.15 Berg and Purcell's patch result is the counterweight: a target that is small but repeatedly revisited does much better than its area suggests.10 Both corrections are geometric, both are order unity to two, and neither changes the decade.

10The measurement that licenses everything

Put the two constants into one expression and the separation of timescales stops being an assumption.

The binding step is a reversible elementary reaction, so a perturbation of it decays exponentially with a rate that is the sum of the forward and backward pseudo-first-order rates:

τbind = 1 / ( kon([E] + [S]) + koff ),    koff = konKD (3)

Use the medians of Part I and nothing else. Enzyme at 1 µM, substrate at 1 mM, KD = 100 µM from the median KM, and kon = 108 M−1s−1, which is already two decades below the ceiling:

τbind = 9 µs   against   τcat = 1/kcat = 100 ms   ⇒   separation ≈ 104 (4)
The crux

A model that writes the bound fraction of an enzyme as an equilibrium expression while writing the flux as a differential equation is not making a convenient approximation. It is quoting a measured four-decade ratio. Every quasi-steady-state reduction in this course inherits its validity from equation (4), and so does the use of equilibrium statistical mechanics on a cell that is manifestly not at equilibrium.

Part III

What the frame buys

Five consequences, each with a number, and two of them you can move with a slider.

11Timescale separation, measured

Michaelis and Menten assumed it. Briggs and Haldane weakened the assumption. Segel and Slemrod said what it costs. The number in §10 says when you may.

The history here is a slow clarification of one question. Michaelis and Menten took the binding step to be at equilibrium, which requires kcat ≪ koff. Briggs and Haldane replaced that with the steady state d[C]/dt ≈ 0, which is weaker and holds under a different condition.13 The two give the same functional form and different meanings for KM, and generations of textbooks have blurred them.

Segel and Slemrod made it precise by identifying the small parameter. The quasi-steady-state approximation is valid when

ε = ET / (KM + ST) ≪ 1 (5)

which is a statement about concentrations, not about rate constants: there must be far less enzyme than substrate. Gunawardena's reading is the one to carry: the assumption is fundamentally about timescale separation, and it is the same mathematical move as singular perturbation.17 Lecture 6 makes that rigorous.

What Part I adds is that both conditions are satisfied in a real cell for ordinary metabolic enzymes, and satisfied by a wide margin, and that this is a fact you can check rather than a hope. An enzyme at 1 µM with KM = 100 µM and substrate at 1 mM gives ε = 10−3.

Demo 1 · the separation, on a logarithmic clock

The full mass-action system on ten decades of time. The complex fills first and then sits still while product accumulates. Push kcat up until the two curves collide, and the reduction of lecture 6 stops being legal.

12The regulatory layer is forced

The same conclusion from the other end, using two timescales and no rate constants at all.

Ask a physiological question instead of a kinetic one. Can a cell control its metabolism by changing how much enzyme it has?

A metabolite pool turns over in pool size divided by flux. E. coli on glucose takes up about 8 mmol per gram dry weight per hour, and a gram dry weight occupies about 3 mL of cell, so the glycolytic flux density is 0.73 mM/s. Bennett et al. measured the pools.3 Dividing gives turnover times of 0.12 s for phosphoenolpyruvate, 0.44 s for ATP, 1.0 s for 3-phosphoglycerate and 21 s for fructose-1,6-bisphosphate.

Gene expression cannot approach that, and the reason is a one-line derivation rather than a measurement. For a protein that is not actively degraded in a cell growing at rate μ, dilution is the only loss:

dP/dt = β − μP  ⇒  response half-time = ln2/μ = the doubling time (6)

Forty minutes for a well-fed E. coli. Even the fastest conceivable transcriptional route is slow: 80% of E. coli mRNA half-lives lie between 3 and 8 minutes.18

The conclusion, and it is forced

Expression responds in 2400 seconds. The PEP pool empties in 0.12 seconds. Expression is 2×104 times too slow to control it, and still more than a hundred times too slow for the slowest pool measured. A cell regulating metabolism only through expression would empty or overflow its pools thousands of times before the first new enzyme arrived. Therefore a faster regulatory layer must exist, and binding at 9 µs is the only candidate on the ladder.

Demo 2 · two ways to hold a metabolite pool

Influx doubles five minutes in. One cell corrects it by making more enzyme, which it can only do at the dilution rate. The other corrects it by allosteric feedback on the enzyme it already has. Same gain, different speed. Note the second failure mode of the slow route: high gain applied late overshoots and empties the pool.

13And it was found where the argument says

The prediction of §12 was confirmed nine years before anyone made it, by people who were not looking for it.

In 1956 Umbarger reported that isoleucine inhibits threonine deaminase, the first enzyme of its own biosynthetic pathway.19 In the same year Yates and Pardee reported the same architecture for pyrimidines: the end product of the pathway inhibits its first committed step.20 Neither inhibitor resembles the substrate of the enzyme it shuts down, so the inhibition cannot be competition for the active site. There had to be a second site.

Monod, Changeux and Jacob named that arrangement allosteric in 1963, and Monod, Wyman and Changeux gave it a model in 1965 that is still the one taught: two conformations in equilibrium, ligands with different affinities for each, and cooperativity arising from the shift of that equilibrium rather than from any interaction between sites.21 Stadtman's 1966 review collected what had by then become a general principle of metabolic control.22

Why the model is an equilibrium model

The MWC model is written entirely in terms of equilibrium constants. No rate appears in it. That is not a simplification made for tractability, it is licensed by equation (4): the conformational and binding equilibria settle four decades faster than the catalytic step they modulate. The 1965 paper does not argue this. It did not need to, because everything the model was fitted to was a steady-state measurement. The timescale argument is what says the model is right rather than merely convenient.

The modern version of the same finding is systematic rather than anecdotal. Link, Kochanowski and Sauer screened for allosteric metabolite-protein interactions in E. coli and found them throughout central metabolism, including regulatory couplings nobody had proposed.23 The layer predicted by two timescales is not a handful of famous examples. It is the normal way metabolism is controlled.

14Where the estimate is beaten

The interesting cases are the ones that break the bound, and both known mechanisms are worth knowing.

The lac repressor was measured in 1970 to find its operator faster than 1010 M−1s−1, about a hundred times the protein-DNA diffusion limit of 108. The resolution is facilitated diffusion: the protein binds DNA non-specifically anywhere along its length, then slides in one dimension until it reaches the operator or falls off. A one-dimensional search of a large effective target beats a three-dimensional search for a small one.

The signature is visible in the data and it is unambiguous. EcoRI methyltransferase reaches 5.1×107 M−1s−1 on a 14 bp substrate and four times that on a 429 bp substrate carrying the same site.16 Adding DNA that is not the target makes the target easier to find. That is impossible for a three-dimensional search and automatic for a one-dimensional one. Adam and Delbrück had proposed exactly this reduction of dimensionality in 1968.

A correction to how this story is usually told

Halford and Marko went through the record and found the generalisation does not hold.15 The pre-1986 lac experiments used DNA carrying several repressor sites and a filter assay that could not say which was occupied. Later footprinting did reproduce 1010 M−1s−1 for lac, and Gal repressor and integration host factor are also above 1010. But the majority of measured protein-DNA associations sit within a factor of three of 108 M−1s−1, which is the ordinary limit. "DNA-binding proteins are impossibly fast" is folklore built on a handful of systems.

The second mechanism is electrostatic steering. A charged substrate falling into an oppositely charged field around an active site arrives faster than diffusion alone would allow. Barnase associates with barstar at 5×109 M−1s−1 despite requiring a precise alignment, and colicin nucleases bind their immunity proteins above 1010 at low salt.16 The caveat is important and it is usually dropped: raising the salt to physiological levels drops those same rates to about 107 M−1s−1, because the charge is screened.15 Electrostatic acceleration is real, and it is largely a low-salt laboratory phenomenon.

15Where the estimate is exact

One enzyme sits at 73% of a ceiling computed from nothing but two radii and two diffusion coefficients.

Superoxide dismutase is the cleanest test of equation (2) in biology, because its substrate is tiny. Superoxide has a radius near 0.2 nm, so Stokes-Einstein gives it D = 1230 µm²/s, and Cu,Zn SOD is a 32 kDa dimer with Rmin = 2.1 nm. The bound for this specific pair is

kon = 4π(1230 + 117)(2.3 nm) = 2.3×1010 M−1s−1 (7)
Measured against computed

Eukaryotic Cu,Zn SOD runs at 2×109 M−1s−1. Prokaryotic versions from Photobacterium leiognathi and Salmonella typhimurium reach 8.5×109 and 1.3×1010. A single Glu→Gln mutation gives 1010, and a second engineered variant reaches 1.7×1010, the fastest rate ever reported for any enzyme.16 Against the bound in equation (7), that is 73%. Computed in analysis/cell_ledger.py.

This is worth more than the usual statement that SOD is diffusion-limited. A pair-specific bound, computed from geometry with no fitted parameter, is within a factor of 1.4 of a measured rate constant. That is the level at which the physics of section 5 is actually true, and it is why the ceiling is worth teaching as a law rather than a rule of thumb.

16The correction the ledger made to itself

Every rate in Part I was measured in water. A cell is not water, and the difference is a factor of eleven.

The cytoplasm of E. coli carries protein at about 0.24 g per mL and total macromolecule near 0.3 g per mL, which puts macromolecular volume occupancy somewhere between a sixth and a third of the cell. That is not a dilute solution, and the consequence was measured directly.

Elowitz and colleagues bleached and photoactivated GFP inside living E. coli and found D = 7.7 ± 2.5 µm²/s, against about 87 in dilute buffer.24 The slowdown is size-dependent and steep: a 72 kDa MBP-GFP fusion drops to 2.5 ± 0.6, and even a six-histidine tag on GFP halves it. Konopka and colleagues later showed that the effect is not a fixed viscosity but depends on cytoplasmic water content, so it changes with osmotic condition.25

What this changes, and what it does not

It changes the mixing times of §10 by an order of magnitude, and it changes kon in a cell from 1.4×1010 to 3.8×109 M−1s−1. It does not change the conclusion of §10, because the gap it has to survive is four decades and crowding costs one. Order-of-magnitude arguments earn their keep exactly here: a correction that would ruin a two-fold claim leaves a ten-thousand-fold claim standing.

Part IV

How it came to be taught

The pedagogy is a programme of its own, and it has an argument.

17A feeling for the numbers

Why a table of cell numbers became a thing worth teaching, and who argued for it.

Physics has taught estimation deliberately for a long time. Caltech ran an order-of-magnitude physics course, Mahajan wrote it up twice, and the tradition has a canon of worked problems. Biology did not, and the reason is not laziness. For most of the twentieth century the numbers were not there to be taught: nobody knew how many ribosomes a cell had, or how fast a polymerase moved, to better than an order of magnitude, and a course built on unreliable numbers teaches unreliability.

That changed, and Phillips and Milo's 2009 essay is the moment it became a programme.26 Their argument has two parts. The first is that biology now has enough good numbers to reason with. The second, and the interesting one, is that a number is only useful if you have a feel for the ones around it, so the numbers have to be learned as a connected system rather than looked up. BioNumbers was the infrastructure built for that, with each entry carrying its provenance so a reader can see how a number was obtained and decide whether to trust it.27 Cell Biology by the Numbers and Physical Biology of the Cell are the textbooks.

What this course adds to that tradition is the insistence that the ledger is a premise and not a product. Lecture 2 spends its first half counting, and the counting would be inert if it stopped there. It does not stop there because the counts have a consequence, and the consequence is the architecture of the rest of the course. A student who leaves with the table has half the lecture. A student who leaves with equation (4) has the lecture.

The problem set as a record of intent

There is a second record of what a course thinks its material is for, and it is more specific than the lecture notes: the problem set. What a lecturer chooses to make students do is a sharper statement than what the lecturer chooses to say.

This lecture's own history makes the point. The argument the 2026 lecture ends on, that metabolite pools turn over far faster than gene expression can respond and that a fast regulatory layer is therefore forced, was not a result in the 2025 course. It was homework 1, problem 2.2, set as a three-part exercise with the conclusion left to the student: find the turnover time of a metabolite pool, find the time to change an enzyme level by expression, compare them, and then say whether the comparison justifies the existence of allostery and post-translational modification.

From exercise to result

An argument that is set as an exercise is one the teacher believes a student can reconstruct. An argument that is stated as a result is one the course is willing to build on. Moving problem 2.2 from the first category to the second is the largest structural change between the 2025 and 2026 versions of this lecture, and it is what licenses lecture 8 to treat the two-layer architecture as established rather than as a modelling choice.

The promotion also cost something, and it is worth naming. A student who derives the conclusion owns it. A student who is handed it has to be persuaded. That is why the 2026 core carries the argument twice, once through rate constants and once through pools and fluxes, and why the harder open-ended version of the problem survives in the exposition.

The same set's problem 3 is a different kind of statement. It asks students to read six papers and write a chemical reaction network for each, and the six are chosen so that no two fail in the same way: the toggle switch and the repressilator for synthetic circuits, combinatorial BMP signalling for promiscuous binding, mammalian multistability for the limits of counting steady states, the lac utilization network for feedback through a transporter, and Chandra, Buzi and Doyle's glycolytic oscillation paper, which is control theory rather than biology and is on the list because it asks what the controller is. The instruction attached to all six is a single sentence: keep the elementary and composite distinction in mind. That sentence is the lecture.

18What an agent does not remove

The arithmetic in this document is now free. The judgement in it is not, and it is what the lecture is for.

Every number here could be produced by an AI agent in seconds. The Stokes-Einstein evaluation, the unit conversion in equation (2), the pool-over-flux division, all of it. That is not a reason to stop teaching them, and it is not a reason to keep teaching them either. It is a reason to be clear about what the exercise was ever for.

Look at what actually had to be decided in writing this. That the calibration particle is a spherosome and not a pollen grain, which no amount of arithmetic would have caught, and which required going back to a 2010 paper that went back to the plant. That the naive protein count is an upper bound rather than an estimate, which is a judgement about which term in a product is doing the wrong work. That the specificity constant, not KM, is the quantity with a ceiling, which required reading a translation of a 1913 paper. That the lac repressor result is real but has been over-generalised, which required a review that says so. That crowding costs one decade and the argument needs four, so the correction can be absorbed.

The skill, stated

Order-of-magnitude reasoning is not the ability to multiply approximately. It is the ability to decide which constraint decides the problem, to notice when an estimate is a bound rather than a value, and to know how much error an argument can absorb before it stops being an argument. None of that got automated. What got automated is the part that was never the point.

Part V

Situating and testing

Where this sits, where it breaks, and what a student could do about it next week.

19Where this sits

Programme by programme: what is compatible, what is a real disagreement, and what is a restatement of something already known.

Table 2. The claim of §7 against the five programmes of Part I.
ProgrammeRelation to the claim
The ledger (Neidhardt, Bremer-Dennis, Milo, Phillips)Supplies the premises, states no conclusion. Every number in §10 and §12 comes from this tradition. The inference from those numbers to an architecture is not made in it.
Brownian motion (Brown, Einstein, Sutherland, Perrin)Compatible, and prior. It supplies equation (1) and the licence to calibrate on the visible. Nothing here disagrees with it.
Mass action (Guldberg-Waage, van't Hoff, Horn-Jackson)Compatible, and constraining. It is what makes the elementary-composite distinction load-bearing rather than pedantic. The claim of §7 lives inside its formalism.
Diffusion-controlled kinetics (Smoluchowski, Debye, Collins-Kimball, Berg-Purcell)Supplies the ceiling. This programme reads its own bound as a statement about enzymes. The reading here is that it is also a statement about the separability of timescales, which the programme does not make.
Enzyme efficiency (Michaelis-Menten to Bar-Even)A real disagreement about interpretation. Bar-Even et al. read the gap as weak selection. This essay reads it as a structural condition. Both fit the histogram. §20 says how they differ.

Two things here are restatements and should be labelled as such. That binding is fast and catalysis is slow is not news to any enzymologist. That allostery regulates metabolism on a fast timescale is textbook. What is not standard is putting the two together and treating the ratio as the thing that defines the model class of a cell, which is the thesis of lecture 8 and the reason lecture 2 exists in the form it does.

20Where it could be wrong

Three ways, stated so that someone could go and check.

The evolutionary reading might be the whole story. Bar-Even et al. argue the gap reflects weak selection on rate, and their evidence is real: a thirty-fold difference in median kcat between central and secondary metabolism is exactly what selection would produce.14 If that is all it is, the separation is contingent and could fail in any given system, and the framework here is a good bet rather than a principle. The two readings differ in one prediction. The structural reading says the separation should hold even where selection for speed is strong, because it is a condition for control rather than a consequence of neglect. Central-metabolism enzymes at 79 s−1 are still 104 below their ceiling, which is weak evidence in the structural direction. Stronger evidence would be a systematic look at the most heavily selected enzymes in the cell.

The separation fails in real systems, and it is not rare. A transcription factor at 10 nM binding an operator with KD = 1 nM has τbind of tens of milliseconds, comparable to a catalytic turnover. Promoter occupancy is therefore not always quasi-static, and the telegraph model of lecture 6 exists precisely because it is not. Bennett et al. found the same thing metabolically: concentrations exceed KM for most pairs, but in lower glycolysis they sit near KM and every reaction is near equilibrium.3 Where the cell needs reversible flux it gives up the separation deliberately.

The bound is pair-specific and can be misapplied. Table 1 shows the ceiling moving by a factor of fifty between a small ion and two proteins in cytoplasm. Quoting "the diffusion limit is 109" without saying which pair is how the lac repressor folklore started. Any use of the bound should carry the radii and diffusion coefficients that produced it.

What would settle it

A measurement, on a single organism, of the joint distribution of kcat and in vivo substrate concentration for the enzymes carrying the largest fluxes, with the pair-specific Smoluchowski bound computed for each. If the most heavily selected enzymes still sit three or more decades below their own bounds, the structural reading survives its hardest test. Davidi and colleagues built most of the machinery for this when they extracted in vivo catalytic rates across the E. coli proteome.28

21What to do on Monday

Four things a student in this course could actually do, cheapest first.

  1. Redo §12 for a mammalian cell. Volume 2000 µm³, doubling time about 24 hours, metabolite concentrations similar. Does the argument get stronger or weaker, and by how much? Then predict what fraction of mammalian metabolic regulation should be post-translational, and go and check whether it is.
  2. Compute the pair-specific bound for five enzymes you care about. Take their molecular weights, get Rmin from Erickson's formula29, get substrate radii from their molecular weights the same way, and evaluate equation (2). Then look up their kcat/KM in BRENDA and plot the ratio. Anything above 0.1 is remarkable and worth reading about.
  3. Break demo 1 on purpose. Find the parameter region where the quasi-steady-state reduction is wrong by more than 10%, express the boundary in terms of Segel and Slemrod's ε, and check whether that boundary is where the theory says it should be.
  4. Find a paper that writes a mass-action rate law under a squiggly arrow. This takes about ten minutes. Then work out whether the error matters for the paper's conclusion, which takes longer and is the actual exercise.

22What the record establishes

Four things are settled, one is an argument, and one is a correction to the field's memory.

Settled. There is a computable ceiling on bimolecular rate constants in solution, it is 4π(DA+DB)(aA+aB), and it is accurate: the fastest enzyme ever measured sits at 73% of its own pair-specific value. The median enzyme sits five decades below. Binding therefore equilibrates about 104 times per catalytic turnover, and that ratio is a measurement rather than a modelling assumption. And metabolite pools turn over hundreds to tens of thousands of times faster than gene expression can respond, so a fast regulatory layer is not optional.

An argument. Whether the gap is best understood as weak selection or as a structural condition for control. Both readings fit the data of §6, they differ in what they predict about the most heavily selected enzymes, and §20 says what would settle it. This course takes the structural reading, and lecture 8 states it as a thesis that students are invited to attack.

A correction. Four pieces of standard retelling did not survive contact with the sources. Brown was watching organelles half a micron across, not pollen grains a hundred microns across, and the corrected calibration is better than the textbook one. The familiar count of 2×107 proteins per bacterium is an upper bound that assumes the cell is dry. The constant Michaelis and Menten actually determined in 1913 was the specificity constant, not the Michaelis constant. And the lac repressor's superlative on-rate, though real, has been generalised into a claim about DNA-binding proteins that the measurements do not support.

None of those corrections changes the conclusion. All four of them change how it should be taught, which is the point of writing this down.
