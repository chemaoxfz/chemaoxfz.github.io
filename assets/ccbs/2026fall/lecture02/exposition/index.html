The cell in numbers, and the two reactions that follow

A full tutorial on lecture 2. How to count what is in a cell, how to turn a size into a speed, how to write a reaction so that its rate law is honest, and how to bound that rate from first principles. Everything is derived rather than quoted, and the point of all of it arrives in section 22.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · the exposition for lecture 2 · about 90 minutes to read, longer if you do the checkpoints

There are three documents for this lecture and they do different jobs. The core page is the spine, and it is what happens in the room. The lineage essay is the scholarship: where these ideas came from, what their authors actually said, and where the retelling has drifted. This is the third one. It is the document you read afterwards, slowly, if you want to be able to do this yourself rather than recognise it when someone else does it.

It assumes almost nothing. If you can multiply powers of ten and you remember what a derivative is, you have enough. Everything else is built here. It is longer than it needs to be for an exam and about the right length to actually learn something.

How to use this

Read parts 1 and 2 with a pen. There are four checkpoints with worked solutions at the end, and they are the only part of this document that will tell you whether you have understood it. Two interactive tools sit in sections 10 and 19, and the one in section 19 is genuinely useful for your own work: it computes the diffusion-limited on-rate for any pair of molecules you name. Section 25 says how this document was built, because this is also the worked example of what your team's exposition should look like.

Part 0

Orientation

What you should be able to do at the end, and the only three moves you need to get there.

1What this is

Four abilities, stated concretely enough that you can check whether you have them.

By the end of this you should be able to do the following without looking anything up.

  1. Convert between counts and concentrations in a cell, in your head. Someone says "there are about 50 copies of that transcription factor". You say "so about 80 nanomolar, which is around its dissociation constant, so it is operating in the interesting regime".
  2. Turn a size into a speed, and a speed into a time. Someone shows you a compartment. You say how long it takes to mix, and therefore whether treating it as well stirred is legitimate.
  3. Look at a reaction diagram and know what it is claiming. If the arrow is elementary you write the rate law immediately. If it is not, you say so, and you say what would have to be measured.
  4. Bound a rate. Given any two molecules, compute the fastest they could possibly react, and compare a claimed number against it.

The fifth thing is not an ability but a piece of knowledge, and it is the one the rest of the course is built on: binding equilibrates about ten thousand times during one catalytic turnover. Section 22 derives that and section 23 shows what it forces.

2The three moves

Every estimate in this document is one of these, or two of them stuck together.

Lecture 1 introduced order-of-magnitude reasoning on physical problems. Here is the compressed version, because you are about to use it eleven times.

Move 1 · Bound it

Replace the quantity you want by something you can compute that is definitely bigger, or definitely smaller. A bound is often easier than an estimate and it is always more honest. Example ahead: section 5 bounds the number of proteins in a cell by assuming the cell is entirely protein.

Move 2 · Calibrate on something you have seen

If a law has one unknown constant, one observation of any system fixes it, and then it applies to every other system. Example ahead: section 10 fixes kBT/6πη from a particle under an 1827 microscope and then uses it on a protein.

Move 3 · Scale

Once you have one case and a scaling law, you have every case. Example ahead: section 11 gets the mixing time of a neuron from the mixing time of a bacterium and nothing else, because τ ∝ x².

There is a fourth thing that is not a move but a discipline: carry the units. Most errors in this material are unit errors, not conceptual ones, and section 18 is a whole section on the single conversion that trips everyone.

Part 1

Counting

Five estimates that between them fix every concentration you will meet this term.

3A cubic micron is a femtolitre

The single most useful identity in quantitative cell biology, and the conversion that falls out of it.

Start from the definition of a litre: 1 L = 10−3 m³. A cubic micron is (10−6 m)³ = 10−18 m³. So

1 µm³ = 10−18 m³ = 10−18 / 10−3 L = 10−15 L = 1 femtolitre (1)

An E. coli is about that size. Measured properly it runs from 1.5 fL when growing slowly to 4.4 fL in rich medium, with the width holding near 1.26 µm and the length doing the changing.1 Use 1 fL when you want a round number and remember that it is the small end.

Now the conversion. A concentration is molecules per volume divided by Avogadro's number. One molecule in one femtolitre is

c = 1⁄NA ÷ 10−15 L = 1⁄6.02×1023 ÷ 10−15 L = 1.66×10−9 M = 1.7 nM (2)
Learn this one

One molecule per bacterium is 1.7 nanomolar. Ten copies is 17 nM. A thousand copies is 1.7 µM. A million copies is 1.7 mM. You will use this more often than any other number in the course, and it is worth being able to produce it without thinking.

4How heavy is a protein

Two ranges, one trick, and a number you should carry for the rest of your career.

We want the mass of a typical protein, and neither ingredient is a single number. Amino acids run from glycine at 75 Da to tryptophan at 204 Da. Proteins run from about 50 residues to about 5000. Both spreads are multiplicative, which means the arithmetic mean is the wrong average.

The log-average, and why

If a quantity spans orders of magnitude, average its logarithm. The estimate from two endpoints a and b is the geometric mean √(ab). The reason is that you are equally uncertain about each decade, not about each unit, so the middle of your uncertainty is the middle of the exponent range. Taking (a+b)/2 instead would put your estimate near the top of the range, because the top dominates a sum.

Check the trick on the amino acids, where we know the answer. Two endpoints give √(75×204) = 124 Da. The geometric mean over all twenty is 133 Da. The arithmetic mean is 137 Da. All three agree, which is the sign of a well-behaved distribution, and it tells you the trick is not doing much work here.

One correction, and it matters. A residue inside a chain has lost a water molecule to the peptide bond, so subtract 18 Da:

mresidue ≈ 133 − 18 ≈ 110 Da (3)

Now protein length, where the trick does do work. Two endpoints give √(50×5000) = 500 residues. The measured abundance-weighted mean for E. coli is 300 residues, and for eukaryotes 400.2 The estimate is high by a factor of 1.7. That is a completely typical order-of-magnitude outcome and you should not be disappointed by it. Use the measured value:

mprotein = 300 × 110 Da = 33 000 Da = 33 kDa (4)
Why the log-average was still worth doing

Because you will meet quantities where nobody has measured the mean. The point of checking a trick on a case with a known answer is to learn how much to trust it on cases without one. Here the answer is: within a factor of two, which is exactly what this course promises and never more.

5How many are there

Do it wrong first. The wrong answer is instructive and it is the one most courses teach.

A cell of 1 fL at the density of water weighs about a picogram. Bacteria are slightly denser than water, around 1.1 g/mL, so call it 1.1 pg. Divide by the mass of one protein:

N ≤ 1.1×10−12 g⁄33 000 × 1.66×10−24 g = 1.1×10−12⁄5.5×10−20 = 2×107 (5)

Stop and look at what you just assumed. You divided the entire wet mass by the mass of a protein, which is only correct if the cell is made of nothing but protein. It is not. It is mostly water. Equation (5) is move 1, a bound, and it is a legitimate and useful one as long as you say so.

To turn it into an estimate, put in what a cell is actually made of. About 70% of the wet mass is water, so the dry mass is 30%. Protein is about 55% of the dry mass, with RNA at 20% and DNA at 3%. Therefore

cp = 1.1 × 0.30 × 0.55 = 0.18 g/mL of protein (6)

and the measured value for E. coli is 0.24 g/mL.2 Either one gives

N = cp⁄mprotein = 3 to 4 million proteins per femtolitre (7)
The five-fold error, and who else makes it

The bound in (5) is about five times the truth, and the whole error is the water. This is not a pedantic point. Milo went through the published proteome-wide censuses and found that several of them report totals three to ten times below the correct estimate, and argued they should be renormalised against it.2 An estimate whose job is to catch an experiment is worth more than one whose job is to agree with it.

6The concentration ladder

Three rungs. A dissociation constant means nothing until you know which rung it sits next to.

E. coli has about 4400 protein-coding genes. Spread 3.5 million molecules over them and the mean is around 800 copies per species, which by equation (2) is about 1.4 µM. Real copy numbers span from a handful to tens of thousands, but micromolar is the centre.

Metabolites are three decades higher, and this has been measured directly. Bennett and colleagues quantified over a hundred metabolites in glucose-fed E. coli and found a total pool near 300 mM, dominated by a few compounds, with glutamate alone at 96 mM.3

The reading you should take away

Metabolic enzymes have KM near 100 µM and see substrate at millimolar, so they run saturated: the rate barely depends on how much substrate there is. Transcription factors have Kd near 1–10 nM and sit at tens of nanomolar, so they run poised: the rate depends on concentration very sensitively. Same physics, opposite design choice. When you meet a new system, find its rung first.

7Is a cell crowded

Two volume budgets, and the asymmetry between information and machinery.

Volume follows from mass through the partial specific volume of protein, 0.73 mL/g, which converts to 1.21 Å³ per dalton. So a 33 kDa protein occupies about 40 nm³. If it were a sphere, its radius would be

Rmin = (3V/4π)1/3 = 0.066 · M1/3 nm = 0.066 × 330001/3 = 2.1 nm (8)

That formula, with M in daltons, is Erickson's, and it is worth memorising because it turns any molecular weight into a size.4 Two warnings. It is a minimum: a real protein carries a hydration shell and is not spherical, so the radius that governs its motion through water is 20 to 40 percent larger. And it applies to compact globular proteins, not to fibrous ones.

Now the budget. Three and a half million proteins at 40 nm³ each is 1.4×108 nm³ = 0.14 µm³, so protein alone is about 15% of the cell volume. Add RNA and DNA and the total macromolecular mass concentration is around 0.26 g/mL. The cell is not a dilute solution.

Compare the genome. The E. coli chromosome is 4 641 652 base pairs. B-form DNA rises 0.34 nm per base pair and is 2 nm across, so

L = 4.64×106 × 0.34 nm = 1.6 mm,    V = π(1 nm)² × 0.34 nm × 4.64×106 = 0.005 µm³ (9)
The asymmetry

The genome is 1600 times longer than the cell and takes up half a percent of it. The proteome is made of things a few nanometres across and takes up fifteen percent. Information is long and cheap. Machinery is small and expensive. That asymmetry is why a cell can afford to carry instructions for far more proteins than it expresses at once, and it is the physical reason regulation of expression exists at all.

Do these before going on. Solutions are at the end, and looking at them early wastes the exercise.

  1. A protein is present at 12 copies in an E. coli. What is its concentration? If its dissociation constant for a DNA site is 5 nM, is the site mostly occupied or mostly empty?
  2. A mammalian cell is 2000 µm³. How many proteins does it contain? (You need one number from this part and nothing else.)
  3. Which is bigger, an mRNA or the protein it encodes? Take a 300-residue protein and 330 Da per nucleotide, and answer in mass. Then sketch the two to scale, remembering that mRNA is not globular.
  4. A ribosome is about 2.5 MDa. Use equation (8) to get its radius, and compare with the measured diameter of about 21 nm. Why is your answer too small?
Part 2

Moving

One coin flip, one formula, and the most useful scaling law in biology.

9Why ⟨x²⟩ grows like t

Derived from a coin flip in six lines. No probability theory needed.

A molecule in water is hit from all sides by other molecules. Model that as a walker that takes a step of fixed length ℓ every τ seconds, left or right with equal probability. After n steps its position is

xn = ℓ(s1 + s2 + … + sn),    si = ±1 with equal probability (10)

The average of xn is zero, because every step is as likely to go one way as the other. That is not useful. Square it first:

⟨xn²⟩ = ℓ² ⟨(∑si)²⟩ = ℓ² ⟨∑isi² + ∑i≠jsisj⟩ (11)

Each si² = 1, so the first sum is n. Every cross term ⟨sisj⟩ with i ≠ j averages to zero, because the two steps are independent and each is equally likely to be +1 or −1. So

⟨xn²⟩ = nℓ² = (t/τ)ℓ² ≡ 2Dt,    with   D ≡ ℓ²/2τ (12)
What just happened

The root mean square displacement grows like √t, not like t. A random walker is not slow, it is differently slow: to go twice as far takes four times as long. Every counterintuitive fact in the rest of this part is that one square root. And the diffusion coefficient is not a fundamental constant, it is a combination ℓ²/2τ of a step size and a step time, which is why it depends on the particle and the fluid.

In three dimensions the same argument applies to each axis independently, so ⟨r²⟩ = 6Dt. For order-of-magnitude work we drop the factor and write τ ≈ x²/D, which overestimates by six. That is fine here and it would not be fine in a paper.

10Stokes-Einstein, and the calibration

One formula gives D for anything, and one 1827 observation fixes its only constant.

Equation (12) defines D but does not tell you its value. For that you need the physics of the balance between thermal kicks and viscous drag. Einstein's relation says the diffusion coefficient is the mobility times the thermal energy, D = μkBT, where the mobility is the velocity per unit applied force. For a sphere of radius a in a fluid of viscosity η, Stokes' law gives the drag as F = 6πηav, so μ = 1/6πηa and

D = kBT⁄6πηa   ⇒   D ∝ 1/a (13)

The scaling is the useful part and it is worth saying out loud: a particle ten times smaller diffuses ten times faster. Not a hundred times, not the square root, exactly ten.

Calibrating on Brown's microscope

Everything in (13) except the radius is one constant, kBT/6πη. One observation of any particle fixes it. Take the founding one.

In 1827 Robert Brown watched particles from Clarkia pulchella pollen jiggle in water. He was not watching the pollen grains, which are 100 µm across and do not visibly move. He was watching organelles ejected from burst grains, and the spherical ones, spherosomes, have a modal radius of 0.5 µm.5 Such a particle moves about 0.74 µm in one second. Inverting ⟨|x|⟩ = 0.80√(2Dt):

D = (0.74/0.80)² / 2 = 0.43 µm²/s  ⇒  kBT/6πη = D·a = 0.21 µm³/s (14)

The true value at 25 °C is 0.245 µm³/s. A qualitative description from 1827, with no measurement in it, gets a fundamental constant to 13%. That is move 2, and it is the whole reason order-of-magnitude reasoning works.

Now divide by the radius of a protein, 2.1 nm from equation (8):

Dprotein = 0.21 µm³/s ÷ 0.0021 µm = 100 µm²/s in water (15)

Tool 1 · the random walk, and where √t comes from

Two dozen walkers in two dimensions, with the √(4Dt) circle drawn around them. Change the particle radius and watch D change by the inverse. The plot on the right is ⟨r²⟩ against time, which should follow the dashed 4Dt line. It wanders, because 24 walkers is a small sample, and that wandering is itself the point: a single molecule does not obey the law, a population does.

The correction that changes every number below

Equation (15) is the value in water. Inside a cell it is wrong by an order of magnitude. Elowitz and colleagues measured GFP directly in living E. coli and found D = 7.7 ± 2.5 µm²/s against about 87 in buffer.6 The slowdown is worse for bigger things: a 72 kDa fusion falls to 2.5, and even adding six histidines to GFP halves it. Use 10 µm²/s for a protein and 200 µm²/s for a metabolite inside a cell, and always say which you mean.

11Mixing times

Combine the last two sections and you can say whether any compartment is well stirred.

Equation (12) rearranged is τ ≈ x²/D. That is the whole tool. Put in the in-cell diffusion coefficients from section 10.

The move that makes this science rather than bookkeeping

Read the last row as a prediction. Passive diffusion cannot supply a metre-long axon. Not slowly: four thousand years against a human lifetime. 12Checkpoint 2

  1. A mitochondrion is about 1 µm long. Is it well mixed on the timescale of ATP synthesis, which runs at roughly 100 turnovers per second per synthase?
  2. Your friend says a signalling protein diffuses from the membrane to the nucleus of a mammalian cell "instantly". Give the actual time, and say what you would need to measure to check whether the cell is instead using active transport.
  3. Why is it that no organism has evolved a single cell one centimetre across that relies on diffusion? Give the number, not the adjective. Then name a real cell of about that size and say what it does instead.
  4. Equation (13) says D ∝ 1/a. Somebody claims that a protein twice as heavy diffuses half as fast. Are they right? By how much are they wrong?
Part 3

Reacting

What a reaction is at the level of one collision, and what a diagram is entitled to claim.

13Where mass action comes from

Derive it from collisions rather than asserting it, because the derivation tells you when it fails.

Consider two species, A and B, in a volume V, with NA and NB molecules. Suppose that whenever one A and one B come within a distance a of each other, they react with some fixed probability. How often does that happen?

Fix attention on one particular A. It sweeps out some volume per unit time as it moves, call it σ, with units of volume per time. Any B whose centre lies in that swept volume gets hit. The number of B molecules per unit volume is NB/V, so this one A is hit at a rate

rate for one A = σ · NB/V (16)

There are NA of them, and each is hit independently, so the total number of collisions per unit time is σ NANB/V. Divide by V to express the flux as a concentration per unit time and write [A] = NA/V:

v = σ[A][B] ≡ k[A][B] (17)
Three things this derivation tells you that the textbook statement does not
  1. The rate constant is a swept volume per unit time. That is why its units are M−1s−1, and section 17 computes exactly that volume.
  2. The product form comes from independence. If A and B are not independently distributed, because they are tethered, or confined to a membrane, or the cell is not well mixed, then (17) is wrong. Section 11 is what licenses independence inside a bacterium.
  3. The exponents come from how many molecules must meet at once. Two molecules give a product of two concentrations. That is what "elementary" means, and it is why the exponents in an elementary rate law are the stoichiometric coefficients.
1864

Guldberg and Waage stated the law in Christiania in 1864, and their statement is more careful than the version usually attributed to them. They wrote that the force is proportional to the product of the masses "provided each is raised to a particular exponent", explicitly allowing arbitrary exponents, and separately that the action is inversely proportional to the volume, which is what makes it a law about concentrations.7 What they did not say is when the exponents equal the stoichiometry. That took another twenty years and it is the entire content of the next section.

14Elementary, composite, and four traps

The most important distinction in the course, and the four ways people get it wrong.

Definition

A reaction is elementary if the step as written is a single microscopic event: the listed reactants meet, and the listed products result, with nothing intervening. Drawn with a straight arrow. Its rate law is v = k∏i[Xi]αi with α the stoichiometric coefficients.

A reaction is composite if it summarises several steps. Drawn with a squiggly arrow, or in net-change form (X1,…,Xn) ⤳ (X1+γ1,…,Xn+γn). No rate law follows.

The word people reach for is "molecularity" versus "order". Molecularity is how many molecules take part in one microscopic event, and it is a claim about mechanism. Order is the exponent you measure, and it is a claim about data. They coincide exactly when the step is elementary. Everywhere else they can differ, and often do.

Four traps

  1. Writing mass action under a squiggly arrow. "Transcription is G ⤳ G + M, so the rate is k[G]." That happens to be nearly right, but not because of mass action: it is right because RNA polymerase is in excess and initiation is rate-limiting, and both of those are facts you would have to check. Change the growth condition and the rate law changes.
  2. Assuming a reversible arrow means detailed balance. Drawing A ⇄ B asserts that both directions happen. It does not assert that the system reaches equilibrium, because the cell may be driving one direction with ATP. Lecture 7 is about exactly this.
  3. Reading Michaelis-Menten as elementary. v = VmaxS/(KM+S) is a derived rate law for a composite step, valid under conditions you can check and that sometimes fail. Lecture 6 derives it and says when.
  4. Forgetting the hidden reactant. Writing H⁺in ⤳ H⁺out for a proton pump looks like it moves protons uphill for free. The ATP is inside the squiggle. If a reaction as written seems to violate thermodynamics, look for what has been hidden.

15Writing down a network

Three worked examples, from a real paper each, done at increasing difficulty.

This is the exercise that transfers. Take a described biological mechanism and write it as a set of reactions with the arrows correct.

Example A: gene expression, the honest version

The usual two-line model is composite throughout:

G ⤳ G + M,   M ⤳ M + P,   M ⤳ ∅,   P ⤳ ∅ (18)

Every arrow hides something. The first hides polymerase binding, open-complex formation, escape and elongation. The second hides ribosome loading and 300 elongation steps. The last two hide nucleases, proteases, and dilution by growth. That is fine, and it is why the model works: the accounting is exact even when the mechanism is not written. What you may not do is claim that the first arrow's rate is k[G][\mathrm{RNAP}] without deriving it.

Example B: an enzyme, elementary

E + S ⇄kon, koff C,    C →kcat E + P (19)

Here every arrow is elementary and every rate law is forced: kon[E][S], koff[C], kcat[C]. Strictly the third is composite too, since catalysis is a sequence of atomic rearrangements, but it involves one molecular species so it is first order regardless. That is the useful exception: a composite step whose reactant is a single species is still first order, because there is nothing for the concentration dependence to be other than linear.

Example C: a toggle switch, from the paper

Gardner, Cantor and Collins built two repressors, each blocking the other's promoter. Written honestly:

P1 + 2R2 ⇄ P1R2₂  (elementary, binding)
P1 ⤳ P1 + R1  (composite, expression from the free promoter)
R1 ⤳ ∅  (composite, degradation and dilution)(20)

plus the same three with the indices swapped. The binding reactions are elementary and fast. The expression and degradation reactions are composite and slow. That split is the whole architecture of the course, and you have just written it down without being told to. Section 22 says why it is always there.

16Checkpoint 3

  1. Classify each arrow: (a) 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O as written for a burning gas; (b) glucose ⤳ 2 lactate; (c) Ca²⁺out → Ca²⁺in through an open channel; (d) DNA + polymerase ⇄ complex.
  2. For (a) above, mass action would give a rate k[H₂]²[O₂], requiring three molecules to meet at one point. Estimate how much rarer a three-body collision is than a two-body one in a gas, and say what that implies about whether (a) is elementary.
  3. A paper writes S ⤳ P and then fits v = k[S]. Under what conditions is that fit legitimate? Name two situations in which it would fail badly.
  4. Write the reaction network for a phosphorylation cycle: a kinase phosphorylates a substrate, a phosphatase removes the phosphate, and both are enzymes with their own binding steps. How many species and how many reactions? Which arrows are elementary?
Part 4

Bounding

The anchor derivation, the unit conversion that trips everyone, and a tool you can keep.

17The absorbing sphere, every step

Section 13 said a rate constant is a swept volume per unit time. Here is that volume, computed.

The set-up. An enzyme sits at the origin. Treat it as a sphere of radius a that consumes any substrate molecule touching it, instantly. Far away, substrate sits at concentration C∞. Let everything settle into a steady state. How many molecules arrive per second?

Step 1. Steady state means the flux through every shell is equal. Draw a sphere of radius R around the enzyme. In steady state nothing accumulates in the shell between R and R + dR, so whatever crosses in must cross out. Writing j(R) for the flux per unit area,

4πR² j(R) = constant ≠ 0  ⇒  j ∝ 1/R² (21)

Step 2. Write Fick's law and integrate. Fick's first law says flux is diffusion coefficient times gradient, j = D\,dC/dR. Substituting into (21) gives R²\,dC/dR = A for some constant A, so

dC/dR = A/R²  ⇒  C(R) = −A/R + B (22)

Step 3. Two boundary conditions, two constants. The surface is a perfect sink, so C(a) = 0. Far away nothing has changed, so C(∞) = C∞. The second gives B = C∞ immediately. The first then gives 0 = −A/a + C∞, so A = aC∞, and

C(R) = C∞(1 − a/R) (23)

Step 4. Differentiate at the surface and multiply by the area. From (23), dC/dR = aC∞/R², which at R = a is C∞/a. So the flux at the surface is j = DC∞/a, and the total arrival rate is that flux times the surface area:

J = DC∞⁄a × 4πa² = 4πDa C∞  ≡  konC∞ (24)
Sanity checks worth doing on any derivation
  • Does the answer depend on a the way it should? Bigger target, faster capture. Yes, linearly.
  • Does it depend on D the way it should? Faster diffusion, faster capture. Yes, linearly.
  • Why not a²? Because of the depletion well, as the caption says. This is the part people find surprising and it is the part the derivation is for.
  • What if both molecules move? Replace D by DA+DB and a by aA+aB. The relative coordinate does the diffusing, and contact happens when the separation equals the sum of the radii.

18Units, done painfully

Equation (24) gives a volume per time. Turning that into M−1s−1 is where most people make their only error.

So kon is a volume per time, exactly as section 13 predicted. To express it per mole rather than per molecule, and per litre rather than per cubic metre, you need two factors and only two:

kon[M−1s−1] = kon[m³s−1] × 10³ L⁄m³ × NA (25)
If your conversion needs more than these two factors, it is wrong

Work it through once. DA+DB = 770 µm²/s = 7.7×10−10 m²/s. aA+aB = 2.4 nm = 2.4×10−9 m. Then

4π × 7.7×10−10 × 2.4×10−9 = 2.32×10−17 m³/s

× 10³ = 2.32×10−14 L/s,   × 6.02×1023 = 1.4×1010 M−1s−1

That is the number. Each enzyme sweeps out about 2×10−17 cubic metres per second, which is 20 femtolitres, which is about twenty times its own cell's volume. Every second.

19The on-rate calculator

A tool, not an illustration. Use it on your own system.

Everything needed to evaluate 4π(DA+DB)(aA+aB) is now in hand: molecular weights give radii through equation (8), radii give diffusion coefficients through equation (13), and equation (25) does the conversion. So the bound for any pair is two molecular weights away.

Tool 2 · the diffusion limit for any pair

Give it two molecular weights. It computes radii, diffusion coefficients and the Smoluchowski bound, and places the answer against measured rate constants. Try 180 Da and 33 kDa for a metabolite meeting an enzyme, or 33 kDa and 33 kDa for two proteins.

Two ways to beat the bound, and one way to appear to

The bound assumes an unguided three-dimensional search. Break either assumption and you can go faster.

  1. Electrostatic steering. Put opposite charges on the two partners and the substrate falls in rather than wandering in. Barnase associates with barstar at 5×109 M−1s−1 despite needing a precise alignment.8 The catch, usually omitted: at physiological salt the charge is screened and these rates fall to about 107.9
  2. Reduce the dimension of the search. A protein that binds DNA anywhere and then slides along it is searching a line, not a volume. The evidence is unambiguous: EcoRI methyltransferase reaches 5.1×107 M−1s−1 on a 14 bp substrate and four times that on a 429 bp substrate carrying the same site.8 Adding irrelevant DNA makes the target easier to find, which is impossible in three dimensions.
  3. Measure the wrong thing. The lac repressor's famous 1010 M−1s−1 is real, but it has been generalised into a claim about DNA-binding proteins that the data do not support: most protein-DNA associations sit within a factor of three of 108, which is the ordinary limit for a small target on a large molecule.9

The payoff

Two derivations that arrive at the same architecture from opposite directions.

22The separation, and its licence

Put the ceiling and the floor into one expression. What comes out is the reason this course exists.

The binding step is a reversible elementary reaction. Perturb it and the perturbation decays exponentially, at a rate that is the sum of the forward and backward pseudo-first-order rates. Derive that once and you have it forever.

Write C for the complex and linearise about equilibrium. The gain is kon[E][S] and the loss is koff[C], so

dC/dt = kon(ET−C)(ST−C) − koffC,    τbind = 1⁄kon([E]+[S]) + koff (26)

Now put in the medians from parts 1 and 4, and nothing else. Enzyme at 1 µM. Substrate at 1 mM. KD = 100 µM from the median KM, so koff = konKD. And kon = 108 M−1s−1, which is already two decades below the ceiling you computed in section 18:

τbind = 1⁄108×1.001×10−3 + 104 = 9 µs,    τcat = 1/kcat = 100 ms (27)
The number the whole course rests on

Binding reaches equilibrium about 104 times during one catalytic turnover. So when a model writes the bound fraction of an enzyme as an equilibrium expression while writing the flux as a differential equation, it is not making a convenient approximation. It is quoting a measured four-decade ratio. This licenses every quasi-steady-state reduction you will meet in lecture 6, and it licenses lecture 7's use of equilibrium statistical mechanics on a cell that is nowhere near equilibrium.

Two honest caveats. The separation is not universal: a transcription factor at 10 nM binding an operator with KD = 1 nM relaxes in tens of milliseconds, comparable to a turnover, which is why lecture 6's telegraph model exists. And Bennett et al. found that in lower glycolysis, metabolite concentrations sit near KM and every reaction is near equilibrium, so the cell gives up the separation where it needs flux to run both ways.3

23Metabolism cannot wait

The same conclusion again, from timescales alone, with no rate constants at all. Follow this one with a pen.

The question. Can a cell control its metabolism by changing how much enzyme it has?

Step 1. How long does a metabolite pool last? Turnover time is pool divided by flux. E. coli on glucose takes up about 8 mmol per gram dry weight per hour. From section 5, one gram dry weight occupies 1/(1.1 × 0.30) = 3.0 mL of cell. So

J = 8 mmol gDW−1h−1⁄3.0 mL gDW−1 = 2.6 mol⁄L h = 0.73 mM/s (28)

Step 2. Divide the measured pools by it. Remember that glycolysis carries two triose units per glucose, so anything below the aldolase step sees twice the flux.3

Step 3. How fast can expression change an enzyme level? This is a derivation, not a lookup, and it is the most under-appreciated fact in gene regulation. Write the balance for a protein that is not actively degraded, in a cell growing at rate μ. The only loss is dilution as the cell doubles:

dP/dt = β − μP (29)

This relaxes with time constant 1/μ, so the half-time is ln2/μ. But μ = ln2/Td by the definition of a doubling time. Therefore

response half-time = ln2⁄μ = Td, the doubling time itself (30)
Read equation (30) twice

A cell cannot change the concentration of a stable protein faster than it can grow. Not "slowly". Not "in about a generation". Exactly one doubling time, and no amount of transcription helps, because the problem is the removal and not the production. For an E. coli at 40 minutes, halving an enzyme level takes 40 minutes. Even the fastest imaginable route is bounded: 80% of E. coli mRNA half-lives are between 3 and 8 minutes,11 so nothing transcriptional acts in under a few minutes.

Step 4. Compare. Expression: 2400 seconds. The PEP pool: 0.12 seconds. The ratio is 2×104. Even the slowest pool in Table 5 is a hundred times too fast for expression to track.

The conclusion, and it is forced

A cell that regulated metabolism only through expression would empty or overflow its pools thousands of times before the first new enzyme arrived. So a faster regulatory layer is not a design choice, it is a requirement. Look at the timescale ladder in figure 8 and there is exactly one candidate that is fast enough: binding, at 9 µs. That layer is allostery and covalent modification, and this argument predicts them from two numbers.

And they were found, nine years before anyone made the argument

In 1956 Umbarger showed that isoleucine inhibits the first enzyme of its own biosynthetic pathway, and Yates and Pardee showed the same for pyrimidines.12 Neither product resembles the substrate of the enzyme it shuts down, so the inhibition could not be competition for the active site: there had to be a second site. Monod, Wyman and Changeux named it allostery and gave it a two-state equilibrium model in 1965.13 That the model contains no rate constants at all is not a simplification. It is equation (27).

24The habit, and where it goes

Five steps you can apply to a system nobody has told you about, and the map of the rest of the term.

  1. Count. Volume, copy number, concentration. Put every species on the ladder in Table 1. This takes ten minutes and it changes what questions are worth asking.
  2. Time it. Mixing time from τ = x²/D. Turnover time from pool over flux. Response time from equation (30). Put them on one logarithmic axis, as in figure 8. Gaps in that axis are where the architecture is.
  3. Bound it. Compute 4πDa for the pairs that matter. Anything claimed above it is wrong or is a mechanism.
  4. Check the arrows. For each reaction, elementary or composite? If composite, what would have to be derived or measured?
  5. Look for the gap. Where two timescales are far apart, one of them is a control layer and the other is the process being controlled. That is the finding, and it is usually the paper.

25How this was made

Read this one if you are teaching a lecture this term, because it is the standard you are being held to.

This document is the worked example of an exposition. Here is exactly how it was built, so that you can do the same.

  1. Nothing is quoted from memory. Every number here traces either to a paper in the reference list whose full text was obtained and read, or to analysis/cell_ledger.py in the lecture's research folder, which runs with no arguments and prints all of them. If you cannot say where a number came from, do not use it.
  2. Corrections are the product. Four standard pieces of retelling did not survive contact with the sources, and all four are stated in the text where they are used rather than hidden: Brown was watching organelles and not pollen. The familiar protein count is an upper bound that assumes the cell is dry. The diffusion coefficient inside a cell is ten times smaller than in water. And the lac repressor's speed has been generalised beyond what the measurements support. Finding one of these in your own topic is worth more than any amount of polish.
  3. Figures are computed, not drawn. Every figure with an axis was generated by analysis/make_svg.py from the same numbers, so a figure cannot disagree with the text. Then each one was opened in a browser and measured programmatically for labels outside the frame, labels overlapping each other, and curves running through labels. Reading the source is not enough.
  4. Derivations are carried, not cited. Sections 9, 13, 17 and 23 do the algebra in full. A derivation you have followed is a tool. A derivation you have been shown the conclusion of is a fact you will misremember.
  5. The reader is given something to do. Four checkpoints with solutions, and two tools that work on inputs the reader chooses. An exposition that can only be read is a worse exposition.
  6. Say what is still an argument. Section 20 gives the evolutionary reading of the enzyme-speed gap and section 22 gives the structural one, and the text says both fit the data. Presenting a live disagreement as settled is the most common failure of a student exposition.

Not the writing. Getting the full texts, and then finding that four of them said something different from what the lecture had been repeating. That is the part that cannot be shortcut, and it is the part that separates this from something an agent produces in one pass from its own memory.

BSolutions

Worked, not just answered. If your number differs by less than a factor of two, you are right.

Checkpoint 1

1. Twelve copies is 12 × 1.7 = 20 nM. With Kd = 5 nM, the fractional occupancy of a single site is c/(Kd+c) = 20/25 = 0.8, so mostly occupied, but not saturated. That is the interesting regime: a twofold change in copy number still moves occupancy. Had the concentration been 200 nM the site would be locked on and the regulator useless.

2. The count scales with volume, because the composition does not change much: 3.5×106 per fL × 2000 = 7×109 proteins. Milo's survey gives 2 to 3 billion for mammalian cells of that size, so this is right to the factor of two the method promises.2

3. A 300-residue protein needs 900 nucleotides of coding sequence, plus untranslated regions, so call it 1000 nt. Mass: 1000 × 330 = 3.3×105 Da against 33 kDa for the protein. The mRNA is ten times heavier than the protein it encodes. In volume the gap is larger still, because mRNA is an extended, hydrated, highly charged chain rather than a compact globule. The blueprint is bigger than the machine, which is the opposite of every macroscopic analogy, and it is why cells do not keep many copies of an mRNA around.

4. Rmin = 0.066 × (2.5×106)1/3 = 0.066 × 136 = 9.0 nm, so a diameter of 18 nm against a measured 21. The estimate is low because a ribosome is not a smooth compact sphere: it has a large RNA component with a different specific volume, deep clefts, and a tunnel through it. Equation (8) is a minimum, and here it is 15% low, which is about as well as it ever does on a non-protein assembly.

Checkpoint 2

5. τ = (1 µm)²/200 µm²s−1 = 5 ms for a metabolite. One ATP synthase turnover is 10 ms. So the mitochondrion mixes in about half a turnover: well mixed, but only just, and this is exactly the regime where you should be suspicious of the assumption rather than confident in it. For proteins, at 0.13 s, it is not well mixed on the turnover timescale at all, which is one reason respiratory complexes are organised into supercomplexes rather than left to find each other.

6. Membrane to nucleus is about 10 µm, so τ = 100/7.7 = 13 s. Not instant, and not negligible against signalling responses that happen in tens of seconds. To distinguish diffusion from active transport, check the scaling: diffusive arrival time goes as x², motor-driven transport goes as x. Measure arrival time in cells of two different sizes and the exponent tells you which.

7. A centimetre is 104 µm, so τ = 108/7.7 = 1.3×107 s, which is five months, for a protein to cross once. A real cell of that size is the squid giant axon, and it does not rely on diffusion: it runs kinesin and dynein along microtubules at about a micron per second, which crosses a centimetre in three hours. Note the scaling difference again: motors are linear in distance, diffusion is quadratic, so motors win at exactly the point where the two curves cross, around 100 µm.

8. They are wrong. D ∝ 1/a and a ∝ M1/3, so D ∝ M−1/3. Doubling the mass slows diffusion by 21/3 = 1.26, only 26%, not 50%. This is why size differences matter much less for diffusion than people expect, and it is worth remembering when someone proposes that a fusion tag will "obviously" ruin the kinetics.

Checkpoint 3

9. (a) Composite. It is written as a balanced equation, not a mechanism, and combustion proceeds through a radical chain. (b) Composite, obviously: glycolysis is ten steps. (c) Elementary as written, since one molecule crosses in one event, though the rate constant hides the physics of the pore. (d) Elementary, and this is the one case in the list where writing a mass-action rate law is legitimate.

10. Two-body collisions require two molecules in the same small volume, which has probability proportional to c². Three-body requires three, so c³. The ratio is c times the interaction volume, which for a gas at atmospheric pressure and molecular sizes is around 10−3. So three-body events are about a thousand times rarer, and any mechanism that needs them is a thousand times slower than one that does not. Real combustion therefore proceeds by chains of two-body steps, and (a) cannot be elementary.

11. The fit is legitimate when the composite step is limited by a single first-order process in S, which happens if an enzyme is far from saturation ([S] ≪ KM, giving v ≈ (kcat/KM)[E][S]) or if transport is rate-limiting. It fails badly when the enzyme is saturated, since then v is independent of [S] entirely, and when the pathway has a bottleneck elsewhere, since then v is set by a different species and the correlation with [S] is spurious.

12. Six species (S, SP, K, P, KS, PSP) and six reactions: two binding pairs (four elementary arrows counting each direction) plus two catalytic steps. The binding arrows are elementary. The catalytic arrows are composite but first order, by the argument in section 15 example B. This network is lecture 8's central example and it has a property you would not guess: with both enzymes saturated it can be far more switch-like than any single binding curve, which is Goldbeter and Koshland's zero-order ultrasensitivity.

Checkpoint 4

13. A proton at radius 0.1 nm has D = 0.245/0.0001 = 2450 µm²/s by equation (14), and a 50 kDa enzyme has R = 0.066 × 500001/3 = 2.4 nm and D = 100 µm²/s. So kon = 4π(2550×10−12)(2.5×10−9) × 10³ × NA = 4.8×1010 M−1s−1. Eigen's measured proton-transfer rates of 1010 to 1011 straddle it. The conclusion is that proton transfer runs essentially at the diffusion limit, and that the real proton diffusion coefficient in water is higher than Stokes-Einstein gives because protons hop along hydrogen bonds rather than moving as particles. When an estimate is beaten, look for the mechanism.

14. A 300 Da substrate has R = 0.066 × 3001/3 = 0.44 nm and D = 550 µm²/s. With a 33 kDa enzyme, kon = 4π(650×10−12)(2.5×10−9)×10³×NA = 1.2×1010. The measured 3×108 is 2.5% of that. So: fast, in the top few percent of all enzymes, but not diffusion-limited. About one encounter in forty is productive, which is what an orientational requirement looks like.

15. One encounter per turnover means konC = kcat, so C = 10/1.4×1010 = 7×10−10 M, which is 0.7 nM, or under one molecule per cell. Every concentration in Table 1 is far above that. So substrate supply is essentially never the limitation on a metabolic enzyme in a cell. The limitation is always catalysis, which is the premise of everything in part 5.

16. In order: (i) the reactants are strongly and oppositely charged and the buffer is low-salt, so the rate is electrostatically steered and will fall by two orders of magnitude at physiological ionic strength; (ii) the reported constant is not an association rate but was inferred from Kd and an off-rate assuming a single-step mechanism, which is invalid if binding is two-step; (iii) an error. Halford and Marko discuss both of the first two.9
