Follow the pool before comparing the output

A response depends on the controlled inventory, its free and bound allocation, the removal rule, and the measured output.

CCBS · Lecture 8 audience extension
Editorial synthesis of seven Week 4 student contributions · prepared with Codex assistance · 19 October 2026

A binding constant does not specify a complete input-output experiment. The core separates reversible allocation from catalytic conversion. The audience papers show how that distinction changes predictions when the ligand pool is finite, the bound fraction is large, downstream sites store a signal, or a measurement counts accumulated products.

The common task is to keep track of what moves and what is counted. We begin with a total concentration and reconstruct its free component. We then let the pool change through production and removal. Finally, we compare outputs formed from the resulting trajectories. This progression connects Lecture 8's allocation problem to Lecture 9's closed circuits.

Names link to accepted, commit-pinned submissions. New derivations and regenerated figures are editorial synthesis. All numerical examples are illustrative model choices, except the explicitly sourced physical half-life of lutetium-177.

Part I

Identify the pool that the experiment changes

Free concentration determines binding. A controlled total determines free concentration only after conservation is enforced.

1Reconstruct free regulator before predicting promoter occupancy

A finite ligand pool cannot be substituted directly into a reservoir binding curve. Yibing Li makes this the input question. Runzi Yan asks how a binding partner can place a sharp response near a stoichiometric threshold. Both questions are answered by the same conservation relation.

Let free regulator RR bind a free sequestration partner TT in a one-to-one complex CRTC_{RT}. Define totals qR=R+CRTq_R=R+C_{RT} and qT=T+CRTq_T=T+C_{RT}. At binding equilibrium, RT=Kd,TCRTRT=K_{d,T}C_{RT}, where Kd,T>0K_{d,T}>0. Solving that constraint with the totals gives the unique physical complex concentration:

CRT=2qRqTqR+qT+Kd,T+(qR−qT)2+2Kd,T(qR+qT)+Kd,T2,R=qR−CRT.C_{RT}=\frac{2q_Rq_T}{q_R+q_T+K_{d,T}+ \sqrt{(q_R-q_T)^2+2K_{d,T}(q_R+q_T)+K_{d,T}^2}},\qquad R=q_R-C_{RT}.

The rationalized expression avoids subtracting two nearly equal large numbers. It also keeps the physical constraint visible: 0≤CRT≤min⁡(qR,qT)0\leq C_{RT}\leq\min(q_R,q_T). When binding is tight, most regulator is bound below the partner capacity. Above that capacity, free regulator rises approximately with the excess qR−qTq_R-q_T. At finite affinity, the transition remains continuous.

The matching point exposes the scale that rounds the threshold. If qR=qT=Qq_R=q_T=Q, then R=TR=T and

R=Kd,T2+4Kd,TQ−Kd,T2≃Kd,TQ(Kd,T≪Q).R=\frac{\sqrt{K_{d,T}^2+4K_{d,T}Q}-K_{d,T}}2 \simeq\sqrt{K_{d,T}Q}\quad(K_{d,T}\ll Q).

Free regulator is therefore positive at the matching point. For Q=100 nMQ=100\,\mathrm{nM} and Kd,T=0.001 nMK_{d,T}=0.001\,\mathrm{nM}, it is 0.3157 nM. Neither zero free regulator nor a universal transition width follows from tight binding alone.

A promoter introduces a second concentration scale. Assume the promoter pool is too small to deplete the regulator appreciably, and let its occupancy be θG=R/(Kd,G+R)\theta_G=R/(K_{d,G}+R). Since half occupancy requires R=Kd,GR=K_{d,G}, the total regulator required is

qR,1/2=Kd,G+qTKd,GKd,T+Kd,G.q_{R,1/2}=K_{d,G}+\frac{q_TK_{d,G}}{K_{d,T}+K_{d,G}}.

With the preceding sequestration parameters and Kd,G=1 nMK_{d,G}=1\,\mathrm{nM}, half occupancy occurs at 100.9001 nM total regulator. The 10% to 90% occupancy interval spans 9.77 nM in this example. The partner moves the threshold, while both binding scales help set its width. If promoter depletion matters, its bound regulator must also be included in qRq_R.

Protein sequestration supplies a concrete biological implementation of this idea. Buchler and Cross used a dominant-negative inhibitor of a transcriptional activator in budding yeast. Changing inhibitor abundance changed the response threshold and sharpness. Their system motivates the mechanism. The numerical concentrations above are an editorial illustration, not a fit to that experiment.1

Translate the controlled total into the free species before applying a promoter law. Distinguish the capacity that positions a threshold from the affinities that round it.

2Retain the bound substrate pool when reducing catalytic dynamics

A rapidly adjusting complex can still contain a substantial fraction of the substrate. Siheng Wang tests a reduction that uses total unconverted substrate rather than free substrate as the slow variable. This is the central change of variables in the total quasi-steady-state approximation developed by Borghans, De Boer and Segel.2

Consider free enzyme EE, free substrate SS, complex CESC_{ES} and product PP. Binding has association constant k+k_+ and dissociation constant k−k_-. Product formation from the complex has rate constant kcatk_\mathrm{cat}. The enzyme total qE=E+CESq_E=E+C_{ES} stays fixed. The unconverted substrate total qS=S+CESq_S=S+C_{ES} changes only through catalysis:

q˙S=−kcatCES,C˙ES=k+(qE−CES)(qS−CES)−(k−+kcat)CES,P˙=kcatCES.\dot q_S=-k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES},\qquad \dot C_{ES}=k_+(q_E-C_{ES})(q_S-C_{ES})-(k_-+k_\mathrm{cat})C_{ES}, \qquad \dot P=k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES}.

Setting the complex derivative to zero produces the same quadratic form as finite binding, but with KM=(k−+kcat)/k+K_M=(k_-+k_\mathrm{cat})/k_+. It does not generally use the equilibrium constant Kd=k−/k+K_d=k_-/k_+. Catalytic departure contributes to complex loss. Let CESqss(qS)C_{ES}^{\mathrm{qss}}(q_S) denote the physical quadratic root. The reduced equation is q˙S=−kcatCESqss(qS)\dot q_S=-k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES}^{\mathrm{qss}}(q_S).

The comparison must use the same preparation and product readout. Set qE=qS(0)=1 μMq_E=q_S(0)=1\,\mu\mathrm M, CES(0)=P(0)=0C_{ES}(0)=P(0)=0, k+=10 μM−1s−1k_+=10\,\mu\mathrm M^{-1}\mathrm s^{-1}, k−=9.9 s−1k_-=9.9\,\mathrm s^{-1} and kcat=0.1 s−1k_\mathrm{cat}=0.1\,\mathrm s^{-1}. The standard substrate-depletion approximation instead evolves a variable SstdS_\mathrm{std} from 1 micromolar according to S˙std=−kcatqESstd/(KM+Sstd)\dot S_\mathrm{std}=-k_\mathrm{cat}q_ES_\mathrm{std}/(K_M+S_\mathrm{std}), treating the missing substrate as product.

The total quasi-steady-state approximation is accurate for the slow product curve in this example. Its largest product error over 60 seconds is about 0.16% of initial substrate, compared with 16.11% for the standard substrate-depletion approximation. This does not mean the initial complex trajectory is accurate. The full preparation starts with no complex, while the reduced closure immediately assigns a positive complex concentration. A fast initial layer is omitted.

Choose a slow variable that binding cannot change, then validate the particular output. Good product agreement does not certify the unresolved initial free and bound trajectories.

Part II

Close the dynamics around the binding allocation

A binding relation tells us where molecules are. Production and removal determine how that allocation evolves.

3Count downstream storage before predicting signal speed

A downstream binding pool can change a signal's timing while leaving its free steady level unchanged. Leo Sin considers a regulator that binds nonproductive DNA sites. Del Vecchio, Ninfa and Sontag analyze this kind of loading as retroactivity: a downstream connection changes the upstream system that drives it.3

The removal rule is essential to this example. Let XX be free regulator, let qDq_D be the fixed total of binding sites, and assume fast binding with dissociation constant KdK_d. Regulator is produced at rate α(t)\alpha(t). Only free regulator is removed, at rate γX\gamma X. Bound regulator is protected. Then

CDX=qDXKd+X,qX=X+CDX,q˙X=α(t)−γX.C_{DX}=\frac{q_DX}{K_d+X},\qquad q_X=X+C_{DX},\qquad \dot q_X=\alpha(t)-\gamma X.

Differentiate the total-to-free relation to find how much production is needed to change free concentration. The result is

(1+qDKd(Kd+X)2)X˙=α(t)−γX.\left(1+\frac{q_DK_d}{(K_d+X)^2}\right)\dot X=\alpha(t)-\gamma X.

The factor multiplying X˙\dot X is a dimensionless storage capacity, dqX/dXdq_X/dX. A change in free concentration also fills or empties bound sites. Under constant production, the free steady level remains X∗=α/γX^*=\alpha/\gamma. Linearization about that level gives a relaxation time

trelax=1γ(1+qDKd(Kd+X∗)2).t_\mathrm{relax}=\frac1\gamma\left(1+\frac{q_DK_d}{(K_d+X^*)^2}\right).

Removing bound regulator changes the steady-state conclusion. If both free and bound regulator are removed at the same per-capita rate, with occupied sites released, then q˙X=α−γqX\dot q_X=\alpha-\gamma q_X. Its steady total is fixed, and the free steady level decreases when more sites are added. “Binding changes only the clock” is therefore a result of the declared free-only removal model.

Include the derivative of the total-to-free relation when computing a response speed. State which molecular forms are removed before claiming that the steady output is preserved.

4Distinguish a unique binding allocation from multiple feedback equilibria

The same binding law can support different closed-system behaviors. Álvaro Bátrez compares a sequestration-based negative feedback with a positive feedback. The distinction comes from the production law surrounding the allocation. It does not require several physical solutions to the binding constraint.

In the negative feedback, a fixed activator total qAq_A binds a variable inhibitor total qEq_E. Free activator AA promotes inhibitor production. Binding is fast, with dissociation constant KdK_d. Production adds free inhibitor, and removal acts on both free and bound inhibitor at rate constant γ\gamma, releasing any bound activator. Thus

q˙E=αAKa+A−γqE,qA=A+CAE,qE=E+CAE,AE=KdCAE.\dot q_E=\alpha\frac{A}{K_a+A}-\gamma q_E, \qquad q_A=A+C_{AE},\qquad q_E=E+C_{AE},\qquad AE=K_dC_{AE}.

Adding inhibitor reduces free activator and therefore its own production. To calculate the local attenuation, differentiate binding while holding qAq_A fixed. Since dCAE=−dAdC_{AE}=-dA and dE=dqE+dAdE=dq_E+dA, differentiating AE=KdCAEAE=K_dC_{AE} gives

∂A∂qE=−AKd+A+E,L=αγKa(Ka+A)2AKd+A+E,dlog⁡qE∗dlog⁡α=11+L.\frac{\partial A}{\partial q_E}=-\frac{A}{K_d+A+E},\qquad L=\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\frac{K_a}{(K_a+A)^2}\frac{A}{K_d+A+E},\qquad \frac{d\log q_E^*}{d\log\alpha}=\frac1{1+L}.

These derivatives are evaluated at equilibrium. The gain LL is dimensionless and positive. The production term decreases with qEq_E, while removal increases, so the scalar model has one stable equilibrium for positive qA,α,γq_A,\alpha,\gamma. This is a statement about the specified fast-binding closure.

Positive feedback changes the balance equation. Now a fixed sequestration pool qSq_S binds a regulator with total qEq_E. Free regulator EE increases its own production:

q˙E=α+βEKa+E−γqE,qE=E+CES,qS=S+CES,ES=KdCES.\dot q_E=\alpha+\beta\frac{E}{K_a+E}-\gamma q_E, \qquad q_E=E+C_{ES},\qquad q_S=S+C_{ES},\qquad ES=K_dC_{ES}.

Use the same removal convention, so removal of bound regulator releases the sequestration partner. Each total qEq_E still gives one physical free concentration EE. The production-minus-removal curve can nevertheless cross zero three times. The first and third roots are stable if the curve slopes downward there. The middle root is unstable if the slope is positive.

The negative and positive closures ask different experimental questions. The first asks how much a production perturbation changes the settled total. The second asks whether distinct initial totals approach different settled states under the same final conditions. A sharp binding curve alone answers neither question. The relevant production and removal balances must be closed around it.

Locate multistability in the closed dynamical balance. Do not infer multiple physical binding allocations from multiple equilibria of a feedback circuit.

Part III

Define the output before ranking two mechanisms

Occupied molecules, product flux and accumulated events are different observables. Their rankings need not agree.

5Compare product fluxes using active abundance and turnover

Affinity alone does not determine which enzyme carries most of a metabolic flux. Fei Wang raises this question through tacrolimus metabolism by CYP3A4 and CYP3A5. The official prescribing information identifies both enzymes in its metabolism account. That biological pointer motivates the comparison. The calculation below uses generic illustrative parameters rather than unverified assay values.4

Specify the active enzyme pool and catalytic output for each route. For two enzymes sharing a prescribed free substrate concentration SS, a simple quasi-steady rate law gives

vi=kcat,iqEiactiveSKM,i+S=ViSKM,i+S,ϕ2=v2v1+v2.v_i=\frac{k_{\mathrm{cat},i}q_{E_i}^{\mathrm{active}}S}{K_{M,i}+S} =\frac{V_iS}{K_{M,i}+S},\qquad \phi_2=\frac{v_2}{v_1+v_2}.

The quantity Vi=kcat,iqEiactiveV_i=k_{\mathrm{cat},i}q_{E_i}^{\mathrm{active}} is an active catalytic capacity, not an affinity. In the low-substrate regime, flux is proportional to Vi/KM,iV_i/K_{M,i}. At saturating substrate, it approaches ViV_i. Two enzymes can therefore exchange their flux ranking as substrate changes.

The input and output assumptions determine what this fraction means. Both routes see the same free substrate, their active enzyme totals are specified, and the chosen products count the same substrate-conversion event. A change in enzyme abundance, inhibition, transport or substrate availability changes the comparison. Converting a measured tissue concentration into a patient clearance prediction requires a larger model and additional measurements.

Compare fluxes using active enzyme abundance, turnover and the actual substrate regime. A binding preference or isolated turnover constant does not by itself determine a route's contribution.

6Integrate local events over residence time rather than ranking peak stock

A larger peak molecular stock can produce fewer accumulated local events. Xinyu Wu compares hypothetical radiolabeled candidates with different arrival and retention. The output is the number of radioactive decays occurring within a target compartment. It is not simply the number of radioactive nuclei present at the peak.

Define the arrival and departure model before integrating it. Begin with N0N_0 radioactive nuclei. A fraction ff would arrive at time tdt_d if there were no physical decay. Nuclei decay throughout transit at rate constant λ\lambda. After arrival, the surviving nuclei can either decay locally or leave with biological rate constant kk. There is no re-entry. Thus, for t≥tdt\geq t_d,

Ntarget(t)N0=fe−λtde−(λ+k)(t−td),D˙target(t)N0=λNtarget(t)N0.\frac{N_{\mathrm{target}}(t)}{N_0}=f e^{-\lambda t_d}e^{-(\lambda+k)(t-t_d)}, \qquad \frac{\dot D_{\mathrm{target}}(t)}{N_0}=\lambda\frac{N_{\mathrm{target}}(t)}{N_0}.

Here NtargetN_{\mathrm{target}} counts surviving target nuclei, and DtargetD_{\mathrm{target}} counts local decays accumulated after arrival. Integrating the decay rate gives

Dtarget(∞)N0=fe−λtdλλ+k.\frac{D_{\mathrm{target}}(\infty)}{N_0} =f e^{-\lambda t_d}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+k}.

The first factor is survival-weighted delivery. The last factor is the probability that an arrived nucleus decays before it leaves. This is a competition between two exit hazards, so a longer residence can outweigh a smaller delivered peak.

For an illustration, use the 6.647-day physical half-life of lutetium-177, so λ=log⁡2/(6.647 days)\lambda=\log2/(6.647\,\mathrm{days}).5 Candidate A has f=0.04f=0.04, arrival at 0.25 days and a one-day biological retention half-life. Candidate B has f=0.03f=0.03, arrival at one day and a four-day biological retention half-life. These delivery and retention values are hypothetical. For each candidate, k=log⁡2/t1/2,biok=\log2/t_{1/2,\mathrm{bio}}.

Peak activity has the same ranking as peak surviving nuclei here because both candidates use the same isotope and activity is λNtarget\lambda N_{\mathrm{target}}. Integrated local decays reverse that ranking. Absorbed dose would need further information about energy per decay, spatial deposition, escaping radiation and target mass. Neither peak stock nor the decay count alone supplies those quantities.

The chapter's next experiment follows directly from this distinction. Under one controlled input perturbation, measure free species, bound species and accumulated output in the same preparation. Test whether a single conservation-and-rate model explains all three. If two candidate mechanisms agree on total abundance but disagree on free concentration or integrated output, those are the measurements that can separate them.

Name the event being integrated and the competing ways a molecule can leave. Rank mechanisms by the required output, with its observation window and physical units explicit.
