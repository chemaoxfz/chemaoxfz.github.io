Time-scale separation: fast recovery, slow freedom

A fast complex does not stop moving. It rapidly adjusts to totals that change slowly. Work one enzyme from reaction events to a moving slow manifold, then use the same idea to understand regulation.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Lecture 6 · core page (full) · 2026-09-17 (Thu) 10:40–12:15 · students present, lecturer-backed

In Lecture 2 we estimated how quickly biological reactions happen. Binding, catalysis, gene expression, and growth need not run on the same clock. In Lecture 3 we used that observation repeatedly: a binding intermediate or promoter occupancy adjusted rapidly enough to become a Michaelis–Menten or Hill law. Lectures 4 and 5 then showed how varied deterministic and stochastic behavior can emerge from reaction networks. We now make the separation behind those effective laws precise.

The story

Fast reactions restore relationships among species; slow reactions move the totals those relationships depend on. This can make a system robust to fast occupancy disturbances while leaving its slower biological response free to change. Exact total accounting identifies the slow coordinates. Singular perturbation explains when a moving fast-state closure is justified.

Part I

The question and the right coordinates

Begin with something the cell does, then ask which motion the model must retain.

1Robustness need not mean immobility

A useful biological system can recover after a small disturbance yet change behavior when its input changes. Those demands are compatible if recovery acts in different directions from the intended response. Binding occupancy may relax rapidly, while substrate supply, protein abundance, and regulatory inputs move more slowly. Time-scale separation is one mechanism for this organization, not a proof that every cell is stable under every perturbation.

Make the comparison concrete before reducing anything. Consider an illustrative enzyme with association constant k+=10 μM−1s−1k_+=10\,\mu\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}, dissociation constant k−=10 s−1k_-=10\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}, and catalytic constant kcat=0.5 s−1k_\mathrm{cat}=0.5\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}. The affinity scale is Kd=k−/k+=1 μMK_d=k_-/k_+=1\,\mu\mathrm{M}. Unbinding takes 0.1 s0.1\,\mathrm{s}, while catalysis takes 2 s2\,\mathrm{s}. Association at free substrate 1 μM1\,\mu\mathrm{M} contributes another rate of 10 s−110\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

Estimate, then compare

These are model numbers, not a measured universal enzyme. A second-order association constant needs a concentration before it becomes a rate. Binding often supplies a natural fast subsystem, but scarce ligand, slow unbinding, conformational change, or rapid catalysis can remove that separation. “Fast” is always relative to the process we intend to retain.

We will use the same enzyme throughout. The first task is not to write a small parameter. It is to understand a puzzle in its observed motion.

Name the perturbation a fast subsystem can resist and compare its state-dependent clock with the slower process of interest.

2One enzyme makes the puzzle concrete

In a well-mixed, fixed-volume closed vessel, free enzyme EE binds free substrate SS to form complex CESC_{ES}. The bound substrate is converted to free product PP, returning enzyme. Each displayed step is explicitly resolved as elementary. Neglect of reverse catalytic conversion is an assumption of this simple model.

Mass action gives the complex equation

C˙ES=k+ES−k−CES−kcatCES.\dot C_{ES}=k_+ES-k_-C_{ES}-k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES}.

The usual quasi-steady-state move sets its right-hand side approximately to zero. But start with no complex: binding must first make some. Later, substrate becomes product and the complex must decline toward zero. A derivative that is literally zero for all time cannot describe either stage.

This is the central question: how can an approximately balanced complex still keep changing? Historically, Briggs and Haldane's original argument already allowed the complex to decrease after its initial transient.1 We can make that statement precise by identifying what binding conserves and then choosing the clock.

Explain why quasi-steady state cannot mean a complex concentration that remains constant throughout substrate conversion.

3Totals identify what binding cannot change

Let B=k+ES−k−CESB=k_+ES-k_-C_{ES} denote the net binding flux. All four species balances are

E˙=−B+kcatCES,S˙=−B,C˙ES=B−kcatCES,P˙=kcatCES.\begin{aligned} \dot E&=-B+k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES},& \dot S&=-B,\\ \dot C_{ES}&=B-k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES},& \dot P&=k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES}. \end{aligned}

Binding rapidly changes both free enzyme and free substrate. Neither is automatically a slow coordinate. Add free and bound amounts instead:

qE=E+CES,qS=S+CES,qP=P.q_E=E+C_{ES},\qquad q_S=S+C_{ES},\qquad q_P=P.

Here a qq counts a molecular constituent across its forms. Adding the corresponding species equations cancels binding exactly:

q˙E=0,q˙S=−kcatCES,q˙P=kcatCES.\dot q_E=0,\qquad \dot q_S=-k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES},\qquad \dot q_P=k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES}.

No small parameter has been used. Binding alone preserves these totals at any rate. Catalysis preserves enzyme total but converts substrate total into product total; the closed-system sum qS+qPq_S+q_P is conserved. A “binding invariant” need not be constant under the whole network.

Total coordinates make the separation visible: binding redistributes species within a fixed total; slow chemistry changes the total itself. This is why total-variable reductions can handle appreciable sequestration.2

Derive exact binding-invariant totals and distinguish them from quantities conserved by the entire reaction network.

Part II

Make the separation precise

The same system has two useful limiting descriptions.

4Two clocks expose a singular limit

Scale concentrations by KdK_d and time by the catalytic clock:

u=qSKd,c=CESKd,e=qEKd,p=PKd,τ=kcatt,ε=kcatk−.u=\frac{q_S}{K_d},\quad c=\frac{C_{ES}}{K_d},\quad e=\frac{q_E}{K_d},\quad p=\frac{P}{K_d},\quad \tau=k_\mathrm{cat}t,\quad\varepsilon=\frac{k_\mathrm{cat}}{k_-}.

Enzyme total ee is a constant parameter. Free enzyme and substrate are Kd(e−c)K_d(e-c) and Kd(u−c)K_d(u-c). Substitution into the full equations gives

dudτ=−c,εdcdτ=(e−c)(u−c)−c−εc,dpdτ=c.\frac{du}{d\tau}=-c,\qquad \varepsilon\frac{dc}{d\tau}=(e-c)(u-c)-c-\varepsilon c,\qquad \frac{dp}{d\tau}=c.

Our limit speeds association and dissociation together while keeping their ratio KdK_d fixed, as well as totals and catalytic rate. On the fast clock θ=τ/ε=k−t\theta=\tau/\varepsilon=k_-t,

dudθ=−εc,dcdθ=(e−c)(u−c)−c−εc.\frac{du}{d\theta}=-\varepsilon c,\qquad \frac{dc}{d\theta}=(e-c)(u-c)-c-\varepsilon c.

At zero epsilon, the fast problem keeps uu fixed and relaxes binding. On slow time, the zero-epsilon equations instead give u′=−cu'=-c and the algebraic relation 0=(e−c)(u−c)−c0=(e-c)(u-c)-c. A differential equation has disappeared. This loss of order, and of an independently specifiable initial complex, makes the perturbation singular.

5A slow manifold moves

The fast balance is a quadratic. Its physical root is

c=h0(u)=e+u+1−(e+u+1)2−4eu2.c=h_0(u)= \frac{e+u+1-\sqrt{(e+u+1)^2-4eu}}{2}.

The smaller root lies between zero and both available totals; the other root would require a negative free species. As uu varies, these fast fixed points form the critical manifold. Write g0=(e−c)(u−c)−cg_0=(e-c)(u-c)-c. Its derivative at the physical root is

∂cg0=−D(u),D(u)=e+u+1−2h0(u)>0.\partial_cg_0=-D(u),\qquad D(u)=e+u+1-2h_0(u)>0.

A small transverse displacement decays like e−Dθe^{-D\theta}. This is the same negative-eigenvalue intuition used in Lecture 4, now applied to fast motion at a fixed total. On suitable compact attracting portions, small positive epsilon gives a nearby invariant slow manifold.3

Then slow chemistry carries the system along that family of states. To leading order,

dudτ=−h0(u),dcdτ=h0′(u)dudτ=−h0(u)h0′(u)<0.\frac{du}{d\tau}=-h_0(u),\qquad \frac{dc}{d\tau}=h_0'(u)\frac{du}{d\tau} =-h_0(u)h_0'(u)<0.

On fast time the same derivative is dc/dθ=−εh0h0′dc/d\theta=-\varepsilon h_0h_0', small rather than literally zero. The approximation retains the leading balance of fast binding fluxes while allowing a small imbalance to supply the slow derivative.

The paradox resolved

A complex near its instantaneous fast balance tracks a moving target. The limiting algebraic relation is not an exact finite-speed trajectory with zero derivative. In general, an invariant graph c=hε(u)c=h_\varepsilon(u) must satisfy εhε′(u)(−hε)=g0(u,hε)−εhε\varepsilon h_\varepsilon'(u)(-h_\varepsilon)=g_0(u,h_\varepsilon)-\varepsilon h_\varepsilon, by the chain rule.4

6Three closures carry different hypotheses

The calculation so far makes binding fast relative to catalysis. Standard QSSA can use a different limit: little substrate is sequestered while complex forms. Its kinetic constant is KM=(k−+kcat)/k+K_M=(k_-+k_\mathrm{cat})/k_+, not generally the dissociation constant. A useful initial criterion, when initial complex is negligible, is qE/(KM+S0)≪1q_E/(K_M+S_0)\ll1.5

ReductionFast-state closureWhat must be checked
Standard QSSACES≈qES/(KM+S)C_{ES}\approx q_ES/(K_M+S)Free substrate changes little in the complex-formation layer; use a suitable small-enzyme regime.
Rapid equilibriumCES≈H(qS;qE,Kd)C_{ES}\approx H(q_S;q_E,K_d)Binding relaxation is fast relative to catalysis and boundary motion at fixed affinity.
Total QSSACES≈H(qS;qE,KM)C_{ES}\approx H(q_S;q_E,K_M)Complex follows a rapidly attracting flux-balance graph in total coordinates; total bookkeeping alone is not a validity theorem.

Here H(qS;qE,K)H(q_S;q_E,K) is the physical smaller root of (qE−CES)(qS−CES)=KCES(q_E-C_{ES})(q_S-C_{ES})=KC_{ES}. Rapid equilibrium and total QSSA use the same quadratic with different constants.

Do not remove every transient from the comparison. A closure may predict product well after binding relaxes and still miss early complex formation. Nor does eventual substrate depletion automatically invalidate a total-coordinate rapid-binding description. Validity belongs to a particular limit, region, time window, and observable.

Select a closure with its actual hypotheses instead of treating all Michaelis–Menten formulas as the same approximation.

Part III

Recover biological behavior

Use exact accounting to carry the same argument into open processing and regulation.

7Open the vessel, keep exact accounting

Return to the supplied-and-drained enzyme from Lecture 3. Substrate enters at constant flux vinv_\mathrm{in}, and product leaves at koutPk_\mathrm{out}P. The boundary processes are composite. Volume is fixed, and enzyme has no addition or removal in this model.

The simpler total equations remain exact:

q˙E=0,q˙S=vin−kcatCES,q˙P=kcatCES−koutqP.\dot q_E=0,\qquad \dot q_S=v_\mathrm{in}-k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES},\qquad \dot q_P=k_\mathrm{cat}C_{ES}-k_\mathrm{out}q_P.

They are not yet closed because the right side still needs the complex. Only now introduce an approximation, such as CES≈H(qS;qE,Kd)C_{ES}\approx H(q_S;q_E,K_d). This produces two slow differential equations with a fixed enzyme-total parameter.

Supply and drain must also be slow on the binding clock. In dimensionless form, hold j=vin/(kcatKd)j=v_\mathrm{in}/(k_\mathrm{cat}K_d) and δ=kout/kcat\delta=k_\mathrm{out}/k_\mathrm{cat} fixed as epsilon shrinks. If input itself moves totals appreciably during binding, the same reduction needs re-examination.6 Growth or enzyme turnover would add further terms to the exact total equations; they are not present in this fixed-volume vessel.

Write an open system in total coordinates and mark the precise step where exact accounting becomes approximate closure.

8Fast recovery leaves versatile slow behavior

Fast attraction erases a disturbance in binding occupancy at a fixed total. It does not erase a change in the total or in the supply. A steady processing state needs CES∗=vin/kcatC_{ES}^*=v_\mathrm{in}/k_\mathrm{cat}, while enzyme availability imposes CES≤qEC_{ES}\leq q_E. Thus positive finite steady processing requires input below capacity:

vin<kcatqE.v_\mathrm{in}<k_\mathrm{cat}q_E.

Above capacity, q˙S≥vin−kcatqE>0\dot q_S\geq v_\mathrm{in}-k_\mathrm{cat}q_E>0 is an exact proof of accumulation. No acceleration of binding can fix it. Below capacity, increasing substrate raises the catalytic flux and pulls substrate back down. More formally, the reduced flux v(qS)=kcatH(qS;qE,K)v(q_S)=k_\mathrm{cat}H(q_S;q_E,K) has positive slope; the two slow linearized eigenvalues are −v′(qS∗)-v'(q_S^*) and −kout-k_\mathrm{out}. Near saturation that first recovery rate becomes small.

This is a bounded answer to the opening question. Fast organization can make occupancy robust while slower controls remain effective. The varied behavior studied in Lectures 4 and 5 can still reside in the reduced slow network.

Distinguish fast-state robustness from slow stability, saturation, and the ability to respond to changing input.

9Average a promoter, not its whole phenotype

Lecture 5 left a binary promoter G∈{0,1}G\in\{0,1\} switching on at rate α\alpha and off at β\beta. These are effective state transitions. RNA addition has hazard kGkG, and RNA removal has hazard γNM\gamma N_M. The fast object is now a distribution, with

pon=αα+β,tswitch=1α+β.p_\mathrm{on}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},\qquad t_\mathrm{switch}=\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}.

Speed both switches at fixed on fraction while keeping transcription and removal fixed. Between slow events, average over the promoter's rapid history. The effective RNA addition hazard is kponkp_\mathrm{on}. One promoter remains binary; it has not become a fractional molecule.7

The stationary mean ⟨NM⟩=kpon/γ\langle N_M\rangle=kp_\mathrm{on}/\gamma is actually exact at every switching speed. The variance is more discriminating:

FM=1+kβ(α+β)(γ+α+β).F_M=1+\frac{k\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)(\gamma+\alpha+\beta)}.

This limit is not Lecture 5's large-volume limit. Volume stays fixed while a hidden clock accelerates. With feedback and sequestration, the correct average conditions on the retained total count; inserting a deterministic Hill curve as a stochastic propensity can fail.8

Eliminate a rapidly mixing promoter while checking which count statistics the averaged model preserves.

10Binding becomes a regulatory law

Make promoter switching mechanistic. A repressor RR binds promoter DD through D+R⇌k−k+CDRD+R\xrightleftharpoons[k_-]{k_+}C_{DR}. Only unbound promoter transcribes. This composite production process is D⇝D+MD\rightsquigarrow D+M, with separate rate vtx=ρDv_\mathrm{tx}=\rho D.

Fast binding equilibrium gives CDR/D=R/KdC_{DR}/D=R/K_d. Divide the promoter total qD=D+CDRq_D=D+C_{DR} by DD, solve for its active fraction, and substitute:

DqD=11+R/Kd,vtx=ρqD1+R/Kd.\frac{D}{q_D}=\frac{1}{1+R/K_d},\qquad v_\mathrm{tx}=\frac{\rho q_D}{1+R/K_d}.

If the bound state is active, the active fraction is R/(Kd+R)R/(K_d+R). Both laws describe binding controlling a slower composite transcription process. They use free regulator; if binding appreciably depletes it, solve its total balance as well.

Optional branch · Where exponent two can come from

Two sequential binding steps have weights 1,R/K1,R2/(K1K2)1,R/K_1,R^2/(K_1K_2). If only the twice-bound state is active, its fraction is R2/(K1K2+K2R+R2)R^2/(K_1K_2+K_2R+R^2). When the singly bound weight is negligible near the transition, this approaches a Hill law with exponent two and half-activation scale K1K2\sqrt{K_1K_2}. Fast binding alone does not create cooperativity.

Derive a regulatory law from fast binding states and state the extra assumptions behind a cooperative Hill exponent.

11Close totals, then ask what equilibrium means

The enzyme is the smallest version of a general construction. Let xx collect species, Γbind\Gamma_\mathrm{bind} and Γcat\Gamma_\mathrm{cat} collect reaction-change columns, and b(x)b(x) collect boundary addition-removal. A constituent-counting matrix LL defines totals q=Lxq=Lx and satisfies LΓbind=0L\Gamma_\mathrm{bind}=0. Therefore

q˙=LΓcatvcat(x)+Lb(x).\dot q=L\Gamma_\mathrm{cat}v_\mathrm{cat}(x)+Lb(x).

This identity is exact and still contains fast species. To close it, reconstruct their state at the current totals, for example x≈xeq(q)x\approx x_\mathrm{eq}(q) under justified fast equilibrium. Slow differential equations plus algebraic binding constraints form a differential-algebraic system, or DAE.

A fast steady state can sustain currents in a driven cycle. Fastness alone does not establish equilibrium or compatibility of binding constants around cycles. That is the next question, not an assumption to smuggle into this lecture.

Separate exact total projection from equilibrium closure and identify the missing condition needed for a binding-catalysis DAE.
