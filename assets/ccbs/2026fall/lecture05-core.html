Stochastic dynamics: the master equation and Gillespie

Specify integer jumps and event clocks once. From that one object, derive the probability equation, sample exact paths, and know when the concentration ODE is allowed to replace them.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · lecture 5 · core page (full) · 2026-09-15 (Tue) 10:40–12:15 · students present, lecturer-backed

A deterministic rate equation says how a concentration moves. It does not say when the next molecule is made, whether zero can be reached, or how different two genetically identical cells may look. Today we rebuild a reaction network as a jump process. The payoff is a single specification that generates three distinct objects: a chemical master equation for probabilities, a Gillespie trajectory for events, and an ODE only after a large-system limit. This follows the derivation spine of the 2025 Lecture 5 and 6 notes, with the count conversion, notation, and exactness claim repaired.1

The deliverable

By minute 95, every team should be able to turn a reaction list into state-change vectors and propensities, write the corresponding CME with the zero boundary correct, execute one direct-method step by hand, implement the loop, and test it against the Poisson birth–death model.

Part 1

Turn reactions into probability

Counts make events visible. A reaction list then becomes a probability-flow machine.

1Concentrations hide the first molecule

The decision to use a stochastic model starts with a conversion, not with the word “cell.”

For concentration cc in volume VV, the expected molecule count is

N=cVNA.N=cVN_A. (1)

In a 1 fL1\,\mathrm{fL} volume, 1 μM1\,\mu\mathrm{M} is 602.214602.214 molecules, not one. One molecule in that volume is 1.66054 nM1.66054\,\mathrm{nM}.8 That correction matters because “micromolar” does not by itself imply small-copy noise. A promoter with one genomic copy is discrete by construction. A metabolite with hundreds of thousands of copies may be well represented by concentration. Count the observed species before choosing the mathematics.

Room check, 60 seconds. A transcription factor is present at 5 nM5\,\mathrm{nM} in a 2 fL2\,\mathrm{fL} bacterium. Estimate its mean count. Would a negative Gaussian fluctuation be a harmless approximation?

2A network becomes clocks

For every reaction, write one integer jump and one state-dependent clock.

Let the state be the count vector X(t)∈Z≥0nX(t)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{n}. Reaction rr changes it by the stoichiometric vector νr\nu_r. Its propensity ar(x)a_r(x) is defined so that, conditional on state xx,

Pr⁡{one r event in [t,t+dt)∣X(t)=x}=ar(x) dt+o(dt).\Pr\{\text{one }r\text{ event in }[t,t+dt)\mid X(t)=x\}=a_r(x)\,dt+o(dt). (2)

The propensity has units of events per time. For ∅→X\varnothing\to X at constant production rate bb, use ν+=+1\nu_+=+1 and a+(n)=ba_+(n)=b. For X→∅X\to\varnothing with per-molecule loss rate dd, use ν−=−1\nu_-=-1 and a−(n)=dna_-(n)=dn. The factor nn counts eligible molecules. It also switches death off automatically at zero.

The same counting rule scales. For 2X→Y2X\to Y, the eligible unordered pairs are nX(nX−1)/2n_X(n_X-1)/2, so the propensity is a constant times that count and the jump is (−2,+1)(-2,+1). For X+Y→ZX+Y\to Z, the eligible pairs are nXnYn_Xn_Y. Writing eligibility before a formula prevents two common coding errors: allowing a reaction when a reactant count is zero and forgetting that identical reactants form unordered pairs.

Mass action changes when the state is a count

A bimolecular concentration constant and a count-based propensity constant are not numerically interchangeable. Volume and Avogadro's constant enter the conversion. Write the desired probability per unit time first, then check its units.

3Probability flows across a count ladder

Every CME term is either probability arriving through a jump or probability leaving through a clock.

Let p(x,t)=Pr⁡{X(t)=x}p(x,t)=\Pr\{X(t)=x\}. During a short interval, state xx loses probability through every clock running at xx. It gains probability when reaction rr fires from the predecessor x−νrx-\nu_r. Adding those accounts gives the general chemical master equation:3

∂p(x,t)∂t=∑r[ar(x−νr)p(x−νr,t)−ar(x)p(x,t)].\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}=\sum_r\left[a_r(x-\nu_r)p(x-\nu_r,t)-a_r(x)p(x,t)\right]. (3)

Apply the rule to birth and death. At count nn, birth arrives from n−1n-1, death arrives from n+1n+1, and both clocks remove probability from nn:

p˙n=b pn−1+d(n+1)pn+1−(b+dn)pn.\dot p_n=b\,p_{n-1}+d(n+1)p_{n+1}-(b+dn)p_n. (4)

4Zero closes the equation

The boundary is not bookkeeping trivia. It is part of the model's biology.

Equation (4) appears to ask for p−1p_{-1} when n=0n=0. Negative molecules do not exist, so define p−1=0p_{-1}=0. Death already has propensity d⋅0=0d\cdot0=0. The boundary equation is therefore

p˙0=d p1−b p0.\dot p_0=d\,p_1-b\,p_0. (5)

Now summing equation (4) over all nonnegative counts makes every internal flow cancel, leaving d∑npn/dt=0d\sum_n p_n/dt=0. This is the probability analogue of a conservation law. A model of an autocatalyst may instead have zero as an absorbing state. Then extinction is not a numerical failure. It is a qualitative prediction that a concentration ODE cannot represent.

This gives a quick CME audit. First, each gain term must point to a state that can reach the state being described. Second, each outgoing propensity must appear once with a minus sign. Third, summing over the admissible state space must conserve total probability unless probability is deliberately sent to a named absorbing or external state. These checks catch an index error before a simulator hides it inside a plausible-looking trace.

Part 2

Make analysis and simulation agree

A solvable process lets us audit the equation, the code, and the interpretation against one another.

5One solvable model calibrates everything

At stationarity, constant birth plus first-order death produces a Poisson distribution with mean b/db/d.

At steady state, probability flow between neighboring counts balances:

b pn=d(n+1)pn+1⟹pn+1=b/dn+1pn.b\,p_n=d(n+1)p_{n+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_{n+1}=\frac{b/d}{n+1}p_n. (6)

Iterating gives pn=p0(b/d)n/n!p_n=p_0(b/d)^n/n!. Normalization sets p0=e−b/dp_0=e^{-b/d}. Thus N∼Poisson⁡(λ)N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda) with λ=b/d\lambda=b/d. The mean equation also closes directly: multiply the CME by nn, sum, and reindex to obtain d⟨N⟩/dt=b−d⟨N⟩d\langle N\rangle/dt=b-d\langle N\rangle. At stationarity,

⟨N⟩=Var⁡(N)=bd,F=Var⁡(N)⟨N⟩=1.\langle N\rangle=\operatorname{Var}(N)=\frac{b}{d},\qquad F=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(N)}{\langle N\rangle}=1. (7)

Homework 5 makes this derivation the baseline for every later burst model.2 A Fano factor above one is evidence against this baseline, not a diagnosis by itself. Promoter switching, translation bursts, feedback, cell-cycle variation, and mixed cell states can all widen a histogram. The classic two-stage gene-expression analysis already shows how translation can lift protein variance above the Poisson reference.6

6One draw gives the next time

Do not march through empty timesteps. Sample the next event time directly.

At fixed state xx, let a0(x)=∑rar(x)a_0(x)=\sum_r a_r(x). If no reaction has fired, the state and all propensities remain unchanged. The probability S(τ)S(\tau) that the system survives without an event for another τ\tau obeys

S(τ+dτ)=S(τ)[1−a0dτ]⟹S(τ)=e−a0τ.S(\tau+d\tau)=S(\tau)[1-a_0d\tau]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S(\tau)=e^{-a_0\tau}. (8)

If u1u_1 is uniform on (0,1)(0,1), inverse-transform sampling gives

τ=−ln⁡u1a0(x).\tau=-\frac{\ln u_1}{a_0(x)}. (9)

The memoryless clock is not added noise. It follows from the Markov assumption encoded in equation (2). If molecular history changes the hazard while the count is fixed, then the stated CME was already the wrong model.

8A path is not a distribution

One jagged history answers “what happened.” An ensemble answers “how likely.” Neither is the mean trajectory.

A Gillespie run is one possible history. Repeating the same initial condition builds an empirical distribution at a chosen time. Solving the CME builds that distribution directly. Averaging many paths estimates ⟨N(t)⟩\langle N(t)\rangle, which for this linear model follows the deterministic rate equation exactly. That agreement of means does not make any individual path deterministic.

The comparison must also align its question. A time average from one long path, an ensemble average across many cells at one time, and a stationary expectation coincide only under additional ergodic and equilibration conditions. A growing colony sampled after ten minutes may not have reached stationarity. A switching cell may not explore both promoter states during its lifetime. Record the initial condition, observation time, and averaging operation with every reported statistic.

Increasing volume while holding concentration and rate constants fixed increases event counts and reduces relative fluctuations. Kurtz's density-dependent limit makes this convergence precise.5 The deterministic ODE is therefore a large-system projection of the jump process under stated scaling, not the microscopic law with decorative noise added.

Part 3

Know what was made exact

The algorithm is rigorous. Its biological adequacy still depends on the model it samples.

9Exact is conditional

The direct SSA exactly samples the stated well-mixed Markov jump process. It does not make the process an exact cell.

The claim requires discrete molecular counts, a well-mixed volume, reaction hazards determined by the current state, and propensities that remain fixed between events. Spatial gradients, molecular crowding, delayed reactions, hidden conformational memory, changing volume, and extrinsic cell states can violate those hypotheses. Gillespie and colleagues emphasize that “exact” belongs to the chemical master equation being simulated.4

There is a second identification problem. A count histogram can be reproduced by mechanistically different promoter models while their waiting-time distributions differ.7 Keep event times and trajectories whenever possible. A histogram can reject a Poisson model, but it rarely names the missing mechanism alone.

10Build, simulate, falsify

A useful stochastic model ends with a comparison that could prove it inadequate.

Each team now chooses one small biological network and produces a six-line specification:

  1. Name the integer state and the admissible state space.
  2. List every reaction, its jump vector, and its propensity with units.
  3. Write one interior CME equation and every special boundary equation.
  4. Hand-trace one direct-method event with two recorded uniform draws.
  5. Choose an observable: mean, Fano factor, zero probability, waiting time, or first passage.
  6. Name one data pattern that the model cannot explain and one hidden process that might repair it.
The core story

Counts reveal events. Events are specified by jumps and clocks. Probability bookkeeping gives the CME. Survival and competition give the SSA. The Poisson model makes equation and simulation check each other. Only then do we decide whether the observable permits an ODE, demands a distribution, or exposes missing biology.
