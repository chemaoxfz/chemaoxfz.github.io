From order of magnitude in biology to binding and catalysis

Count what is in a cell and you get its timescales. Count how often molecules meet and you get its rates. The two counts land in the same place: a fast reversible layer sitting under a slow irreversible one, four orders of magnitude apart. That gap is where regulation lives, and it is why this course is built from exactly two kinds of reaction.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · lecture 2 · core page · 2026-09-03 (Thu) 10:40–12:15 · lecturer-taught

Lecture 1 estimated the physics. This one estimates the cell, and the estimate does not stop at a table of numbers. Once you know how much stuff is in a cell and how fast it moves, you can bound how fast anything inside it can possibly happen. Two bounds come out. Molecules meet at up to about 1010 M−1s−110^{10}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}. Enzymes turn over at about 10 s−110\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}. Those two numbers, and the distance between them, are the whole lecture.

The distance is not a defect. It is the reason a cell can be controlled. Binding reaches equilibrium about ten thousand times during one catalytic turnover, so the binding layer is a settled statistical-mechanics problem while the catalytic layer is still grinding forward. Lecture 8 will claim a cell is nothing but those two things. Today we measure the premises of that claim.

This page is the lecture note

It carries what happens in the room, and nothing else. Derivations go as far as the board goes, and stop. The exposition is the textbook chapter that finishes them: every step shown, four checkpoints with solutions, two interactive tools, written for someone studying alone. The lineage essay is the history: who made these methods, and where the standard retelling turns out to be wrong. Read this page before the lecture, the exposition after it.

Part 1

The ledger

How much stuff a cell has, and what follows from the fact that there is so little of it.

1A cubic micron is a femtolitre

One identity, and the conversion that falls out of it. Memorise both.

Start with the length ladder, because it fixes the scale of everything that follows.14 An atom is about 1 Å. A bacterium is about 1 µm. A human is about 1 m. A bacterium stands to a human exactly as an atom stands to a bacterium, four orders of magnitude in each step. Every object this course cares about lives in the first two decades above the atom.

1 μm3=10−18 m3=10−15 L=1 fL   and   1 fLof  water  weighs1 pg1\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3} = 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{m}^{3} = 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{L} = \pf{1\,\mathrm{fL}} \,\,\text{ and }\,\, 1\,\mathrm{fL} \text{of \text{ water } weighs} \pf{1\,\mathrm{pg}}(1)

That identity is why a bacterium can be treated as a chemical reactor at all, and it gives the conversion the rest of the course leans on. One molecule in one femtolitre is

c=1/(NA⋅10−15 L)=1/(6.02×1023⋅10−15)=1.7 nMc = 1 / (N_{A} \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{L}) = 1 / (6.02\times 10^{23} \cdot 10^{-15}) = \pf{1.7\,\mathrm{nM}}(2)

Say it out loud. One molecule per cell is 1.7 nanomolar. A transcription factor present in ten copies is at 17 nM, the same order as its dissociation constant for DNA. Lecture 7 will explain why that cannot be a coincidence.

One caveat before we build on it. The 1 µm³ cell is the small end of the range: E. coli runs 1.5 fL in slow growth to 4.4 fL in rich medium, with the width fixed near 1.26 µm and the length doing the changing.1 Use 1 fL for a bound, and remember that cell size tracks growth rate, which is itself physiology (lecture 14).

2What is in it

A protein weighs 33 kDa, there are three million of them, and the classroom number for that count is five times too high.

Two log-averages get the mass of one protein. Amino acids run 75 to 204 Da, so 75×204=124\sqrt{75\times 204} = 124, and a residue in a chain is 18 Da lighter because the peptide bond releases water. Carry 110 Da per residue.2 Lengths run 50 to 5000, so 50×5000=500\sqrt{50\times 5000} = 500 residues against a measured mean of 300. High by 1.7, which is what order of magnitude promises and no better.

mprotein=300×110 Da≈33 kDam_{\text{protein}} = 300 \times 110\,\mathrm{Da} \approx \pf{33\,\mathrm{kDa}}(3)

Now count them, and get it wrong first. Wet mass over protein mass gives 1.1 pg/33 kDa=2×1071.1\,\mathrm{pg} / 33\,\mathrm{kDa} = 2\times 10^{7}. That is the number usually quoted, and it is an upper bound that assumes the cell is dry. A cell is 70% water, so cp=0.24c_{p} = 0.24 g/mL:2

N=cp/mprotein=0.24 g/mL÷5.5×10−20 g=3−4×106 proteins per  fLN = c_{p} / m_{\text{protein}} = 0.24\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{mL} \div 5.5\times 10^{-20}\,\mathrm{g} = \pf{3-4 \times 10^{6} \text{ proteins per }\,\mathrm{fL}}(4)
A correction worth carrying

The naive bound is five times too high, and the error is entirely the water. Milo found 2 to 4 million per cubic micron holds for bacteria, yeast and mammalian cells alike, and that several proteome-wide censuses report values 3 to 10 times lower and are probably mis-calibrated.2 Order of magnitude used as lecture 1 advertised: a benchmark that catches an experiment.

The concentration ladder

Divide by 4400 protein-coding genes: about 800 copies per species, which by (2) is 1.4 µM. Metabolites are three orders higher, with a measured total near 300 mM and glutamate alone at 96 mM.3

Long, and fat

Two entries close the ledger, and they point opposite ways. At 0.34 nm per base pair the 4.64 Mbp genome is 1.6 mm long, sixteen hundred cell lengths, and as a 1 nm cylinder it occupies 0.5% of the volume. Protein does the reverse. Erickson's formula sizes it from nothing but its weight,4 and three million of them fill 15% of the cell:

Rmin=(3V/4π)1/3=0.066⋅M1/3 nm=2.1 nm for 33 kDa (M in daltons)R_{\mathrm{min}} = (3V/4\pi )^{1/3} = 0.066 \cdot M^{1/3}\,\mathrm{nm} = \pf{2.1\,\mathrm{nm}} \text{ for } 33\,\mathrm{kDa} \,(M \text{ in daltons})(5)

That radius is a minimum, since a real protein drags a hydration shell and its hydrodynamic radius is 20 to 40 percent larger. Section 4 needs the distinction. And the asymmetry is the point: the genome is long and takes no room, the proteome is short and fills the cell. Information is cheap to store and expensive to execute. So crowding is not a nuisance correction, and section 4 shows it slowing every machine by elevenfold.

3Why chemistry is all that happens

Fixed temperature, fixed pressure, no mechanical work. What is left is bond rearrangement, and enzymes decide when.

A cell holds temperature and pressure essentially constant. It compresses nothing, heats nothing, does no macroscopic work. Every change of state it undergoes is therefore a chemical reaction. Two questions follow, and this course keeps them apart.

  • Whether a reaction can proceed is thermodynamics. At fixed TT and pp the currency is Gibbs free energy, not heat. That is lecture 7.
  • How fast it proceeds is kinetics, and it is almost entirely about enzymes. That is today.

Insist on it, because biology violates the naive coupling between the two. Favourable reactions sit untouched for years inside a cell: hydrolysis of ATP, cleavage of a peptide bond, degradation of glucose. Enzymes accelerate them by 101010^{10} and more. Downhill tells you nothing about whether a reaction happens. A reaction happening tells you an enzyme decided it should.

Part 2

How fast things move

One formula turns a size into a diffusion coefficient. One scaling turns that into a time.

4Brownian motion, and the formula for D

Watch something jiggle, measure how far it goes in a second, and you have measured a molecular constant.

In June 1827 Robert Brown put pollen of Clarkia pulchella in water and saw particles moving with no apparent cause. What matters for us is what he was watching, and most retellings get it wrong. Not the pollen grain: a Clarkia grain is 100 µm across and far too large to jiggle visibly. Brown saw organelles ejected from the burst grain, spherosomes of modal radius 0.5 µm.5

1827

Brown's contribution was not the observation but the control. He repeated it with dead plants, fossilised wood, ground glass, and a fragment of the Sphinx, and saw the same motion. That killed the vital explanation. Same lesson as lecture 1's null models: the interesting move was removing the only mechanism anyone believed in.

He described displacements of about a micron in a second. For a 0.5 µm sphere the mean one-dimensional displacement in one second is 0.74 µm,5 so inverting ⟨∣x∣⟩=0.802Dt\langle |x|\rangle = 0.80\sqrt{2Dt} gives D=0.43 μm2/sD = 0.43\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}. Now the scaling that makes it useful. Diffusion balances thermal kicks against viscous drag. Stokes drag is F=6πηavF = 6\pi \eta av, so mobility μ∝1/a\mu \propto 1/a, and Einstein's D=μkBTD = \mu k_{B}T gives

D=kBT/(6πηa)  ⇒  D∝1/aD = k_{B}T / (6\pi \eta a) \,\,\Rightarrow \,\, \pf{D \propto 1/a}(6)

So the whole content of the calibration is one constant, kBT/6πη=D⋅a=0.21 μm3/sk_{B}T/6\pi \eta = D\cdot a = 0.21\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s}, against a true 0.245. An 1827 qualitative observation gets a fundamental constant to 13%. Divide by a protein's radius: D≈100 μm2/sD \approx 100\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s} in water.

The cytoplasm is not water

Elowitz and colleagues measured GFP inside E. coli and found D=7.7±2.5 μm2/sD = 7.7 \pm 2.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}, against 87 in dilute solution.6 An elevenfold slowdown, worsening fast with size: a 72 kDa fusion drops to 2.5, and a six-histidine tag halves it. The 2025 version of this lecture quoted 100 µm²/s inside the cell, which is the water value. Use 10 for a protein and 200 for a metabolite in cytoplasm, and say which you mean.

5τ ≈ x²/D, and what it predicts

The most useful scaling in the course, for the second time in two lectures. A null model earns its keep by being wrong in a specific place.

Lecture 1 got t≈x2/κt \approx x^{2}/\kappa for heat by stripping the derivatives off the diffusion equation. Same equation, same argument, for particles:

∂c/∂t=D∇2c ⇒ c/τ≈Dc/x2 ⇒ τ≈x2/D\partial c/\partial t = D\nabla^{2}c \,\Rightarrow \, c/\tau \approx D c/x^{2} \,\Rightarrow \, \pf{\tau \approx x^{2}/D}(7)

Exactly, in three dimensions, ⟨r2⟩=6Dt\langle r^{2}\rangle = 6Dt, so the simple form overestimates by six. That is inside the error bar of everything else here.

Read the last two rows as a prediction, not a curiosity. Passive diffusion cannot move protein along a metre-long axon. It is not slow, it is impossible: four thousand years against a lifetime. So something else must do it, and that something is kinesin and dynein on microtubules. The existence of motor-driven axonal transport is forced by τ∝x2\tau \propto x^{2} before anyone opens a cell.

The same reading works upward. A bacterium mixes a metabolite in five milliseconds, faster than any enzyme turns over. So one well-stirred concentration per species is not a modelling convenience inside a bacterium, it is a licensed approximation, and lecture 3's rate equations inherit their validity from this table. A Xenopus egg has no such licence, which is why developmental biology is full of gradients and bacterial physiology is not.

That is the habit worth installing. Compute the null model first, then find where it fails. The failure is where the biology is, and its size tells you how much biology is needed. Diffusion in an axon fails by 101110^{11}, so it needs a motor. In a bacterium it does not fail at all, so a cell that built a transport system for its cytoplasm would be wasting energy.

Part 3

The ceiling on a rate

What an arrow entitles you to write, one boundary-value problem, and what real enzymes do about the answer.

6What an arrow promises

Straight arrows are mechanisms and come with a rate law. Squiggly arrows are accounting and come with nothing.

This is the most important notational distinction in the course, and it is routinely ignored in the literature to everyone's cost.

The exposition works through five more, from a laser to the lac operon.

The habit to install now

When anyone writes a rate law, ask: is that arrow elementary? If it is, mass action gives the rate for free. If it is composite, the rate law must be derived (Michaelis-Menten, lecture 6) or measured, never assumed from the shape of the arrowhead. Half the modelling disputes you will meet are this question in disguise.

Binding, then catalysis

An enzyme and its substrate collide, bind, and then the enzyme's atoms hold the substrate in a geometry that lets the bond rearrange. Two steps, and only the first is physics:

E+S ⇌ C ⇝ E+PE + S \,\rightleftharpoons\, C \,\rightsquigarrow\, E + P(8)

The first step is how often two molecules meet, which is diffusion, which we can now compute. The second is chemistry inside a protein, which we cannot compute and which the enzyme's structure decides. So the fastest an enzymatic reaction can go is set by the first step alone, assuming catalysis is instant once the complex forms. That bound is the diffusion-limited on-rate. Notice what happened: we split a reaction into a part physics can bound and a part it cannot, and that split reappears in lecture 8 as the two components of a cell.

7The diffusion-limited on-rate, derived

Fick's law in spherical symmetry, two boundary conditions, one integration. This is the anchor derivation of the course.

Put an enzyme at the origin, a perfectly absorbing sphere of radius aa: any substrate touching it is consumed instantly. Far away the substrate sits at bulk C∞C_{\infty}. Let it reach steady state. How many molecules arrive at the surface per second?

Step 1. Steady state means constant flux through every shell. Nothing accumulates anywhere, so the number crossing a sphere of radius RR per second is the same for every RR. With jj the flux per unit area, 4πR2j4\pi R^{2}j is a constant, and it is not zero.

Step 2. Solve the steady diffusion equation. Fick's second law is ∂C/∂t=D∇2C\partial C/\partial t = D\nabla^{2}C. In spherical symmetry the angular terms vanish:

(1/R2)d/dR(R2dC/dR)=0 ⇒ R2dC/dR=A ⇒ C(R)=−A/R+B(1/R^{2}) d/dR (R^{2} dC/dR) = 0 \,\Rightarrow \, R^{2} dC/dR = A \,\Rightarrow \, C(R) = -A/R + B(9)

Step 3. Fix the constants. Absorbing surface, C(a)=0C(a) = 0. Bulk at infinity, C(∞)=C∞C(\infty ) = C_{\infty}. So B=C∞B = C_{\infty} and A=aC∞A = aC_{\infty}, giving C(R)=C∞(1−a/R)C(R) = C_{\infty}(1 - a/R).

Step 4. Differentiate, multiply by the area. The surface gradient is ∂C/∂R∣a=C∞/a\partial C/\partial R|_{a} = C_{\infty}/a, so j=DC∞/aj = DC_{\infty}/a and

J=j⋅4πa2=4πDaC∞ ≡ konC∞,  kon=4πDaJ = j \cdot 4\pi a^{2} = \pf{4\pi Da C_{\infty}} \,\equiv \, k_{\text{on}}C_{\infty}, \,\, k_{\text{on}} = 4\pi Da(10)

Step 5. Check the dimensions, then put numbers in. kon=4πDak_{\text{on}} = 4\pi Da has dimensions (L2/T)(L)=L3/T(L^{2}/T)(L) = L^{3}/T, a volume swept per unit time, which is what a second-order rate constant is once you divide by a molar volume. So the conversion to M−1s−1M^{-1}s^{-1} is a factor of NAN_{A} and a factor of 10310^{3} and nothing else. If your conversion needs more factors than that, you have made an error. For a pair, use the relative coefficient D=DA+DBD = D_{A}+D_{B} and the sum of radii. Glucose against an enzyme in water:

kon=4π⋅7.7×10−10 m2/s⋅2.4×10−9 m⋅103 L/m3⋅6.02×1023/mol=1.4×1010 M−1s−1k_{\text{on}} = 4\pi \cdot 7.7\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s} \cdot 2.4\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m} \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{L}/\mathrm{m}^{3} \cdot 6.02\times 10^{23}/\mathrm{mol} = \pf{1.4\times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}}(11)

Inside cytoplasm, with both coefficients cut by crowding, it gives 3.8×1093.8\times 10^{9}. For two proteins in water, 6×1096\times 10^{9}, against the published 7×1097\times 10^{9} for two 18 Å spheres reacting on every contact.7

The bound

For two species diffusing freely in solution and reacting on every contact, kon=4π(DA+DB)(aA+aB)k_{\text{on}} = 4\pi (D_{A}+D_{B})(a_{A}+a_{B}). No unguided bimolecular reaction in solution exceeds it. For biological molecules that is 10910^{9} to 1010 M−1s−110^{10}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

The derivation matters more than the number. It turns "how fast can this be?" from a question of opinion into a boundary-value problem with one answer. If a paper reports an on-rate above 101010^{10} for an uncharged pair, either the measurement is wrong or there is a mechanism.

8What reaches the ceiling, and what falls short

One enzyme gets to 73% of its own bound. One famous system appears to beat it. The median enzyme is five decades below.

Real enzymes must do more than collide. The substrate has to reach a small active-site patch in the right orientation, and orientational constraints cost up to three orders of magnitude.7 There are only two known ways around that: pull the substrate in with an electric field, or reduce the dimension of the search.

The best enzyme in the world, against its own bound

Superoxide dismutase acts on O2⋅−\mathrm{O}_2^{\cdot-}, a tiny charged ion with D=1230 μm2/sD = 1230\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}. With a 32 kDa enzyme the bound for this pair is 4πDa=2.3×1010 M−1s−14\pi Da = 2.3\times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}. An engineered mutant reaches 1.7×10101.7\times 10^{10}, the fastest rate ever measured for any enzyme.7 That is 73% of the physical ceiling.

The second way is to change the dimension of the search. The lac repressor was measured in 1970 finding its operator a hundred times faster than the 10810^{8} bound for a target that small,8 and the resolution is facilitated diffusion: bind DNA non-specifically anywhere, then slide in one dimension until the operator turns up. The signature is that more DNA around a site makes the site easier to find, which is impossible for a 3D search and automatic for a 1D one. The exposition works the numbers.

Correct the story while you tell it, though. Halford and Marko went through the record and found that most measured protein-DNA associations sit within a factor of three of 108 M−1s−110^{8}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}, the ordinary limit.8 The bound is sound, a few systems beat it by changing the search, and "DNA-binding proteins are all impossibly fast" is folklore.

Now the other side

At 1 mM substrate an enzyme at the diffusion limit would see 10710^{7} encounters per second. Enzymes do not do that. Bar-Even and colleagues collected kinetic parameters for thousands of pairs, filtered to natural substrates, and reported the distribution.9 It is the central fact of enzyme kinetics and it should be uncomfortable.

The distribution to compare it against is the uncatalysed one

Figure 7 looks like a story about how slow enzymes are. Put the other histogram beside it and it becomes a story about how narrow they are.

Wolfenden and Snider measured the spontaneous rates of biological reactions in neutral solution at 25 °C, some by Arrhenius extrapolation from high temperature because they are too slow to watch.16 The half-times span more than sixteen orders of magnitude: 5 seconds for the hydration of CO2, 38 hours for the isomerisation of chorismate, two months for the isomerisation of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, 400 years for a peptide bond, 140 000 years for a phosphodiester, 78 million years for the decarboxylation of orotidine 5′-phosphate, and 1.1 billion years for the decarboxylation of an amino acid.

The catalysed rates span about one decade. Most kcatk_{\mathrm{cat}} values sit between 10210^{2} and 103 s−110^{3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1} in their sample, and Bar-Even's median across a wider set is 10 s−110\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}. Sixteen decades in, one decade out. The rate enhancement is therefore not a number an enzyme has, it is whatever the substrate needed: 10710^{7}-fold at one end and 101910^{19}-fold at the other.

Read that as a control statement, because it is one

A plant whose gain varies over sixteen decades, and an output held inside one. Every control course would call that a compensator, and would immediately ask the two questions that follow. Is the flatness the controlled variable, or is it a consequence of something else? And what supplies the loop, given that no single enzyme knows what the others are doing?

The honest answer is that nobody has settled it. Selection acting on each enzyme separately can produce the same flat output without any loop at all, because the useful output range is set by what the cell can consume, not by what the chemistry offers. That is a real alternative and the histogram alone does not choose between them. It is the first topic in §12.

Why so slow? Two readings, and the argument is live. Bar-Even et al. argue the gap is evolutionary: enzymes are as fast as selection required and no faster, and their evidence is that context predicts speed, with central carbon metabolism at a median 79 s−179\,\mathrm{s}^{-1} against 2.5 s−12.5\,\mathrm{s}^{-1} for secondary metabolism.9 The reading this course pushes is structural: the gap is what makes a cell controllable, because it is the gap that lets binding equilibrate. Both fit the histogram. They differ in what they predict, and section 10 is where the structural reading earns its keep.

Part 4

What the gap buys

Two independent arguments, both ending at the same architecture.

9Binding equilibrates while catalysis works

Put the two rate constants in one expression and the separation of timescales stops being an assumption.

Take the binding step alone. For E+S⇌CE + S \rightleftharpoons C with koff=konKDk_{\text{off}} = k_{\text{on}}K_{D}, a small perturbation relaxes with τbind=1/(kon([E]+[S])+koff)\tau_{\mathrm{bind}} = 1/(k_{\text{on}}([E]+[S]) + k_{\text{off}}). Use the medians from Tables 1 and 4: enzyme 1 µM, substrate 1 mM, KD=100 μMK_{D} = 100\,\mu\mathrm{M}, kon=108k_{\text{on}} = 10^{8}, already an order below the ceiling.

The number the whole course rests on

Binding settles about 10410^{4} times during one catalytic turnover. So when you write a model in which the bound fraction is an equilibrium expression and the flux is a rate, you are not making a convenient assumption. You are quoting a measurement. This licenses every quasi-steady-state reduction in lectures 3, 6 and 8, and licenses lecture 7 to use equilibrium statistical mechanics on a cell that is manifestly not at equilibrium.

Two honest caveats. The separation is not universal: a transcription factor at 10 nM binding an operator with KD=1 nMK_{D} = 1\,\mathrm{nM} relaxes in tens of milliseconds, comparable to a turnover, and lecture 6's telegraph model exists because of that. And in lower glycolysis the pools sit near KMK_{M} with every reaction close to equilibrium.3 Where the cell needs reversible flux, it gives up the separation deliberately.

10Metabolism cannot wait for gene expression

The same conclusion from the other end, using nothing but two timescales.

Forget rate constants. Ask a physiological question instead. Can a cell control its metabolism by changing how much enzyme it makes?

How fast does a pool turn over? Turnover is pool over flux. E. coli on glucose takes up 8 mmol per gram dry weight per hour, and from the ledger one gram dry weight occupies 3.0 mL of cell, so

J=8 mmol⋅gDW−1h−1÷3.0 mL⋅gDW−1=2.6 M/h=0.73 mM/sJ = 8\,\mathrm{mmol}\cdot \mathrm{gDW}^{-1}h^{-1} \div 3.0\,\mathrm{mL}\cdot \mathrm{gDW}^{-1} = 2.6\,\mathrm{M}/\mathrm{h} = \pf{0.73\,\mathrm{mM}/\mathrm{s}}(13)

How fast can gene expression change an enzyme level? Write the balance for a protein that is not actively degraded, in a cell growing at rate μ\mu. Dilution by growth is the only loss term:

dP/dt=β−μP ⇒  response half −time =ln⁡2/μ=the doubling timedP/dt = \beta - \mu P \,\Rightarrow \, \text{ response half }-\text{time } = \ln2/\mu = \pf{\text{the doubling time}}(14)

That is a derivation, not a citation, and it is the most under-appreciated fact in gene regulation. For a stable protein a cell cannot change concentration faster than it can grow. At a 40-minute doubling time, halving an enzyme level takes 40 minutes. Even the fastest route is slow: 80% of E. coli mRNA half-lives fall between 3 and 8 minutes,10 so transcriptional control cannot beat a few minutes in principle.

The conclusion, and it is forced

Gene expression responds in 2400 seconds. The PEP pool empties in 0.12 seconds. Expression is 2×1042\times 10^{4} times too slow to control it, and still 117 times too slow for the slowest pool in the table. A cell regulating metabolism only through expression would run its pools to zero or overflow them thousands of times before the first new enzyme arrived. So a faster layer must exist, and the timescale ladder offers one candidate: binding, at 9 µs. That layer is allostery and covalent modification, and this argument predicts them rather than describing them.

Same conclusion as section 9, reached without writing a single rate constant. Two independent routes, one architecture. That is what makes it a claim about the world rather than a modelling convention.

1956, and what it took to see this

The prediction was confirmed before it was made. In 1956 Umbarger showed isoleucine inhibiting the first enzyme of its own pathway, and Yates and Pardee showed the same for pyrimidines.11 Neither product resembles the substrate of the enzyme it inhibits, so the inhibition cannot be competitive, and the resolution was a second site. Monod, Wyman and Changeux named it allostery in 1965 and gave it a two-state equilibrium model.12 Lecture 7 derives that model. Lecture 8 argues it is one of only two things a cell does.

12Eight things worth extending

Eight questions that this lecture opens and does not close. Each one is reachable with the numbers on this page plus one paper, and each names what a finished answer looks like.

An extension mini-essay is short. Take one of these all the way down rather than surveying three. What makes a good one is a number at the end that was not in the lecture, together with an honest account of what it rests on.

  1. Sixteen decades in, one decade out. Uncatalysed half-times span 101610^{16}; turnover numbers span about 10110^{1}.16 Is the narrow output a controlled variable or a coincidence of what cells happen to consume? Take Wolfenden's reactions, pair each with its enzyme's kcatk_{\mathrm{cat}}, and plot enhancement against uncatalysed rate. A slope of exactly −1-1 means perfect compensation, and anything else says how much of the flattening is real. This is the best question on the page and it has a plot at the end of it.
  2. The factor of four in 4πDa4\pi Da. Smoluchowski's radius is an encounter radius, so for two identical spheres the constant is 16πDa16\pi Da, not 4πDa4\pi Da. Go and check a dozen papers and textbooks that quote the bound for a self-association and count how many get the factor right. A finished answer is a table with a column headed "which radius did they mean", and a statement of how much the published conclusions would move.
  3. Which volume do you mean. §7 of the exposition gets 15% of cell volume as protein using the anhydrous partial specific volume, 0.73 mL/g. Zimmerman and Trach measure an occupancy near 0.43 mL per mL of cytoplasm, using an effective specific volume of 1.2 to 1.3 mL/g.17 The factor of three between them is entirely a definition. Work out which one governs the excluded-volume effect, which one governs the mass balance, and where each is the right answer. Then say which one belongs in the crowding argument.
  4. Does the diffusion limit ever bind in vivo? The ceiling in this lecture is computed for water. In cytoplasm DD drops elevenfold for a protein, so the in-cell ceiling drops with it. Davidi and colleagues measured apparent catalytic rates in living cells.18 Take their distribution, compute the pair-specific in-cell bound for each enzyme, and ask how many are within a decade of it. If none are, the ceiling is not what shapes the distribution and this course should say so.
  5. Perfection, on the original definition. "Catalytic perfection" was defined by Albery and Knowles as a property of the whole free-energy profile at a given substrate concentration, not as a threshold on kcat/KMk_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_{M}. The lineage page states the criterion. Pick one enzyme other than triosephosphate isomerase for which the profile is known, evaluate their efficiency function, and compare the verdict with what its kcat/KMk_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_{M} alone would suggest. Any disagreement is the essay.
  6. Berg and Purcell's receptors, for a real cell. A sphere covered in NN small absorbing patches of radius ss captures at 4πDa⋅Ns/(Ns+πa)4\pi Da \cdot Ns/(Ns + \pi a), so half the maximum rate arrives at a coverage far below complete. Put in measured receptor counts and sizes for one chemotactic bacterium and say where it sits on that curve. If it sits near the knee, that is a design statement. If it sits far below, ask what else the receptors are for.
  7. Where the timescale separation fails. §9 licenses quasi-steady state from a factor of 10410^{4}. That factor is a median. Find the enzymes in the tail where it is not 10410^{4}, and work out what a model that assumed separation would get wrong about them. Briggs and Haldane's condition is two lines long and stating it exactly is half the essay.
  8. The ledger for something that is not E. coli. Every number in Part 1 is for one bacterium. Redo the whole ledger for a yeast cell, a human cell, or a mitochondrion: volume, protein count, mean spacing, mixing time, on-rate, turnover. Which conclusions survive the change of scale, and which quietly stop being true? Mixing time is the one to watch, because it goes as x2x^{2}.
What makes one of these good rather than long

State the question in one sentence. Say what you assumed and where each input came from. Do the arithmetic in the open. Give the answer to one digit. Then say what would change it. An essay doing those five things in a page beats one doing none of them in six.

§Exercises

Three that can be done on paper, in the order the lecture built them. The first two are from the 2025 problem set.14 The first extension mini-essay may be on any of them.

  1. The blueprint and the machine. Which is bigger, an mRNA or the protein it encodes, in mass and in volume? Do it for a 300-residue protein, using 330 Da per nucleotide, and draw the two to scale. Then say what the answer implies about the cost of holding a message versus holding a machine. (2025 homework 1, problem 2.1.)
  2. Head space in a culture tube. The rule of thumb is to leave five-fold head space when growing aerobic bacteria. Estimate the oxygen a saturated 1 mL E. coli culture needs, using 0.2% glucose by mass in the medium and a biomass yield of about 0.5, and check whether the rule follows. Does dissolved oxygen matter? (2025 homework 1, problem 2.3.)
  3. Redo section 10 for a mammalian cell. A human cell is 2000 µm³ and doubles in about 24 hours. Metabolite concentrations are similar. Does the conclusion get stronger or weaker, and by how much? What does that predict about how much of mammalian metabolic regulation should be post-translational?
