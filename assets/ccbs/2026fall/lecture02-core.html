From order of magnitude in biology to binding and catalysis

Count what is in a cell and you get its timescales. Then notice that everything a cell does is a chemical reaction, so those timescales are reaction rates. The rest of the lecture is what a reaction arrow does and does not promise, how fast one can possibly go, and what a cell buys with the distance between that ceiling and where its enzymes actually sit.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · lecture 2 · core page · 2026-09-03 (Thu) 10:40–12:15 · lecturer-taught

Lecture 1 estimated the physics. This one estimates the cell, in three moves. Count the molecules, so you know the concentrations. Count how fast they move, so you know the times. Part 1

What is in a cell

How much stuff a cell has, and what follows from there being so little of it.

1A cubic micron is a femtolitre

One identity, and the conversion that falls out of it. Memorise both.

Start with the length ladder, which fixes the scale of everything after it.12 Atom 1 Å, bacterium 1 µm, human 1 m.

1 μm3=10−18 m3=10−15 L=1 fL   and   1 fLof  water  weighs1 pg1\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3} = 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{m}^{3} = 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{L} = \pf{1\,\mathrm{fL}} \,\,\text{ and }\,\, 1\,\mathrm{fL} \text{of \text{ water } weighs} \pf{1\,\mathrm{pg}}(1)

That identity gives the conversion the rest of the course leans on. One molecule in one femtolitre is

c=1/(NA⋅10−15 L)=1.66×10−9 M≈1 nMc = 1 / (N_{A} \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{L}) = 1.66\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{M} \approx \pf{1\,\mathrm{nM}}(2)

Say it out loud. One molecule per cell is one nanomolar. The arithmetic gives 1.7, and order of magnitude resolves one, few and ten, so 1.7 is one. Round it and it becomes a number you keep. A transcription factor present in ten copies is then at 10 nM, the same order as its dissociation constant for DNA. Lecture 7 explains why that is not a coincidence.

One caveat. The 1 µm³ cell is the small end: E. coli runs 1.5 fL in slow growth to 4.4 fL in rich medium.1 Use 1 fL for a bound, and remember that cell size tracks growth rate (lecture 14).

2What is in it

A protein weighs 33 kDa and a cell holds three million of them. Neither number is looked up. Both come out of brackets you can write on the board.

To count the proteins in a cell you need two quantities: the mass of one protein, and the total mass of protein. Take them in that order. Neither is a number to memorise, and getting both from brackets is the point of the section.

The move: bracket it, then take the geometric mean

When you know a quantity's two extremes but not its middle, estimate it as xmin⁡xmax⁡\sqrt{x_{\min}x_{\max}}. That is the arithmetic mean of the logarithms, so it lands in the middle of the range of magnitudes rather than being dragged towards the top by the larger endpoint. One rule for using it: bracket what is common, not what is possible. A rare extreme is still an extreme, and the mean cannot know it is rare. The exposition proves the rule: the estimate's error is exactly the bracket's log-asymmetry about the truth, and nothing else.

The mass of one protein. Two brackets, multiplied. Amino acids are small organic molecules, and the extremes are easy: glycine is the bare backbone at 75 Da, tryptophan carries a double ring at 204 Da. A peptide bond releases one water, so a residue inside a chain weighs 18 Da less than the free acid.

mresidue≈75×204 Da−18 Da=106 Dam_{\text{residue}} \approx \sqrt{75\times 204}\,\mathrm{Da} - 18\,\mathrm{Da} = \pf{106\,\mathrm{Da}}(3)

Carry 110 Da per residue, which is the round number the field quotes. The bracket's job here was to tell us we can trust it, and checking a quoted constant against a bracket is the cheapest use this method has. Now the length. One folded domain is about 100 residues and a large multi-domain enzyme about 1000, so 100×1000=316\sqrt{100\times 1000} = 316, call it 300 residues. Bracket what is possible instead, 50 residues to 5000, and the same move returns 500, which is high by 1.7. Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic. The upper endpoint is simply rare, and that is the rule in the box earning its keep. Multiply the two brackets:

mprotein≈300×110 Da=33 kDam_{\text{protein}} \approx 300 \times 110\,\mathrm{Da} = \pf{33\,\mathrm{kDa}}(4)

The total mass of protein. Equation (1) says one femtolitre of water weighs one picogram, and a cell is a little denser than water at 1.1 g/mL, so a 1 µm³ cell weighs about 1.1 pg wet. Two subtractions turn that into protein, and they are where this estimate is usually lost.

First the water. Biomass is about 70% water, which is the one composition number worth memorising, and it leaves 0.33 pg of dry mass. Then the rest of the dry mass: protein is roughly half of it, and RNA, DNA, lipid and the wall take the other half. So a cell carries about 0.18 pg of protein, a protein mass concentration of cp≈0.2 g/mLc_{p} \approx 0.2\,\mathrm{g/mL}. Divide by (4):

N=cpVmprotein=0.18 pg33 kDa=3.3×106 per fLN = \frac{c_{p}V}{m_{\text{protein}}} = \frac{0.18\,\mathrm{pg}}{33\,\mathrm{kDa}} = \pf{3.3\times 10^{6}} \text{ per fL}(5)

Both subtractions are worth doing out loud, because each one is a factor. Put the whole wet mass on top and you get 2×1072\times 10^{7}, six times too many. Stop after the water and you get 6×1066\times 10^{6}, which is wrong as an answer and useful as a bound: a cell cannot hold more protein than that.

Against the measurements

The measured protein mass concentration in E. coli is 0.24 g/mL, so the honest count is 4.4 million and (5) is low by a third. Milo's systematic pass over the census data puts it at 2 to 4 million per cubic micron, and finds the same density in bacteria, in yeast and in mammalian cells.2 That near-universality is why the number is worth carrying at all. The same pass finds several proteome-wide censuses reporting values 3 to 10 times lower, and argues they are mis-calibrated: order of magnitude used as lecture 1 advertised, as a benchmark that catches an experiment.

Both brackets hold. The abundance-weighted mean residue mass is 110 Da, against 106 from (3). The abundance-weighted mean protein length is 300 residues in E. coli and 400 in yeast and human, against 316 from the common bracket and 500 from the wide one.

The concentration ladder

Three million proteins, but of how many kinds? A third bracket, and this one is free, because (4) already fixed it. A 300-residue protein needs 3×300=9003\times 300 = 900 nucleotides of coding sequence, and a bacterial genome is dense, about 90% coding. E. coli's is 4.6 Mbp, so

ngenes≈0.9×4.6×106 nt900 nt=4600n_{\text{genes}} \approx \frac{0.9 \times 4.6\times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{nt}}{900\,\mathrm{nt}} = \pf{4600}(6)

against 4,400 annotated. The average species is therefore present in 3.3×106/4400≈7503.3\times 10^{6}/4400 \approx 750 copies, and by (2) one copy per cell is one nanomolar, so the average protein sits near 1 µM. That is the whole ladder in one line: a count becomes a concentration by multiplying by a nanomolar. Metabolites are three orders higher, with a measured total near 300 mM and glutamate alone at 96 mM.3

Two entries close the ledger, pointing opposite ways. The 4.6 Mbp genome that (6) just used is 1.6 mm of DNA and takes 0.5% of the volume. The three million proteins take 13%.4 Information is cheap to store and expensive to execute, and §3 shows the crowding slowing every machine elevenfold.

Part 2

How fast things move

One formula turns a size into a diffusion coefficient. One scaling turns that into a time.

3Brownian motion, and the formula for D

Watch something jiggle, measure how far it goes in a second, and lecture 1's scaling turns it into a molecular constant.

Brown, 1827, pollen in water under a microscope. What jiggled was not the grain. A Clarkia grain is 100 µm across and far too heavy to move visibly, and what moved were the granules the grain bursts and releases. Their size is bracketed by what he could do: far smaller than the 100 µm grain, and still visible in a light microscope, which resolves about half a micron. Call them 1 µm across, so a≈0.5 μma \approx 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}. Their wandering is of the same order in about a second, x≈1 μmx \approx 1\,\mu\mathrm{m}.

That is already enough for DD. Lecture 1 got t≈x2/κt \approx x^{2}/\kappa out of the heat equation by replacing every derivative with its variation over one characteristic scale. Diffusion of matter is that equation with DD in place of κ\kappa, and here we read it the other way round: the size and the time are what we measured, so the constant is what falls out.

∂c∂t=D∂2c∂x2 ⇒ ct≈Dcx2 ⇒ D≈x2t=(1 μm)21 s=1 μm2/s\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^{2}c}{\partial x^{2}} \,\Rightarrow\, \frac{c}{t} \approx \frac{Dc}{x^{2}} \,\Rightarrow\, D \approx \frac{x^{2}}{t} = \frac{(1\,\mu\mathrm{m})^{2}}{1\,\mathrm{s}} = \pf{1\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}}(7)

Two numbers read off a slide, and a molecular constant comes out. Section 4 runs the same line forwards, turning a size into a time.

What the careful version gives

Pearle and colleagues repeated the observation with a microscope of Brown's own period, and measured what he could have measured. The granules have radius 0.5 µm and a mean displacement of 0.74 µm in one second, and inverting ⟨∣x∣⟩=0.802Dt\langle |x|\rangle = 0.80\sqrt{2Dt} gives D=0.43 μm2/sD = 0.43\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}.5 Equation (7) is high by 2.3, which is what reading a squiggle off a slide should give. The lineage has the rest of the story.

Now go from a granule to a protein, which is a factor of a few hundred in radius. That needs to know how DD depends on size, and the answer is less obvious than it looks.

Diffusion is thermal kicks balanced against viscous drag, so the question is the drag on a sphere of radius aa moving at speed vv. Two powers of aa are in play, and they pull opposite ways. A viscous stress is the viscosity times a velocity gradient, and the fluid has to go from vv at the sphere's surface to zero far away, over the only length in the problem. So the gradient is v/av/a and the stress is ηv/a\eta v/a. That stress acts over the sphere's surface, which is a2a^{2}. Multiply:

F≈ηva⋅a2=ηav,one power of a, not twoF \approx \frac{\eta v}{a}\cdot a^{2} = \pf{\eta a v}, \quad \text{one power of }a\text{, not two}(8)

That near-cancellation is the whole answer to why 1/a, and it is worth saying out loud, because the naive guess is that drag goes as the area. A bigger sphere does present more surface, and it sits in a gentler gradient by the same factor, so the drag grows only linearly in size. This also fixes what aa means: it is the one length that sets both the area and the gradient, so it is the hydrodynamic radius, the size of the object plus whatever solvent it drags along. Stokes did the flow properly in 1851 and got the prefactor, F=6πηavF = 6\pi \eta a v. Mobility is μ=v/F=1/(6πηa)\mu = v/F = 1/(6\pi\eta a), and Einstein's relation D=μkBTD = \mu k_{B}T closes it:

D=kBT/(6πηa)  ⇒  D∝1/aD = k_{B}T / (6\pi \eta a) \,\,\Rightarrow \,\, \pf{D \propto 1/a}(9)

Everything in that formula except the size is one constant, D⋅a=kBT/6πηD\cdot a = k_{B}T/6\pi \eta, and Brown's jiggle has already measured it: 1 μm2/s×0.5 μm=0.5 μm3/s1\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s} \times 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m} = 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s}, against a true 0.245. A qualitative observation from 1827 gets a fundamental constant to a factor of two, and the careful numbers in the box get it to 13%.

Now use it. A protein is close-packed amino acids, so it weighs what packed organic matter weighs, about 1.4 g/mL. The 33 kDa of (4) then occupies 40 nm³ and has radius (3V/4π)1/3≈2 nm(3V/4\pi)^{1/3} \approx 2\,\mathrm{nm}. That is the factor of a few hundred promised above. Divide the constant by it:

Dprotein≈0.245 μm3/s2 nm=100 μm2/sin waterD_{\text{protein}} \approx \frac{0.245\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s}}{2\,\mathrm{nm}} = \pf{100\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}} \quad \text{in water}(10)
The cytoplasm is not water

Elowitz and colleagues measured GFP inside E. coli and found D=7.7±2.5 μm2/sD = 7.7 \pm 2.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}, against 87 in dilute solution.6 An elevenfold slowdown, worsening fast with size: a 72 kDa fusion drops to 2.5. The 2025 version of this lecture quoted 100 µm²/s inside the cell, which is the water value. Use 10 for a protein and 200 for a metabolite in cytoplasm, and say which you mean.

Part 3

It is all chemistry

Why the two ledgers just built are ledgers of reaction rates, and why a cell can run only a small corner of chemistry.

5The core is chemistry

Chemistry contains every cell, which is a gift. It also contains a zoo no cell can use, and one fact about how a cell works makes the cut.

A cell holds temperature and pressure constant, compresses nothing and does no macroscopic work. So every change of state it undergoes is a chemical reaction. Motion, decision, memory, division, the whole repertoire. Mechanical, electrical, magnetic, thermal and optical signals matter, and every one of them acts on the boundary of that chemistry rather than replacing it.

Take that seriously, because a class that provably contains your system is what makes a failed model informative. Mechanics has one and circuits have one. Chemistry is biology's, and has been since the 1970s.23

Now the bill. Chemistry lets you write down almost anything: A→BA \to B, a binding A+B⇌CA + B \rightleftharpoons C, a catalysis A+B→A+CA + B \to A + C, and in general any list of species turning into another list,

α1X1+⋯+αnXn ⟶ β1X1+⋯+βnXn\alpha_{1}X_{1} + \dots + \alpha_{n}X_{n} \,\longrightarrow\, \beta_{1}X_{1} + \dots + \beta_{n}X_{n}(12)

including absurdities such as 10X→9X10X \to 9X, ten molecules colliding at once and one vanishing. The class is enormous, and most of it is unreachable.

A cell cannot reach most of it, and the reason is a constraint rather than a preference. In a beaker you drive a reaction by raising the temperature, changing the pressure, or swinging the pH. A cell can do none of those and live. Its one lever is whether the catalyst is present, and in what state. So favourable reactions sit untouched for years: ATP hydrolysis, peptide-bond cleavage, glucose degradation. Wolfenden's group measured the uncatalysed half-times and they run from 27 seconds to 1.1 × 1012 years.13 Enzymes accelerate those by 10710^{7} to 101910^{19}.

Every reaction that matters in a cell is therefore enzymatic, and an enzymatic reaction is two moves and no more. Enzyme and substrate meet and stick, which is binding. The complex rearranges bonds and lets go, which is catalysis.

E+S ⇌k1k−1 C ⟶k2 E+PE + S \,\underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_{1}}{\rightleftharpoons}}\, C \,\overset{k_{2}}{\longrightarrow}\, E + P(13)
The cut, and it is the shape of the course

All of chemistry contains every cell. The part a cell can actually run is binding and catalysis, because the only lever a cell has is the catalyst. That is why this course is built out of exactly two kinds of step, and lecture 8 turns it into a thesis about what a cell is.

Keep the two questions apart while you use it. Whether a reaction can proceed is thermodynamics, and at fixed TT and pp the currency is Gibbs free energy: lecture 7. How fast it proceeds is kinetics, and it is almost entirely about enzymes: today. Downhill tells you nothing about whether a reaction happens. A reaction happening tells you an enzyme decided it should.

Part 4

What a reaction is

One word, two objects. Getting them confused is the most common modelling error in the field, and it is entirely avoidable with a notation.

6Two meanings of the word

A reaction step is a mechanism. An overall reaction is an accounting identity. Only the first one comes with a rate law.

Two different objects go by the name reaction, and the whole of Part 4 turns on holding them apart. Definitions first, then the notation, then what the difference buys.

Definition

A reaction step is a single microscopic event: the listed reactants meet, and the listed products result, with nothing intervening. Also called an elementary reaction, 基元反应. Irreducible, either because it cannot be broken down further or because it is useless to do so for your concern.

An overall reaction is the net change produced by a sequence of reaction steps. Also called a composite reaction, 复合反应. It says what was consumed and what was made. It says nothing about the route.

Now the notation, and be pedantic about it, because at least half the people working with these models confuse the two objects in print. Write a step with a straight arrow carrying its rate constant. Write an overall reaction with a bare squiggly arrow. The contrast is doubled on purpose: the shape says which object it is, and whether anything sits above the arrow says it again.

The third form in the table is the net-change notation, written on counts rather than species symbols. It registers only who lost what and who gained what, which is what lecture 3's stoichiometric matrix needs and what lecture 5 runs on, since a Gillespie step is a net change applied to a count.

Why the step is the special one

A reaction step is special because it comes with a rate law, and the rate law is a consequence rather than a convention. Fix attention on one A molecule. It sweeps out some volume per unit time as it moves, call it σ\sigma, and any B whose centre falls inside is hit. There are NB/VN_{B}/V of those per unit volume and the NAN_{A} molecules of A are independent, so

v=σ [A][B]  ≡  k [A][B], and in general v=k∏i[Xi]αiv = \sigma\,[A][B] \;\equiv\; k\,[A][B], \qquad\text{ and in general }\qquad \pf{v = k\prod_{i}[X_{i}]^{\alpha_{i}}}(14)

which is the law of mass action. Three things follow that the textbook statement hides. The rate constant is a swept volume per unit time, which is why its units are M−1s−1\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1} and why §8 can compute it. The product form comes from independence, so it fails for partners that are tethered, membrane-confined or not well mixed, and §4 is what licenses independence inside a bacterium. And the exponents are the stoichiometric coefficients because they count how many molecules must meet at once, which is what elementary means. The exposition derives it slowly and dates it to Guldberg and Waage in 1864.

The habit to install now

When anyone writes a rate law, ask: is that arrow a reaction step? If it is, mass action gives the rate for free. If it is an overall reaction, the rate law must be derived (Michaelis-Menten, lecture 6) or measured, never assumed from the shape of the arrowhead. Half the modelling disputes you will meet are this question in disguise.

7Three systems, written both ways

Take a described mechanism and write it down twice, as the overall reaction and as the steps underneath. The three examples get harder in one direction, and the third one stops being writable.

This is the exercise that transfers, so do it at the board rather than read it. Watch what opening an arrow costs and what it buys.

An enzyme, fed and drained. Substrate arrives, is converted, and product leaves. Opening it adds the enzyme and the complex, so two species become four and E+CE + C is conserved. What that buys is every rate law at once, with nothing left to choose. Lecture 6 starts here and reduces it back.

Gene expression. Open transcription once and polymerase binding becomes a genuine step. The next arrow, from bound complex to finished message, is still an overall reaction: promoter escape and a thousand elongation steps are inside it. Opening a squiggle does not always finish the job, and you stop when opening it stops changing your answer. Note the trap while it is in front of you. That first rate is usually written k[G]k[G], and it is right because polymerase is in excess and initiation is rate-limiting, not because of mass action.

A cell divides. As accounting, N⇝2NN \rightsquigarrow 2N. As mechanism, hopeless: the hidden route is an entire cell cycle and nobody writes it. So take the smallest system of the same shape. A ribosome is an enzyme whose product is ribosome, so R⇝2RR \rightsquigarrow 2R is autocatalytic, and it opens into an amino-acid influx, a binding step and a catalytic step. That last arrow compresses every peptide bond in a new ribosome into one event, which is legal only because they run in series with no branching, so the chain behaves as one first-order step.

What the opened arrow predicts

Once R⇝2RR \rightsquigarrow 2R has a mechanism it has a rate, and the rate is a growth rate. An E. coli ribosome carries 1.461.46 MDa of rRNA out of a 2.3 MDa particle, leaving 0.84 MDa of ribosomal protein, about 7000 amino acids. Peptide elongation runs at 16 to 17 aa/s in good growth conditions.21 If every ribosome built nothing but ribosomal protein, then dR/dt=(v/n)RdR/dt = (v/n)R and

t2=nln⁡2/v=7056×0.693/16.5 s−1=297 s≈5 minutest_{2} = n\ln 2 / v = 7056 \times 0.693 / 16.5\,\mathrm{s}^{-1} = 297\,\mathrm{s} \approx \pf{5\text{ minutes}}(15)

The fastest E. coli actually doubles is about 20 minutes. The estimate is not wrong, it is a bound, and the factor of four between them is a prediction: at most a quarter of all translation can be going into ribosomes, and the rest is everything else the cell needs. Measured ribosome-affiliated protein is indeed a few tens of per cent of the proteome and rises linearly with growth rate.22 Lecture 14 is built on that line.

One rule covers all three. An arrow crossing the boundary of the model is always an overall reaction, because nothing enters or leaves a cell without a transporter. Writing ∅→  vin  S\varnothing \xrightarrow{\;v_{\text{in}}\;} S holds the outside world fixed, and that is fine as long as you say so.

Part 5

How fast a reaction can go

One boundary-value problem gives the ceiling. Real enzymes sit five decades under it, and all the reactions in a cell together span ten.

8The diffusion limit, in three lines

A rate constant is a volume swept per second. An encounter has only two ingredients, so there is only one thing that volume can be, and that is most of the answer.

Of the two steps in (13) only the first is physics: how often two molecules meet is diffusion, and diffusion §3 has already given us. So the ceiling on any enzymatic reaction is set by that step alone. This is the anchor number of the course.

Line one, before any physics. Section 6 said a second-order rate constant is a volume swept per unit time. An encounter in a fluid has exactly two ingredients: how fast things diffuse, DD, and how big the target is, aa. There is only one way to build a volume per time out of them.

[D]=L2/T,[a]=L  ⟹  kon∼Da[D] = L^{2}/T, \quad [a] = L \,\,\Longrightarrow\,\, k_{\text{on}} \sim \pf{D a}(16)

That is the whole result up to a number of order one, and it cost nothing. Everything below is finding out that the number is 4π4\pi, and then that the aa cancels out too.

Line two, the gradient. Take the enzyme to be a sphere of radius aa that consumes any substrate touching it. Then C=0C = 0 at its surface and C∞C_{\infty} in the bulk. Over what distance does it climb? There is no other length in the problem, so the depletion zone is about one radius thick, and the gradient at the surface is C∞/aC_{\infty}/a.

Line three, Fick and the area. A gradient times DD is a flux density, and a flux density times the surface area is molecules per second:

j≈D C∞a  ⟹  J=j⋅4πa2=4πDa C∞ ≡ konC∞j \approx D\,\frac{C_{\infty}}{a} \,\,\Longrightarrow\,\, J = j\cdot 4\pi a^{2} = \pf{4\pi D a\, C_{\infty}} \,\equiv\, k_{\text{on}}C_{\infty}(17)

The one guess in that chain turns out to be exact. Solving the steady diffusion equation on this geometry gives C(R)=C∞(1−a/R)C(R) = C_{\infty}(1 - a/R), whose surface gradient is C∞/aC_{\infty}/a on the nose. Line two was not an approximation, and the depletion well really is one radius thick. The exposition does the boundary-value problem every step.

Now watch the size cancel too. A real pair both move and meet at contact, so use DA+DBD_{A}+D_{B} and aA+aBa_{A}+a_{B}. Put Stokes-Einstein (9) into (17):

kon=4π ⁣(kBT6πη) ⁣ ⁣(1aA+1aB) ⁣(aA+aB)=2kBT3η ⁣(2+r+1r),r=aAaBk_{\text{on}} = 4\pi\!\left(\tfrac{k_{B}T}{6\pi \eta}\right)\!\!\left(\tfrac{1}{a_{A}}+\tfrac{1}{a_{B}}\right)\!(a_{A}+a_{B}) = \tfrac{2k_{B}T}{3\eta}\!\left(2 + r + \tfrac{1}{r}\right), \quad r = \tfrac{a_{A}}{a_{B}}(18)
The bound

Only the ratio of the two sizes survives. For two equal spheres of any size the bracket is exactly 4 and

konequal=8kBT3η=7.4×109 M−1s−1in water at 25 ∘Ck_{\text{on}}^{\text{equal}} = \frac{8k_{B}T}{3\eta} = \pf{7.4\times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}} \quad\text{in water at }25\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}(19)

Across every pair a cell contains, from two proteins to an enzyme catching a proton, the bracket spans one decade. So “the diffusion limit” really is one number, 10910^{9} to 1010 M−1s−110^{10}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}, and no unguided bimolecular reaction in solution beats it.

In cytoplasm both partners are slowed elevenfold, so the in-cell ceiling is a decade lower. Now look back at (16). Put in D⋅a=0.245 μm3/sD\cdot a = 0.245\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s} and it reads 1.5×108 M−1s−11.5\times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}, low by 50. That 50 is the entire content of the physics, and it factors: 4π4\pi from the geometry, and 4 more because both partners move. The units fixed the form and got within two decades. Everything after was a shape.

9What enzymes actually do

One enzyme gets to two thirds of its own bound. The median enzyme is five decades below it.

Real enzymes must do more than collide: the substrate has to reach a small active-site patch in the right orientation, which costs up to three orders of magnitude.7 Two known ways around it, and both change the geometry of the search rather than the chemistry.

The two exceptions, and the folklore around them

Steering. Superoxide dismutase acts on a tiny charged ion and funnels it electrostatically. An interface mutant reaches 1.7×1010 M−1s−11.7\times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}, the fastest rate ever measured for any enzyme, which is two thirds of the bound (18) gives for that pair.7 Steering is switchable: a colicin nuclease and its immunity protein associate at 101010^{10} at low salt and 10710^{7} at high.8

A lower-dimensional search. The lac repressor was measured in 1970 finding its operator a hundred times faster than the 10810^{8} bound for a target that small, and the resolution is facilitated diffusion: bind DNA anywhere, then slide in one dimension. The signature is that more DNA around a site makes it easier to find. But correct the story while telling it: Halford and Marko went through the record and most measured protein-DNA associations sit within a factor of three of 10810^{8}.8 The bound is sound, a few systems beat it, and “DNA-binding proteins are all impossibly fast” is folklore.

Now the other side

At 1 mM substrate an enzyme at the diffusion limit would see 10710^{7} encounters per second. Enzymes do not. Bar-Even and colleagues collected kinetic parameters for thousands of pairs and reported the distribution, and it should be uncomfortable.9

Part 6

What the gap buys

The six decades between binding and catalysis are not waste. They are the same quantity as the equilibration of binding, and that is what every model in this course is quoting.

11Slow is the same thing as equilibrated

Two lines of algebra on Briggs and Haldane turn the last three sections into one statement. This is the sentence to leave the room with.

Briggs and Haldane replaced Michaelis and Menten's equilibrium assumption with a steady state on the complex. Two pages, 1925, and the expression this lecture needs:10

kcatKM=k1k2k−1+k2  ⟹  kcat/KMk1=k2k−1+k2\frac{k_{\mathrm{cat}}}{K_{M}} = \frac{k_{1}k_{2}}{k_{-1}+k_{2}} \,\,\Longrightarrow\,\, \frac{k_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_{M}}{k_{1}} = \frac{k_{2}}{k_{-1}+k_{2}}(20)

Read both sides. The left is what §9 measured: how much of the ceiling this enzyme uses. The right is the probability that a complex, once formed, goes on to product rather than falling apart. They are the same number. Invert it:

k−1k2=k1kcat/KM−1=binding equilibrations per catalytic turnover\frac{k_{-1}}{k_{2}} = \frac{k_{1}}{k_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_{M}} - 1 = \pf{\text{binding equilibrations per catalytic turnover}}(21)
The number the whole course rests on, and where it comes from

Put the medians into (21). With kcat/KM=105k_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_{M} = 10^{5} and the ceiling at 1.4×10101.4\times 10^{10}, the complex forms and falls apart 1.4×1051.4\times 10^{5} times per molecule of product made. So when you write a model in which the bound fraction is an equilibrium expression and the flux is a rate, you are not making a convenient assumption. You are quoting a measurement, and Figure 10 is where it was measured.

An enzyme cannot be both fast and modellable. Being near the ceiling and having a binding step at equilibrium are the same quantity, by (20), with no biology in it. A cell whose enzymes had been driven to the ceiling would have lost the equilibrium its regulation is written in.

Two reductions are in play and they need different things. Treating the bound fraction as an equilibrium needs k−1/k2≫1k_{-1}/k_{2} \gg 1, which is (21). Setting dC/dt=0dC/dt = 0 needs the small parameter ε=ET/(KM+S0)\varepsilon = E_{T}/(K_{M}+S_{0}), which is Segel and Slemrod's and not the textbook's ET≪S0E_{T} \ll S_{0}.14 Compute ε\varepsilon, not the ratio.

Two caveats. A transcription factor at 10 nM on an operator with KD=1 nMK_{D} = 1\,\mathrm{nM} relaxes in tens of milliseconds, comparable to a turnover, which is why lecture 6's telegraph model exists. And in lower glycolysis the pools sit near KMK_{M}, every reaction close to equilibrium.3 The separation is a setting, not a fact of chemistry, and a cell that needs reversible flux gives it up on purpose.

12Metabolism cannot wait for gene expression

The same conclusion from the other end of Table 5, using nothing but two timescales. One side is a measurement and the other is a derivation.

Forget rate constants and ask a physiological question. Can a cell control its metabolism by changing how much enzyme it makes? Turnover is pool over flux, and E. coli on glucose takes up 8 mmol per gram dry weight per hour into 3.0 mL of cell, so J=0.73 mM/sJ = 0.73\,\mathrm{mM/s}.

Now the other side. For a protein that is not actively degraded, in a cell growing at rate μ\mu, dilution is the only loss term:

A derivation, not a citation, and the most under-appreciated fact in gene regulation. A cell cannot change a stable protein's concentration faster than it can grow. Transcription does not help: 80% of E. coli mRNA half-lives fall between 3 and 8 minutes.15

The conclusion, and it is forced

Gene expression responds in 2400 seconds. The PEP pool empties in 0.12 seconds. Expression is 2×1042\times 10^{4} times too slow to control it, and still 119 times too slow for the slowest pool in the table. A cell regulating metabolism only through expression would run its pools to zero or overflow them thousands of times before the first new enzyme arrived. So control must live in something that moves on the metabolite timescale. Redo it for a mammalian cell and it gets worse, not better: same pools, a 24-hour doubling time, a factor of 10510^{5}.

Say exactly what that establishes, because the usual next sentence overshoots. The argument forces a fast layer. It does not say that layer is allostery, and the measurement says it mostly is not.

Two experiments, and they disagree with the slogan

The fast layer exists. Link, Kochanowski and Sauer switched E. coli between gluconeogenic and glycolytic carbon every 30 seconds and watched the flux reverse.16 Their own justification is (22): transcription cannot influence fluxes in under 30 seconds, so everything they saw was kinetics and binding. The timescale argument is being used as an experimental control.

But the lever is the substrate. Hackett and colleagues measured enzymes, metabolites and fluxes across 25 steady-state yeast cultures and partitioned the flux variability. Metabolites beat enzymes by more than two to one, as the argument requires. But the metabolite carrying the leverage is mostly the substrate, acting through the ordinary rate law, and for reactions that change net direction allostery contributes nothing at all.17 Its share rises only on strongly forward-driven steps.

The repair makes the argument better. Substrate concentration is itself a fast variable, turning over in about a second by Table 6, so it sits inside the gap. Control through the ordinary rate law is the default, and it is free: no apparatus, only an unsaturated enzyme. Allostery is the expensive option, bought on the irreversible committed steps, which is where Umbarger found it.18

1956, and what it took to see this

Umbarger showed isoleucine inhibiting the first enzyme of its own pathway, and Yates and Pardee showed the same for pyrimidines.18 Neither product resembles the substrate of the enzyme it inhibits, so the inhibition cannot be competitive, and Gerhart and Pardee then showed the regulatory site is physically separable from the catalytic one. Monod, Wyman and Changeux named it allostery in 1965 and gave it a two-state equilibrium model.19 That model is licensed by §11, and lecture 7 derives it.
