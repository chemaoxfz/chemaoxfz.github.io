Calculate dynamics from reaction orders

A worked route from concentration responses to stability, adaptation and regime validity.

CCBS · Lecture 11 · lecturer exposition · 19 October 2026 · Prepared with Codex assistance

A biochemical mechanism constrains how a rate changes when a concentration changes. Those fractional responses can reveal local dynamics before every uncertain rate constant is measured. To use that information, we must retain the turnover times, the measured output and the concentration conditions under which the response law applies.

This chapter develops the calculations behind the core. The lineage explains why kinetics, biochemical systems theory and metabolic control analysis use related derivatives for different questions. The worked extension asks how an added output-removal process changes adaptation.

Concentrations and model boundary

A bare species symbol, such as XX, is a free concentration. A constituent total, such as qX=X+CXYq_X=X+C_{XY}, includes bound molecules. A complex is named by its constituents. Use independent dynamical totals after eliminating fixed conservation laws. All logarithmic derivatives below are evaluated at positive concentrations and positive rates. Logarithms of dimensional quantities mean logarithms relative to fixed reference units.

Part I

Calculate the local dynamics

Define an experimentally meaningful slope, then place it inside the concentration balances.

1Define the response experiment before its reaction order

We need a number that compares fractional changes in a rate and an input. Let v(q)>0v(q)>0 be a differentiable rate, with all other independent concentrations and parameters held fixed. Its reaction order with respect to q>0q>0 is

Hv←q:=∂log⁡v∂log⁡q=qv∂v∂q.H^{v\leftarrow q}:=\frac{\partial\log v}{\partial\log q}=\frac qv\frac{\partial v}{\partial q}.

A small relative input change δq/q\delta q/q produces the relative rate change δv/v≈Hv←qδq/q\delta v/v\approx H^{v\leftarrow q}\delta q/q. For a monomial v=kqav=kq^a, the reaction order is the exponent aa. For a composite rate, the reaction order can vary with the operating concentration.

A saturating enzyme shows why the operating point matters. Suppose fast binding allocates enzyme between free enzyme EE and complex CESC_{ES}. A reservoir holds the free substrate SS, and the enzyme total qEq_E is fixed. Binding equilibrium gives KdCES=ESK_dC_{ES}=ES and qE=E+CESq_E=E+C_{ES}. Solving these two equations gives

CES=qESKd+S,v=kcatCES=Vmax⁡SKd+S,Vmax⁡=kcatqE.C_{ES}=\frac{q_ES}{K_d+S},\qquad v=k_{\mathrm{cat}}C_{ES}=\frac{V_{\max}S}{K_d+S},\qquad V_{\max}=k_{\mathrm{cat}}q_E.

Here KdK_d has concentration units and kcatk_{\mathrm{cat}} has inverse-time units. Binding equilibrium is the assumed fast closure. A catalytic quasi-steady-state reduction can instead give KM=(k−+kcat)/k+K_M=(k_-+k_{\mathrm{cat}})/k_+. The same algebra does not make these two physical assumptions identical.

Differentiating the saturating rate gives a continuously changing exponent.

Hv←S=1−SKd+S=KdKd+S.H^{v\leftarrow S}=1-\frac{S}{K_d+S}=\frac{K_d}{K_d+S}.

The order approaches one at low substrate and zero at high substrate. At S=KdS=K_d, it equals one half. The local power law matching the rate and slope there is v^=(Vmax⁡/2)(S/Kd)1/2\widehat v=(V_{\max}/2)(S/K_d)^{1/2}. Define the concentration fold change r=S/Kdr=S/K_d. Its relative error is

v^v−1=1+r2r−1.\frac{\widehat v}{v}-1=\frac{1+r}{2\sqrt r}-1.

This error is about 6.1% at a twofold substrate change and 73.9% at a tenfold change. A local exponent requires a range check before it becomes a finite-change prediction.

Checkpoint: Is a Hill coefficient the same reaction order?

For occupancy θ=Sn/(Kn+Sn)\theta=S^n/(K^n+S^n), the log-odds slope is dlog⁡[θ/(1−θ)]/dlog⁡S=nd\log[\theta/(1-\theta)]/d\log S=n. The occupancy reaction order is dlog⁡θ/dlog⁡S=n(1−θ)d\log\theta/d\log S=n(1-\theta). The observables differ. At half occupancy, the second slope is n/2n/2.

Specify the varied concentration, held-fixed quantities, positive readout and operating state before calculating a reaction order.

2Combine positive fluxes before subtracting addition and removal

A pool can receive several reaction fluxes with different concentration responses. Let f+=∑jcjvjf^+=\sum_j c_jv_j, where every vj>0v_j>0 and the fixed coefficients cj>0c_j>0 include stoichiometric multiplicities. Differentiation gives

Hf+←qi=∑jωjHvj←qi,ωj=cjvj∑ℓcℓvℓ,∑jωj=1.H^{f^+\leftarrow q_i}=\sum_j\omega_jH^{v_j\leftarrow q_i},\qquad\omega_j=\frac{c_jv_j}{\sum_\ell c_\ell v_\ell},\qquad\sum_j\omega_j=1.

The aggregate reaction order is a flux-weighted average. A change in concentration can change those weights even if every component exponent is constant.3 For f+=a+bq2f^+=a+bq^2, the order is 2bq2/(a+bq2)2bq^2/(a+bq^2), rather than always two. Constant basal addition dilutes the influence of the regulated contribution.

Apply this rule separately to addition and removal. Write q˙i=fi+−fi−\dot q_i=f_i^+-f_i^-, with both aggregate rates positive near the state of interest. At a steady state, their difference is zero. Taking the logarithm of that net difference would therefore be undefined. The useful objects are the addition-order and removal-order matrices,

Hij+=∂log⁡fi+∂log⁡qj,Hij−=∂log⁡fi−∂log⁡qj,H=H+−H−.H^+_{ij}=\frac{\partial\log f_i^+}{\partial\log q_j},\qquad H^-_{ij}=\frac{\partial\log f_i^-}{\partial\log q_j},\qquad H=H^+-H^-.

The matrix HH is the balance-order matrix. It compares how addition and removal respond to the same small relative perturbation. A positive entry is a positive influence on the local balance. It need not represent one elementary chemical reaction.1

Checkpoint: What changes when an extra removal channel is added?

For f−=kqXqY+δqXf^-=kq_Xq_Y+\delta q_X, define the catalytic removal fraction ω=kqXqY/f−\omega=kq_Xq_Y/f^-. Then Hf−←qX=1H^{f^-\leftarrow q_X}=1 and Hf−←qY=ωH^{f^-\leftarrow q_Y}=\omega. The controller's removal order falls below one as the constitutive channel becomes appreciable. The catalytic term's own exponents remain unchanged.

Weight component orders by positive flux fractions, then subtract the aggregate removal orders from the aggregate addition orders.

3Expose the turnover times in the ordinary Jacobian

To predict return after a perturbation, connect the balance-order matrix to ordinary linearization. Choose a positive steady state q∗q^* with positive balanced fluxes fi+(q∗)=fi−(q∗)=fi∗f_i^+(q^*)=f_i^-(q^*)=f_i^*. All orders in this section are evaluated there. Taylor expansion in the absolute displacement δq=q−q∗\delta q=q-q^* gives δq˙=Aδq\dot{\delta q}=A\delta q, where

Aij=∂(fi+−fi−)∂qj∣q∗=fi∗qj∗(Hij+−Hij−)=fi∗qj∗Hij.A_{ij}=\left.\frac{\partial(f_i^+-f_i^-)}{\partial q_j}\right|_{q^*}=\frac{f_i^*}{q_j^*}(H^+_{ij}-H^-_{ij})=\frac{f_i^*}{q_j^*}H_{ij}.

Relative coordinates cancel the concentration scale in each column. Define zqi=δqi/qi∗z_{q_i}=\delta q_i/q_i^*. Substituting δqj=qj∗zqj\delta q_j=q_j^*z_{q_j} and dividing each equation by qi∗q_i^* yields

z˙qi=fi∗qi∗∑jHijzqj.\dot z_{q_i}=\frac{f_i^*}{q_i^*}\sum_jH_{ij}z_{q_j}.

The turnover time τi∗=qi∗/fi∗\tau_i^*=q_i^*/f_i^* is the time needed for the steady flux to replace the pool. It is not automatically a relaxation time. Write Λa=diag⁡(a)\Lambda_a=\operatorname{diag}(a) for a diagonal matrix. Then

z˙q=Λτ∗−1Hzq,A=Λq∗Λτ∗−1HΛq∗−1.\dot z_q=\Lambda_{\tau^*}^{-1}Hz_q,\qquad A=\Lambda_{q^*}\Lambda_{\tau^*}^{-1}H\Lambda_{q^*}^{-1}.

The two matrices have the same eigenvalues because they are similar. The ordinary Jacobian and the turnover-scaled balance-order matrix describe the same linear dynamics in different coordinates. A matrix is Hurwitz when every eigenvalue has negative real part. A Hurwitz Jacobian establishes local asymptotic stability. Eigenvalues on the imaginary axis require additional analysis.1

The dimensions also check the calculation: the balance-order matrix is dimensionless, so it must multiply reciprocal turnover times. Fixed conservation constraints must be eliminated before seeking a nonsingular state block. A conserved total otherwise supplies a zero mode that belongs to the coordinate choice.

Calculate local stability from Λτ∗−1H\Lambda_{\tau^*}^{-1}H, using independent pools and positive balanced fluxes.

4Read equilibrium, stability and speed from a scalar balance

A scalar balance separates three questions that can otherwise become conflated. Take q˙=μ−λq\dot q=\mu-\lambda q, with constant addition μ>0\mu>0 and removal frequency λ>0\lambda>0. The rate intersection gives q∗=μ/λq^*=\mu/\lambda. The addition and removal orders give H=0−1=−1H=0-1=-1. The turnover time is τ∗=1/λ\tau^*=1/\lambda.

z˙q=−zqτ∗,zq(t)=zq(0)e−t/τ∗.\dot z_q=-\frac{z_q}{\tau^*},\qquad z_q(t)=z_q(0)e^{-t/\tau^*}.

A negative balance order gives local return in any one-pool model. In general, the scalar eigenvalue is H/τ∗H/\tau^*. Since the turnover time is positive, its sign comes from the balance order. If H<0H<0, the linear relaxation time is τ∗/∣H∣\tau^*/|H|. A small negative balance order can therefore produce slow relaxation despite rapid turnover.

A zero balance order marks a loss of information in this first-order test. Near an autoactivation fold, addition and removal can have equal slopes. The linear eigenvalue then vanishes. Quadratic or higher terms determine the motion, and the steady-state branch may end. The positive turnover time cannot resolve that degeneracy.

Locate the equilibrium from flux balance, read scalar stability from the balance-order sign, and obtain the return time from both order and turnover.

Part II

Test return and adaptation separately

A stable state can still shift after a persistent input. First test return, then test what the new state does to the measured output.

5Certify stability across constant positive turnover times

For several pools, stability can depend on their relative clocks. The illustrative balance-order matrix H=(12−2−3)H=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\-2&-3\end{pmatrix} has trace −2-2 and determinant one. It is Hurwitz. Multiplying its first row by four gives trace one and determinant four, so the new matrix is unstable. Positive row scaling does not generally preserve stability.

D-stability names the stronger property we want. A real matrix HH is D-stable if DHDH is Hurwitz for every constant positive diagonal matrix DD. Here D=Λτ∗−1D=\Lambda_{\tau^*}^{-1}. The definition holds the balance-order matrix fixed while varying the turnover times.

A diagonal quadratic certificate supplies a sufficient test. Find a diagonal matrix PP whose entries are positive and which satisfies

PH+HTP≺0.PH+H^{\mathsf T}P\prec0.

The inequality means that every nonzero real vector has a negative quadratic form. Existence of such a matrix is called diagonal stability. To show why it suffices, use V=zqTD−1PzqV=z_q^{\mathsf T}D^{-1}Pz_q. Positive diagonal matrices commute, so

V˙=zqT(HTP+PH)zq<0(zq≠0).\dot V=z_q^{\mathsf T}\bigl(H^{\mathsf T}P+PH\bigr)z_q<0\qquad(z_q\ne0).

Each constant positive clock choice therefore has a Lyapunov function. Diagonal stability implies D-stability. The converse does not hold in general. A failed search for a diagonal certificate is not proof of instability.1

Structural instability has its own sufficient certificate. Xiao, Khammash and Doyle's Theorem 7 assumes that HH has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis. If a real diagonal PP with at least one negative entry satisfies the same strict inequality, every positive turnover scaling is unstable. The reason is the Lyapunov inertia theorem: a real symmetric matrix satisfying a strict negative Lyapunov inequality has as many negative eigenvalues as the dynamics has eigenvalues with positive real part. For diagonal PP, the matrix D−1PD^{-1}P stays symmetric and keeps those signs for every positive diagonal DD.1

The diagonal entries can rule out this certificate before any numerical search. If Hii=−1H_{ii}=-1 for every species, then (PH+HTP)ii=−2pi<0(PH+H^{\mathsf T}P)_{ii}=-2p_i\lt0 forces every pi>0p_i>0. No diagonal certificate with a negative entry can exist. This does not establish stability. A calculation for the actual turnover times may still show instability.

The certificate has a local and constant-clock scope. Its weights include the chosen turnover times. Arbitrary time-varying clocks would add derivative terms to this calculation. A nonlinear trajectory can also change the balance-order matrix. Neither arbitrary clock switching nor global nonlinear stability follows from the displayed proof.

Use a positive diagonal certificate to establish local stability for every constant positive turnover vector at the stated balance-order matrix.

6Work through a six-pool circuit without solving a sixth-degree polynomial

A larger example shows what the certificate saves. Consider six independent totals with cyclic indices and effective monomial balances

q˙i=aiqi−1−biqiqi+1,i=1,…,6,q0=q6,q7=q1.\dot q_i=a_iq_{i-1}-b_iq_iq_{i+1},\qquad i=1,\ldots,6,\qquad q_0=q_6,\quad q_7=q_1.

The coefficients aia_i have inverse-time units. The coefficients bib_i have inverse-concentration per time units. These effective laws specify an illustrative concentration regime. They do not identify six elementary chemical reactions.

Read each influence from the exponents. Addition has order one in the predecessor. Removal has order one in the same pool and its successor. Define the cyclic matrix RR by (Rzq)i=zqi−1(Rz_q)_i=z_{q_{i-1}}. Then

H=−I+R−RT,H+HT=−2I.H=-I+R-R^{\mathsf T},\qquad H+H^{\mathsf T}=-2I.

The positive and negative cross influences cancel in the symmetric part. Taking P=IP=I gives the certificate. Every positive equilibrium is locally stable for every positive turnover-time vector.

The steady balance also supplies a unique positive candidate. Take a reference concentration qrefq_{\mathrm{ref}}. Dividing each steady equation by its removal term and taking logarithms gives

Hlog⁡(q∗/qref)=(log⁡biqrefai)i=16.H\log(q^*/q_{\mathrm{ref}})=\left(\log\frac{b_iq_{\mathrm{ref}}}{a_i}\right)_{i=1}^6.

The certificate makes HH nonsingular. Solve this linear system and exponentiate. The result is positive. If the monomial laws arose from a binding approximation, substitute that result into the binding-regime inequalities before claiming a state of the molecular system.

Checkpoint: Can arbitrary positive pool values and turnover times be realized in this monomial model?

Yes. Choose fi∗=qi∗/τi∗f_i^*=q_i^*/\tau_i^*, then set ai=fi∗/qi−1∗a_i=f_i^*/q_{i-1}^* and bi=fi∗/(qi∗qi+1∗)b_i=f_i^*/(q_i^*q_{i+1}^*). The chosen pools satisfy every balance and have the requested turnover times. This construction belongs to the monomial model. A biochemical implementation imposes additional feasibility conditions.

Find a simple symmetric-part certificate first, then solve the monomial balances and check the biochemical regime separately.

7Calculate the settled response to a persistent input

Adaptation is a claim about a chosen output after a chosen disturbance. Let a positive scalar input ww enter the balances q˙=f+(q,w)−f−(q,w)\dot q=f^+(q,w)-f^-(q,w). Let the positive scalar readout be y=h(q,w)y=h(q,w). At a positive steady state define

HA=∂log⁡qlog⁡f+−∂log⁡qlog⁡f−,Hb=∂log⁡wlog⁡f+−∂log⁡wlog⁡f−,Hc=∂log⁡qlog⁡h,Hd=∂log⁡wlog⁡h.\begin{aligned}H^A&=\partial_{\log q}\log f^+-\partial_{\log q}\log f^-,&H^b&=\partial_{\log w}\log f^+-\partial_{\log w}\log f^-,\\H^c&=\partial_{\log q}\log h,&H^d&=\partial_{\log w}\log h.\end{aligned}

The state block HAH^A is the balance-order matrix. The input block is a column, the state-readout block is a row, and the direct input-readout block is a scalar. Define zw=δw/w∗z_w=\delta w/w^* and zy=δy/y∗z_y=\delta y/y^*. The relative linearization is

z˙q=Λτ∗−1(HAzq+Hbzw),zy=Hczq+Hdzw.\dot z_q=\Lambda_{\tau^*}^{-1}(H^Az_q+H^bz_w),\qquad z_y=H^cz_q+H^dz_w.

At the new steady state, the turnover matrix cancels. If HAH^A is nonsingular, solve HAzq∗+Hbzw=0H^Az_q^*+H^bz_w=0 and insert the solution into the readout:

Gyw:=dlog⁡y∗dlog⁡w=Hd−Hc(HA)−1Hb.G_{yw}:=\frac{d\log y^*}{d\log w}=H^d-H^c(H^A)^{-1}H^b.

The state dynamics must also be stable for a nearby trajectory to settle. For one scalar input and one scalar output, the block determinant identity gives

det⁡(HAHbHcHd)=det⁡(HA)Gyw.\det\begin{pmatrix}H^A&H^b\\H^c&H^d\end{pmatrix}=\det(H^A)G_{yw}.

Zero determinant is therefore a cancellation test with a nonsingular state block. With several inputs or outputs, test the entire gain matrix. One determinant cannot certify cancellation of every channel.1

A bilinear removal circuit provides a complete example. Take a dimensionless disturbance w>0w>0, output y=q1y=q_1, and

q˙1=k1w−δq1q2,q˙2=k2w−γq2.\dot q_1=k_1w-\delta q_1q_2,\qquad\dot q_2=k_2w-\gamma q_2.

The coefficients k1,k2k_1,k_2 have concentration-per-time units, δ\delta has inverse-concentration per time units, and γ\gamma is a removal frequency. The second pool increases removal of the first. The order blocks are

HA=(−1−10−1),Hb=(11),Hc=(10),Hd=0.H^A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad H^b=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad H^c=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad H^d=0.

The triangular state block is stable for every positive row scaling. Solving HAzq∗=−HbzwH^Az_q^*=-H^bz_w gives zq2∗=zwz_{q_2}^*=z_w and zq1∗=0z_{q_1}^*=0. The output has zero local steady gain.

The exact balances strengthen that local result to an interval result.

q2∗=k2γw,q1∗=k1γδk2.q_2^*=\frac{k_2}{\gamma}w,\qquad q_1^*=\frac{k_1\gamma}{\delta k_2}.

The set point is constant for every positive input for which the bilinear model applies. Zero derivative at one isolated input would not establish that conclusion. Saturation, sequestration or another removal channel can change the order blocks and the feasible branch.

Check stable settling and input-output cancellation separately, then prove a constant equilibrium branch before claiming finite-range adaptation.

8Resolve free and total output before testing adaptation

Binding can make two measurements of the same circuit adapt differently. Input u>0u>0 produces an output component XX and a controller component YY. Fast reversible binding forms CXYC_{XY}. Catalytic conversion removes bound output and releases the controller. Controller removal acts on every controller-containing state and releases any bound output intact. These molecular fates give

q˙X=kXu−kcatCXY,q˙Y=kYu−γqY,KdCXY=XY,\dot q_X=k_Xu-k_{\mathrm{cat}}C_{XY},\qquad\dot q_Y=k_Yu-\gamma q_Y,\qquad K_dC_{XY}=XY,
qX=X+CXY,qY=Y+CXY.q_X=X+C_{XY},\qquad q_Y=Y+C_{XY}.

The input is dimensionless. The production coefficients kX,kYk_X,k_Y are concentration fluxes. The frequencies kcat,γk_{\mathrm{cat}},\gamma are positive. Fast binding equilibrium is an assumption on the observation clock.

The steady balances determine the complex and controller before the free output.

CXY∗=kXkcatu,qY∗=kYγu,Y∗=(kYγ−kXkcat)u.C_{XY}^*=\frac{k_X}{k_{\mathrm{cat}}}u,\qquad q_Y^*=\frac{k_Y}{\gamma}u,\qquad Y^*=\left(\frac{k_Y}{\gamma}-\frac{k_X}{k_{\mathrm{cat}}}\right)u.

A finite positive free controller requires kY/γ>kX/kcatk_Y/\gamma>k_X/k_{\mathrm{cat}}. Binding equilibrium then gives

X∗=KdkX/kcatkY/γ−kX/kcat,qX∗=X∗+kXkcatu.X^*=K_d\frac{k_X/k_{\mathrm{cat}}}{k_Y/\gamma-k_X/k_{\mathrm{cat}}},\qquad q_X^*=X^*+\frac{k_X}{k_{\mathrm{cat}}}u.

The free output is constant. The total output contains an input-dependent bound pool. This difference is exact in the stated equilibrated-binding model. It does not require weak binding.

Local stability follows directly from the binding derivatives. Define D=Kd+X+YD=K_d+X+Y. Differentiating binding and the two totals gives ∂CXY/∂qX=Y/D\partial C_{XY}/\partial q_X=Y/D and ∂CXY/∂qY=X/D\partial C_{XY}/\partial q_Y=X/D. Hence

A=(−kcatY∗/D∗−kcatX∗/D∗0−γ).A=\begin{pmatrix}-k_{\mathrm{cat}}Y^*/D^*&-k_{\mathrm{cat}}X^*/D^*\\0&-\gamma\end{pmatrix}.

Both eigenvalues are negative on the positive feasible branch. The output that a small settled input step measures is therefore decisive. Its gains are

GXu=0,GqXu=CXY∗X∗+CXY∗=Y∗Kd+Y∗.G_{Xu}=0,\qquad G_{q_Xu}=\frac{C_{XY}^*}{X^*+C_{XY}^*}=\frac{Y^*}{K_d+Y^*}.

The same model also supplies a global convergence argument for constant input. The controller total converges exponentially to its prescribed positive value. With that total fixed above the required complex level, output addition minus removal is strictly decreasing in qXq_X, starts positive at zero, and tends to a negative limit. Its unique scalar equilibrium attracts. Once the actual controller total lies between two nearby fixed values, those scalar systems bound the output trajectory. Letting the bounds approach the limiting controller total proves convergence to the stated equilibrium. This argument uses the capacity inequality and the declared molecular fates.

Derive the equilibrium for the actual measured species. A free-output adaptation claim need not survive a change to total-output measurement.

Part III

Check the global claim against the full dynamics

Local matrices are evaluated at states. Before using them to infer a switch or an oscillation, establish where those states and trajectories can occur.

9Substitute each candidate into its own regime conditions

A leading rate law is useful only where its neglected terms are small. Consider an autoactivating protein with cooperative promoter occupancy, negligible sequestration by the promoter, basal addition μ\mu, maximal induced addition Vmax⁡V_{\max}, threshold KK and linear removal λq\lambda q. The effective smooth law is

q˙=μ+Vmax⁡q2K2+q2−λq.\dot q=\mu+V_{\max}\frac{q^2}{K^2+q^2}-\lambda q.

The Hill law here assumes that free and doubly occupied promoter states dominate, with occupancy ratio (Q/K)2(Q/K)^2. Replacing free protein QQ by total qq also requires negligible promoter-bound protein. These are mechanism assumptions that precede the following regime approximation.

Choose units that retain the physical pool. Set qˉ=q/K\bar q=q/K, tˉ=Vmax⁡t/K\bar t=V_{\max}t/K, μˉ=μ/Vmax⁡\bar\mu=\mu/V_{\max} and λˉ=λK/Vmax⁡\bar\lambda=\lambda K/V_{\max}. Then

10Test an oscillatory prediction with the actual vector field

A virtual target identifies where a continued regime model points. It is a fixed point of a leading approximation that lies outside that approximation's own validity region. Such targets can organize a sequence of regime transitions. They do not determine the first boundary exit or prove that the full system has an attracting periodic orbit.2

The three-node repression ring provides a direct test. Assume the same effective cooperative occupancy and negligible sequestration as above. Use a common removal frequency λ\lambda and binding scale KK. With qˉi=qi/K\bar q_i=q_i/K, tˉ=λt\bar t=\lambda t and α=Vmax⁡/(λK)\alpha=V_{\max}/(\lambda K), take cyclic indices in

dqˉidtˉ=α1+(qˉi−1)n−qˉi.\frac{d\bar q_i}{d\bar t}=\frac{\alpha}{1+(\bar q_{i-1})^n}-\bar q_i.

Calculate the local instability threshold before interpreting a regime loop. At the symmetric equilibrium qˉi=s\bar q_i=s, the balance is α=s(1+sn)\alpha=s(1+s^n). The magnitude of the local repression slope is

g=αnsn−1(1+sn)2=nsn1+sn.g=\frac{\alpha n s^{n-1}}{(1+s^n)^2}=\frac{n s^n}{1+s^n}.

13Apply the method to a new model and choose a discriminating experiment

A new circuit requires a sequence of checks with different purposes. Start with the molecular states, independent totals, input and measured output. State which fast subsystem was eliminated and the clock on which its closure applies. Obtain the binding responses, then weight them by their contributions to each positive addition and removal flux.

The local prediction needs both structure and an operating state. Find a feasible positive equilibrium. Calculate its balance-order matrix and turnover times. Test the turnover-scaled Jacobian, or use a sufficient diagonal certificate when the clocks are uncertain. For adaptation, calculate the readout blocks and the settled gain. If the claim concerns a range of inputs, follow the feasible attracting equilibrium branch.

The most useful experiment changes quantities that candidate mechanisms treat differently. Compare total-enzyme addition with an activity change whose binding effects are independently checked. Measure free and bound pools where possible. Small perturbations estimate local orders. Finite steps test range validity. Trajectories and repeated perturbations test convergence, basin boundaries and memory.

The molecular implementation poses the next question. Constitutive removal can act on free output, bound output or both. The worked extension follows a declared process that removes both forms and releases the bound partner. A useful experiment measures the two pools and asks whether the observed loss of adaptation follows the predicted change in removal fractions.

Build the prediction from a specified intervention and readout, then choose the smallest perturbation or pool measurement that can distinguish the competing mechanisms.
