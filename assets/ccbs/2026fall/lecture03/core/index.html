Reaction networks, with the central dogma as the worked example

The one law that turns arrows into equations, and then gene expression as the example that introduces lifetimes, Hill regulation and growth dilution.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · lecture 3 · core page · 2026-09-08 (Tue) 10:40–12:15 · students present, lecturer-backed

A cell is a soup of reactions. This lecture writes that soup as a dynamical system: mass action gives the rates, stoichiometry gives the bookkeeping, and the result is one matrix equation. The central dogma is the worked example throughout, because it is where the abstract machinery meets numbers you can look up: transcription and translation rates, mRNA and protein lifetimes, Hill functions for regulation, and the dilution term that a constant exponential growth rate contributes to every species in the cell.

Still to be written into this core page

This lecture is new in 2026 or substantially re-scoped, so parts of it are not yet carried over from the 2025 notes. The teaching team should treat this list as the commissioning brief, and the lecturer as the backlog.

  • The central dogma as the running example: transcription and translation rates, mRNA and protein copy numbers, and the two-stage production model.
  • Lifetime and the degradation rate: why 1/gamma is the only timescale a first-order species has.
  • Growth dilution derived: a cell growing exponentially at rate mu contributes a mu*x dilution term to every species, so the effective lifetime is 1/(gamma+mu). Stable proteins in fast-growing cells are diluted, not degraded.
  • The Hill function for regulation, presented as a summary of a binding equilibrium rather than a fitted curve.
Part 1

Mass action

The one law that turns arrows into equations, and the accounting that holds even when you know nothing about rates.

1A generic reaction, and the accounting

γ = β − α; every time a reaction fires it changes species j by γⱼ·v.

Write a generic reaction — elementary (→) or composite (⇝), n species, reactants αⱼ, products βⱼ:

α₁X₁ + … + αₙXₙ → β₁X₁ + … + βₙXₙ,   γⱼ = βⱼ − αⱼ (7)

The net-stoichiometry γⱼ is the only thing the accounting needs. Every time the reaction fires with flux v, species j changes by γⱼ·v. This is the net-change notation of lecture 2 made algebraic.

The accounting statement (say it out loud)

The equation of a chemical network is pure bookkeeping: where molecules went. It holds always, for elementary and composite reactions alike, and it depends only on γ — not on α, not on β. You can write it even if you know nothing about the mechanism. What you cannot do without kinetics is close the system: that is exactly what the next section supplies.

2The law of mass action

For an elementary reaction, the flux is k times the product of reactant concentrations raised to their stoichiometries.

Kinetics supplies the flux. For an elementary reaction, the law that organizes all reaction kinetics:

v(x) = k · X₁α₁ X₂α₂ ··· Xₙαₙ = k xα (8)

The rationale is the collision picture: the rate is proportional to how often reactants meet, which scales as the product of their densities. Example from the scribe: E + S → C with rate constant K has rate = K·E·S — and if the step is the elementary binding of an association-limited reaction, then that K is exactly kon ≈ 4πDa from lecture 2: the same quantity, one derived from physics, one assumed from the arrow. That agreement is the reason the course calls (5) an anchor: the law of mass action's constants are not free — lecture 2's physics caps them.

3The CRN rate equation

dx/dt = Γv = f⁺ − f⁻: a network's dynamics, split into production and consumption — the form every later week starts from.

Combine accounting (7) with kinetics (8). For m reactions, n species, the network obeys1

dxⱼ/dt = Σi γij vi = Σi: γ>0 γijvi − Σi: γ<0 |γij| vi = f+j(x) − f−j(x)

in matrix form

dx/dt = Γ v(x) = Γ Λk xα = f+(x) − f−(x) (9)

with Γ the n×m net-stoichiometry matrix and Λk the diagonal matrix of rate constants. Three immediate consequences:

  • Conservation. Any vector c with cTΓ = 0 is conserved; closed networks have such quantities — the accounting's invariant.
  • Steady states. Γv(x*) = 0. Note the split: structure (Γ) and kinetics (v) each constrain the fixed point separately, which is why lecture 11 can say surprising things about what a network's shape alone allows.
  • What (9) is not. It is a proper subset of arbitrary dynamical systems. Positivity (the orthant is forward-invariant), polynomiality and parameter uncertainty are the structural rules lecture 4 encodes as [P], [CP], [PU].
Part 2

Worked models

Three ODEs that carry the course, and the toolkit that solves them.

4Three ODEs that carry the course

Production–degradation, the TXTL circuit, and the S-shaped bistable — every later week is a variation on these.

The 2025 handwritten notes develop the gallery in order:2

ẋ = μ − x (10)

Production–degradation (the scribe's Exp 4). Fixed point x* = μ; relaxation with timescale 1. Nothing else happens — and that "nothing else" is the null model of every gene-expression circuit.

dM/dt = kMG − γMM,   dP/dt = kHM − γPP (11)

The TXTL-style circuit — transcription (with inducible rate) and translation, both diluted/degraded. Two variables, one matrix; its stability analysis is lecture 4's first exercise.

ẋ = f+(x) − x,   f+ an S-shaped (Hill-type) production term (12)

The bistable construction. With an S-shaped production, (12) has three fixed points: two stable, one unstable. This is the toggle switch's skeleton — and lecture 8 will say exactly how the S-shape can arise from a CRN (cooperativity, or the saturation/bottleneck/ultrasensitivity regimes of one binding reaction).

5The simulation toolkit

Deterministic solvers first; the CRN zoo next; Gillespie in lecture 5.

"Let's see" means "let's solve" from here on. Deterministic ODEs (any stiff-aware solver; the 2025 course's scribes and code are published with each lecture) for lectures 3 and 4; the Gillespie algorithm enters in lecture 5 when copy numbers get small. The zoo to keep on hand: birth–death (10), dimerization 2A ⇌ A₂ (the first nonlinearity and a conservation check on (9)), autocatalysis, the enzyme module (1), and the inducible/repressible genes of (11). Every model in the zoo should be written as (9) first: Γ, then v.

6What networks can do

The teaser that opens lecture 4, and the reason this lecture's vocabulary is worth the effort.

With (9) in hand, the course's first big question appears: what can a network do? The CRN equation is a constrained dynamical system, so its behaviors are restricted — but within the restrictions lie bistability, oscillations, adaptation, computation. The lecturer's addendum states the question cleanly: lecture 4 will map what (9) can draw (phase portraits, stability, time-scale separation), lecture 10 will ask how a network adapts, lecture 5 how it behaves when the counts are small, and lecture 12 what it computes. Every one of those questions starts with the same two sentences: write Γ; write v.

Extension mini-essay prompts

One of: (a) estimate + verify the turnover rate of your favorite enzyme against the 10⁹ limit (the derivation of (6) is the reference); (b) derive the rate law of a composite reaction you choose, and show which dominance regime it lives in; (c) build a CRN whose steady state hides a conserved quantity and show (9)'s accounting on it.
