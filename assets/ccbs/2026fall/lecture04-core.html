From reaction networks to possible behaviors

Choose a state, read the vector field globally and locally, then use bifurcations, energy and chemical constraints when a first-order picture is not enough.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · lecture 4 · core page (full) · 2026-09-10 (Thu) 10:40–12:15 · students present, lecturer-backed

Lecture 3 turned binding, catalysis, regulated production, dilution and conserved pools into an autonomous system x˙=F(x)\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}). Now the question changes. What behaviors can that system produce? Which steady states pull the cell back after a perturbation, which repel it, when can two expression states coexist, and when can a trajectory keep moving forever?

The answer is not one long simulation. We will move among four levels of evidence: a trajectory from one initial state, a phase portrait for all initial states, a linear system close to one fixed point, and a global certificate such as an energy or Lyapunov function. Each answers a different question. The lecture works only if those scopes stay separate.

The lecture in one sentence

Choose a state that makes the future depend only on the present, read the resulting vector field with phase lines and nullclines and its local Jacobian, then add bifurcation and Lyapunov arguments where the local test changes or falls silent.

Part 1

Turn motion into geometry

Before drawing arrows, decide what a state is. Then make every view of a scalar system tell the same story.

1State is what makes a future

A state contains enough present information to determine the instantaneous future. If two different velocities are possible at the same plotted point, something is missing from the state.

A spring makes this operational. Let qq be displacement, mm mass, kk the spring constant and cc a viscous-drag coefficient. Force balance gives

mq¨+cq˙+kq=0.m\ddot q+c\dot q+kq=0.(1)

Position qq alone is not a state. The mass can pass the same position moving left or right, and those two presents have different futures. Add velocity v=q˙v=\dot q and define z=(q,v)T\mathbf{z}=(q,v)^{\mathsf T}. Equation (1) becomes two first-order equations:

ddt[qv]=[v−(k/m)q−(c/m)v]=[01−k/m−c/m]⏟A[qv].\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}q\\v\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}v\\-(k/m)q-(c/m)v\end{bmatrix} =\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-k/m&-c/m\end{bmatrix}}_{A} \begin{bmatrix}q\\v\end{bmatrix}.(2)

This conversion is exact. It is not yet a linearization, because the spring model was linear to begin with. More generally, an nnth-order equation becomes nn first-order state equations by retaining the quantity and its first n−1n-1 derivatives. Hidden promoter configurations, delayed intermediates and environmental inputs play the same conceptual role: if they change the next velocity, either include them in the state or state the approximation that removes them.

The state test

Hold the declared state fixed. Can the model assign two different instantaneous velocities because of an omitted history, input or hidden molecular configuration? If yes, the description is not autonomous in that state. Enlarge the state, or write the dependence explicitly as x˙=F(x,t)\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},t).

A phase portrait is the geometry of this first-order system: one velocity vector attached to every admissible state, together with the trajectories those vectors generate. We will return to the spring in section 7. Its state form opens the phase plane; its energy explains the portrait globally.

2One field, three coordinated views

The graph of f(x)f(x), the phase line and the time traces are three views of the same scalar equation. Each view retains information the others compress.

For the autonomous scalar system x˙=f(x)\dot x=f(x), a fixed point is a state x∗x^{*} for which f(x∗)=0f(x^{*})=0. Build the phase line mechanically:

  1. Plot f(x)f(x) against xx and find every zero.
  2. On each interval between consecutive zeros, determine the sign of ff.
  3. Draw rightward motion where f>0f>0 and leftward motion where f<0f<0.
  4. A simple zero is attracting when arrows point toward it and repelling when they point away. Equivalently, f′(x∗)<0f'(x^{*})<0 gives local asymptotic stability and f′(x∗)>0f'(x^{*})>0 gives instability.

Read the first row from left to right. For x˙=−x\dot x=-x, the field crosses zero with negative slope, both phase-line arrows point to the origin, and every trace follows x(t)=x0e−tx(t)=x_{0}e^{-t}. The equilibrium is Lyapunov stable, since a small displacement stays small, and attracting, since it tends to zero. Together those properties make it asymptotically stable.

The second row is the first warning against asking one picture to do every job. For x˙=x2\dot x=x^{2}, the graph touches rather than crosses zero. The origin attracts from the left and repels from the right, so the derivative test is silent because f′(0)=0f'(0)=0. Separating variables gives

x(t)=x01−x0t.x(t)=\frac{x_{0}}{1-x_{0}t}.(3)

For x0>0x_{0}>0, the solution escapes at t=1/x0t=1/x_{0}. The phase line gets the direction right but contains no clock. For x0<0x_{0}<0, the same formula approaches zero without crossing it.

The third row has three roots. The outer two attract, the middle one repels, and the initial condition chooses the final state. Thus one dimension can store a binary memory. What it cannot do is oscillate. Between consecutive roots the sign of ff is fixed, so a nonstationary trajectory is strictly monotone. It cannot return to a state it has left. The correct boundary is: a scalar autonomous system can switch, but it cannot sustain a nonconstant cycle.

Room check, 90 seconds. For x˙=x(1−x)(x−0.2)\dot x=x(1-x)(x-0.2) on x≥0x\ge 0, mark every interval sign, classify all three roots and assign the two basins without differentiating.

3A changing field bifurcates

A bifurcation is a qualitative change in the organization of trajectories as a parameter varies. See the vector fields change before compressing them into a branch diagram.

Use the saddle-node normal form

x˙=f(x;r)=r−x2.\dot x=f(x;r)=r-x^{2}.(4)

Here rr is a control parameter and xx is a local signed coordinate. In a biological application, xx may be the deviation of a concentration from the collision point, so a negative branch does not require a negative molecule count.

  1. Set f=0f=0. For r>0r>0, x∗=±rx^{*}=\pm\sqrt r; at r=0r=0 there is one double root; for r<0r<0 there is no real root.
  2. Differentiate: fx=−2xf_x=-2x. Thus +r+\sqrt r is stable and −r-\sqrt r is unstable.
  3. As r↓0r\downarrow0, those fixed points collide, the number of fixed points changes and the phase line cannot be deformed across the event without creating or destroying a root. That topological change is the bifurcation.

The same derivative predicts a clock. Let u=x−ru=x-\sqrt r measure a small displacement from the stable branch. Keeping its first-order term gives u˙=−2r u\dot u=-2\sqrt r\,u, so

u(t)≈u(0)e−2r t,τrelax=12r.u(t)\approx u(0)e^{-2\sqrt r\,t},\qquad \tau_{\mathrm{relax}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt r}.(5)

At r=1r=1, the relaxation time is 0.5. At r=0.01r=0.01, it is 5. A hundredfold approach in the parameter produces a tenfold slowing. This can be an early-warning observable, but only after noise, sampling interval and the range over which the normal form applies have been checked.4

Part 2

Build a biological portrait

The central two-dimensional example begins as a gene circuit, becomes a reduced rate law and only then becomes geometry.

4The toggle is a reaction network

Mutual repression creates two expression states. The Hill terms are reduced production laws whose hidden assumptions should remain attached to them.

Let gene GXG_X produce repressor XX and gene GYG_Y produce repressor YY. Protein XX represses production from GYG_Y; protein YY represses production from GXG_X. Both proteins are removed by molecular degradation, growth dilution or both. At the composite level the circuit is

GX⇝GX+X,X→∅,GY⇝GY+Y,Y→∅,Y⊣GX,X⊣GY.G_X\rightsquigarrow G_X+X,\quad X\rightarrow\varnothing, \qquad G_Y\rightsquigarrow G_Y+Y,\quad Y\rightarrow\varnothing, \qquad Y\dashv G_X,\quad X\dashv G_Y.(6)

For one arm, suppose repressor-bound promoter and free promoter satisfy the effective binding relation

CGXYnYGX=(YKY)nY,qGX=GX+CGXYnY.\frac{C_{G_XY_{n_Y}}}{G_X}=\left(\frac{Y}{K_Y}\right)^{n_Y},\qquad q_{G_X}=G_X+C_{G_XY_{n_Y}}.(7)

Solving the two equations gives the free fraction

GXqGX=11+(Y/KY)nY.\frac{G_X}{q_{G_X}}=\frac{1}{1+(Y/K_Y)^{n_Y}}.(8)

If promoter redistribution is fast compared with expression, and if the composite protein-production rate is proportional to the free or active promoter fraction, then

X˙=αX1+(Y/KY)nY−δXX,Y˙=αY1+(X/KX)nX−δYY.\dot X=\frac{\alpha_X}{1+(Y/K_Y)^{n_Y}}-\delta_X X,\qquad \dot Y=\frac{\alpha_Y}{1+(X/K_X)^{n_X}}-\delta_Y Y.(9)

Equation (9) is not an elementary mass-action description. It has compressed promoter binding, transcription and translation into two production functions. Rapid equilibrium is one route to (8); quasi-steady promoter occupancy is another related reduction. Sequential binding, allostery, sequestration and unequal sites can produce different rational laws. The point is not that every repressor obeys a Hill equation. The point is that a declared reduction turns a biological network into a tractable slow state system.

Scale concentrations by their repression constants and time by a common removal time. A symmetric teaching case is

x˙=41+y2−x,y˙=41+x2−y,x,y≥0.\dot x=\frac{4}{1+y^{2}}-x,\qquad \dot y=\frac{4}{1+x^{2}}-y,\qquad x,y\ge0.(10)

Now build the portrait without guessing it:

  1. Set x˙=0\dot x=0. The xx-nullcline is x=4/(1+y2)x=4/(1+y^{2}); horizontal motion pauses there.
  2. Set y˙=0\dot y=0. The yy-nullcline is y=4/(1+x2)y=4/(1+x^{2}); vertical motion pauses there.
  3. Only intersections of both curves stop both components. They are the fixed points.
  4. Test one point in each region. Below the xx-nullcline, x˙>0\dot x>0; above it, x˙<0\dot x<0. Apply the corresponding rule to yy.
  5. The stable manifold of the middle saddle separates the two basins. Symmetry makes the diagonal x=yx=y invariant, so here it is the separatrix.

Working phase portrait · click to launch a trajectory

The equations are exactly (10). Nullclines and the separatrix stay fixed; each click supplies one initial condition. Predict the basin before launching the path.

Click inside the square. The endpoint will be assigned to the nearest stable expression state.

Gardner, Cantor and Collins built a mutual-repression toggle in Escherichia coli, with two repressors and two chemical inputs used to set the state.2 The model above is a teaching reduction rather than a parameter fit to that device. What transfers is the dynamical claim: two basins separated by a threshold make history matter.1

5The Jacobian is the local system

Linearization is an explicit first-order dynamical system for small displacements from one fixed point. The matrix must be written, evaluated and interpreted at that point.

Start from a general planar nonlinear system

x˙=f(x,y),y˙=g(x,y),f(x∗,y∗)=g(x∗,y∗)=0.\dot x=f(x,y),\qquad \dot y=g(x,y),\qquad f(x^{*},y^{*})=g(x^{*},y^{*})=0.(11)

Define the displacement u=(u,v)T=(x−x∗,y−y∗)T\mathbf{u}=(u,v)^{\mathsf T}=(x-x^{*},y-y^{*})^{\mathsf T}. Taylor expansion at the fixed point gives the actual nearby dynamics

u˙=[∂f∂x∣∗∂f∂y∣∗∂g∂x∣∗∂g∂y∣∗]⏟A=J(x∗,y∗)u+R(u),∥R(u)∥∥u∥⟶0.\dot{\mathbf{u}}= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\left.\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{*}&\left.\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right|_{*}\\[7pt]\left.\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}\right|_{*}&\left.\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y}\right|_{*}\end{bmatrix}}_{A=J(x^{*},y^{*})}\mathbf{u} +\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{u}),\qquad \frac{\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{u})\|}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}\longrightarrow0.(12)

The canonical linearized system discards the higher-order remainder and uses a fresh variable z\mathbf{z}:

z˙=Az,A=J(x∗,y∗)=[a11a12a21a22].\boxed{\dot{\mathbf{z}}=A\mathbf{z}},\qquad A=J(x^{*},y^{*})=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{21}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}.(13)

Equation (13), not the array of derivatives by itself, is the linearization. It approximates displacement dynamics near one fixed point. A different fixed point generally has a different matrix.

For the toggle in equation (10),

J(x,y)=[−1−8y(1+y2)2−8x(1+x2)2−1].J(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}-1&-\dfrac{8y}{(1+y^{2})^{2}}\\[7pt]-\dfrac{8x}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}&-1\end{bmatrix}.(14)

The diagonal minus signs are protein loss. Each off-diagonal minus sign is repression by the other protein. Evaluate the matrix at every nullcline intersection:

fixed point (x∗,y∗)(x^{*},y^{*})eigenvalues of AAlocal conclusionbiological state
(0.2679,3.7321)(0.2679,3.7321)−0.5, −1.5stable nodelow XX, high YY
(1.3788,1.3788)(1.3788,1.3788)+0.3106, −2.3106saddlethreshold state
(3.7321,0.2679)(3.7321,0.2679)−0.5, −1.5stable nodehigh XX, low YY

The local calculation confirms the global sign picture. At the saddle, its stable eigenvector gives only the tangent to the separatrix at the fixed point. The full basin boundary is a nonlinear trajectory and is not supplied by the Jacobian.3

6Hurwitz turns modes into a stability test

An eigenvalue is a growth rate. Its real part controls the exponential envelope, so all modes must decay for the fixed point to attract locally.

If Aw=λwA\mathbf{w}=\lambda\mathbf{w} and a linear trajectory begins on that eigenvector, write z(t)=a(t)w\mathbf{z}(t)=a(t)\mathbf{w}. Substitution into (13) gives

a˙ w=aAw=λaw⟹a(t)=a(0)eλt.\dot a\,\mathbf{w}=aA\mathbf{w}=\lambda a\mathbf{w}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a(t)=a(0)e^{\lambda t}.(15)

For a diagonalizable matrix, every initial displacement is a sum of these modes:

z(t)=∑jcjeλjtwj.\mathbf{z}(t)=\sum_j c_j e^{\lambda_j t}\mathbf{w}_j.(16)

A complex eigenvalue contributes rotation through its imaginary part and an envelope eRe⁡(λ)te^{\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)t}. A negative real part makes that mode shrink; a positive real part makes it grow. Jordan blocks may multiply a mode by a polynomial in tt, but an exponential with strictly negative real part still wins at long times.

Hurwitz stability rule

A real matrix AA is Hurwitz exactly when Re⁡λj(A)<0\operatorname{Re}\lambda_j(A)<0 for every eigenvalue. Then the origin of z˙=Az\dot{\mathbf{z}}=A\mathbf{z} is globally exponentially asymptotically stable. If A=J(x∗)A=J(\mathbf{x}^{*}) for a continuously differentiable nonlinear system, the fixed point x∗\mathbf{x}^{*} is locally exponentially asymptotically stable. One eigenvalue with positive real part proves instability. A zero real part makes this first-order test inconclusive.

For a real two-by-two matrix, define

τ=tr⁡A,Δ=det⁡A,D=τ2−4Δ,λ±=τ±D2.\tau=\operatorname{tr}A,\qquad \Delta=\det A,\qquad D=\tau^{2}-4\Delta,\qquad \lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\tau\pm\sqrt D}{2}.(17)

The Hurwitz rule reduces to two inequalities:

A is Hurwitz⟺τ<0  and  Δ>0.\boxed{A\text{ is Hurwitz}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tau<0\ \text{ and }\ \Delta>0.}(18)

A positive determinant means the two eigenvalues have the same sign when real, or form a complex-conjugate pair. A negative trace makes their sum negative, hence both real parts negative. If the determinant is negative, the two real eigenvalues have opposite signs and one direction must grow.

Δ>0, D<0, τ<0\Delta>0,\ D<0,\ \tau<0complex, negative real partstable spiral

Part 3

Go beyond first order

When a real part reaches zero, the discarded terms matter. A global scalar quantity can sometimes replace the missing local decision.

7Energy answers what the Jacobian cannot

The spring is not a separate trick. It shows how state opens a phase plane and how a generalized energy can certify the behavior of every trajectory in it.

Return to equations (1) and (2). Define mechanical energy

E(q,v)=12mv2+12kq2.E(q,v)=\frac12mv^{2}+\frac12kq^{2}.(19)

Differentiate EE along the actual dynamics, not along a sketch:

E˙=mvv˙+kqq˙=v(−kq−cv)+kqv=−cv2≤0.\dot E=mv\dot v+kq\dot q=v(-kq-cv)+kqv=-cv^{2}\le0.(20)

If c=0c=0, energy is conserved. Every initial condition remains on one ellipse mv2+kq2=2E0mv^{2}+kq^{2}=2E_0. The origin is Lyapunov stable because nearby energy levels remain nearby, but it is not attracting because a nonzero orbit never loses energy. This is a center, established by the conserved quantity rather than by the purely imaginary eigenvalues alone.

If c>0c>0, energy never increases. Equation (20) is zero whenever v=0v=0, including points with q≠0q\ne0, so it is not correct to claim strict decrease everywhere away from the origin. A point with v=0v=0 and q≠0q\ne0 has v˙=−(k/m)q≠0\dot v=-(k/m)q\ne0, so it immediately leaves that zero-derivative set. The only trajectory that remains there forever is the origin. LaSalle's invariance principle therefore completes the conclusion: all trajectories converge to the origin.

Energy becomes a Lyapunov function

A continuously differentiable scalar V(x)V(\mathbf{x}) is a local Lyapunov candidate around x∗\mathbf{x}^{*} when V(x∗)=0V(\mathbf{x}^{*})=0 and V>0V>0 nearby away from the fixed point. If V˙=∇V⋅F≤0\dot V=\nabla V\cdot\mathbf{F}\le0, it certifies stability under the usual domain conditions. Strict negativity certifies asymptotic stability. With only nonpositive derivative, identify the largest invariant set inside V˙=0\dot V=0. The exposition works this logic through the spring, pendulum and predator-prey examples.

Why is the extra argument necessary? Compare three nonlinear systems in polar coordinates:

r˙=−r3,θ˙=1spiral inward,r˙=0,θ˙=1remain on a circle,r˙=+r3,θ˙=1spiral outward.\begin{aligned}\dot r&=-r^{3},&\dot\theta&=1&&\text{spiral inward},\\\dot r&=0,&\dot\theta&=1&&\text{remain on a circle},\\\dot r&=+r^{3},&\dot\theta&=1&&\text{spiral outward}.\end{aligned}(21)

At the origin all three have the same Jacobian [0−110]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right] and eigenvalues ±i\pm i. The cubic radial term is invisible to the Jacobian and decides all three different fates.

Do not identify a cycle by appearance

A center is a continuum of neutrally stable closed orbits, one for each energy. A stable limit cycle is one isolated periodic orbit that nearby trajectories approach. A closed-looking numerical curve proves neither. Integrate longer, change the step, perturb the initial state and test whether multiple starts approach the same orbit.

8Cycles belong to state-space dimension

One dimension forbids return. Two dimensions can support an isolated periodic orbit. Three dimensions admit more behaviors, but they do not make analysis useless.

A stable limit cycle can be constructed by combining two scalar laws in polar coordinates:

r˙=1−r,θ˙=ω>0.\dot r=1-r,\qquad \dot\theta=\omega>0.(22)

The radial equation has one stable fixed point at r=1r=1. Rotation turns that fixed radius into one periodic orbit. Starts inside move outward, starts outside move inward, and both approach the same cycle. This is qualitatively different from the spring center.

For a smooth planar flow, the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem provides a useful global proof pattern: trap a trajectory in a compact region, show that its limiting set contains no fixed point, and a periodic orbit must remain. It is a theorem about a two-dimensional invariant set. A three-repressor ring such as

p˙i=α1+pi−1 n−pi,i=1,2,3(indices mod 3)\dot p_i=\frac{\alpha}{1+p_{i-1}^{\,n}}-p_i,\qquad i=1,2,3\quad(\text{indices mod }3)(23)

does not fall under that planar theorem. The repressilator was built as a synthetic genetic oscillator, and later designs improved the persistence and synchronization of its oscillations.56 Three dimensions can also support chaos, but “simulate and look” is not the only remaining method. Conservation, monotonicity, Lyapunov functions, singular perturbation and bifurcation theory can still impose structure.

9Chemistry restricts geometry

A biochemical phase portrait lives in a positive, often conserved state space. Test that domain before interpreting any smooth vector field as chemistry.

The quickest test is at a boundary. If concentration xj=0x_j=0, an admissible model must satisfy Fj(x)≥0F_j(\mathbf{x})\ge0. In elementary mass action, every reaction that consumes species XjX_j contains a factor xjx_j, so its consumption rate vanishes at that boundary. Production may point inward; loss cannot point into negative concentration.

Conservation makes the accessible space smaller too. If ℓTS=0\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\mathsf T}S=0, then ℓTx\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x} is constant along x˙=Sv(x)\dot{\mathbf{x}}=S\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}). Stability should then be asked inside the corresponding stoichiometric compatibility class. A zero eigenvalue normal to that class may only report a conserved total, not a physical instability.

These constraints answer the chemical-plant question more carefully than “cells are stable.” Some networks are bounded by conserved pools, some dissipate a Lyapunov function, some use negative feedback, and some deliberately switch or oscillate. Stability is a property to establish for a specified state, parameter set and domain, not a property granted by being biological.78

Part 4

Build and test

A student team should leave with a portrait another person can reconstruct and a claim that names its scope.

10An evidence-backed portrait

The core story is a sequence of questions. Each question demands a different object and licenses a different conclusion.

The story in nine sentences
  1. A state contains enough present information to determine the velocity.
  2. A scalar field can be read as f(x)f(x), a phase line and x(t)x(t), with each view retaining different information.
  3. Ordering permits scalar bistability but forbids a nonconstant scalar cycle.
  4. A saddle-node changes the number and stability of fixed points and slows recovery near collision.
  5. A biological toggle becomes two Hill production-loss equations only after a declared promoter-state reduction.
  6. Nullclines and sign regions assemble its global phase portrait.
  7. The explicit linearized system z˙=Az\dot{\mathbf{z}}=A\mathbf{z} and the Hurwitz rule classify a hyperbolic fixed point locally.
  8. When first-order terms are silent, an energy or Lyapunov function can supply global evidence.
  9. Dimension, positivity and stoichiometry restrict which portraits a biochemical system can realize.

A team can begin in twenty minutes

  1. Write the biological claim. “This circuit has two persistent expression states,” “this fixed point recovers after a small perturbation,” or “this orbit attracts.”
  2. Declare the state. List variables, units, domains, external inputs, conserved totals and hidden states that were reduced.
  3. Write the vector field. Tie every production, loss and regulatory sign to the reaction or approximation that generated it.
  4. Do global geometry first. Find all scalar roots or planar nullclines, label signs and predict basins before simulation.
  5. Do the local system. At every fixed point, write A=J(x∗)A=J(\mathbf{x}^{*}) and z˙=Az\dot{\mathbf{z}}=A\mathbf{z}; record eigenvalues, eigenvectors and whether Hurwitz applies.
  6. Look for a certificate. Test conservation, positivity, trapping regions or a candidate VV whenever the claim is global or the Jacobian is inconclusive.
  7. Make one reproducible figure. Overlay nullclines, all fixed points, several stated initial conditions and the physical domain.
  8. Try to break the conclusion. Lengthen the integration horizon, change numerical resolution, perturb initial conditions and parameters, and test the reduction assumptions.
Minimum handoff

One reaction or mechanism diagram, one state table with units, one vector field, one annotated portrait, one fixed-point and eigenvalue table, one theorem or Lyapunov calculation with its hypotheses, and one failed or surviving stress test. Another team member should be able to regenerate the argument from those objects.
