Order of magnitude: the physics

Estimate fast, estimate robustly, and the world starts to look constrained. From a bomb yield read off a photograph to the one scaling that runs through the whole course.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · lecture 1 · core page · 2026-09-01 (Tue) 10:40–12:15 · lecturer-taught

This lecture opens the course and then teaches its first tool. The first fifteen minutes are the rules. The rest is order-of-magnitude reasoning on the physics side, where the quantities are lengths, times, energies, and the scaling laws that tie them together. Lecture 2 does the same for biology and chemistry.

The claim being made for the method is stronger than it first sounds, so it is worth stating plainly at the top. Order-of-magnitude reasoning is not a way of getting approximate answers quickly. It is a way of finding the one constraint that decides a problem, before you have a model and often before you have data. The answer it produces is a by-product. The constraint is the point.

Part 1

The course

What this is, in one section. The full rules live on the organization page.

1What this course is

Physics, system, industry: three schools asking three questions about life, and one course whose tools aim at all three.

CCBS reads a cell as a machine you can reason about quantitatively. Three schools frame the reasoning, each asking its own question. Physics asks what life is as an object: sizes, rates, energies, and the constraints they impose. That is lectures 1, 2 and 7. System asks how life works as a machine: reaction networks, dynamics, feedback, stochasticity, computation. That is lectures 3 to 6 and 12. Industry asks how life could be useful as a tool: design a protein, engineer a metabolism, grow a machine. That is lectures 13 to 16. Each school has its own origins, its own analysis style, and its own goals. The course's promise is that you will leave able to reason about any biological problem with all three at once.

Nothing needs prior background classes, only "an exuberant love for biology," which is mandatory. What the course does demand is a different relationship to material than you may be used to. In 2026 the raw material for learning is available to everyone: an agent can digest a paper, write first-pass code and draft a figure on demand. So the course moves the emphasis to what an agent cannot do, which is to understand, defend, and communicate.

Order of magnitude comes first because it is the tool that decides which of the other tools you need. Before you write down a differential equation you should know whether the process it describes takes a microsecond or a century, and whether the thing it acts on exists in ten copies or ten million. Half the time that knowledge dissolves the question.

Part 2

The method

Three moves: count the dimensions, name the bottleneck, calibrate against something you have touched.

2What the method is

Numbers can say a lot, but only when they are robust. Details are fragile. Orders of magnitude are not.

The world is connected. Observations that seem unrelated can be deeply constraining, and Eratosthenes measured the size of the Earth from the length of a shadow. The instrument that sees such connections is order-of-magnitude reasoning: estimate the quantity, keep only the power of ten, and let the constraint do the arguing.1

Order of magnitude

An estimate right to within a factor. We keep 1 or 10, not more. The claim is of the form "it is about 106 per second", never "approximately 1.3×106".

Two properties make this the right first tool. First it is fast: you can do it in your head, mid-conversation, which means you can do it before deciding whether a project is worth a month. Second, and more important, it is robust. The factors that dominate an estimate are few, so the answer survives being wrong about every detail, whereas a precisely written answer claims a precision it does not have. Details are fragile. Orders of magnitude are not.

The method has three moves, and the rest of Part 2 is one worked example of each.

3Dimensional analysis: one theorem

If the units of a problem admit only one dimensionless combination, then the answer is that combination, and no physics is required to find it.

The formal core of move 1 is a hundred-year-old theorem. Buckingham stated it in 1914 in a paper whose whole purpose was to show that arguments physicists had been making informally for decades were in fact a consequence of one counting argument.2

The Buckingham Π theorem

Let a physical relation involve n quantities, expressed in a system with k independent fundamental units. Then the relation can be written as a function of exactly n − k independent dimensionless products.

Buckingham's own words for the count: the number of independent arguments "is the maximum number of independent dimensionless products of the form (6) which can be made by combining the n quantities", and there are "(n − k) equations" fixing them.

The consequence that matters is the special case. If n − k = 1, the problem is solved by units alone, up to a single dimensionless constant that no amount of dimensional reasoning can supply. There is exactly one way to combine the quantities into something dimensionless, so that combination has to be a constant, and the functional form of the answer follows.

This is worth dwelling on, because it is the only place in the course where you get something for nothing. You have written down no equation of motion, made no approximation, and solved nothing. You have counted.

What the theorem does not give you

It gives the form, never the constant. The blast wave really goes as t2/5, but the prefactor depends on the ratio of specific heats of air and has to come from solving the flow. Taylor spent a paper on it. The division of labour is the useful part: units decide the shape, physics decides the number in front, and the shape is usually what you needed.

4Trinity: a yield from a photograph

The canonical demonstration. Twenty-five frames of a fireball, one dimensionless group, and a classified number falls out.

In 1941 G. I. Taylor was told that a bomb might be built in which a very large amount of energy would be released by nuclear fission. He asked what the mechanical effects would be, wrote down the self-similar blast-wave solution, and circulated it to the Ministry of Home Security in June of that year. The paper was published only in 1950, after declassification, "as it was first written, without alteration".3 Everything in it predates the explosion it is now famous for analysing by four years.

The second paper is the demonstration.4 After the July 1945 Trinity test, a strip of high-speed photographs was declassified and published. The fireball radius at each frame was public. The yield was not. Taylor read radii and times off twenty-five frames and applied the group from Figure 2:

Π1 = R5ρ0 / (E t2) = const   ⇒   E = K(γ) ρ0 R5 / t2 (1)

Both halves of the prediction can be checked against his own table, and they are checked here rather than asserted.14 First the exponent: a least-squares fit of log R on log t over his twenty-five points, spanning three decades in time, gives a slope of 0.406 against the predicted 2/5 = 0.400, an error of 1.5 per cent. Second the constant: every frame should imply the same energy, and after the first frame they do.

Taylor gave two numbers, 16,800 tons of TNT and 23,700 tons, and said the first was the more likely to be accurate. The modern radiochemical value for the total yield is about 21 kilotons.5 That looks like a twenty per cent miss until you read what he was estimating. His 1941 paper already predicted that a nuclear device would be about half as efficient a blast producer as a chemical explosive of the same total energy, because much of the energy leaves as radiation, and his 16,800 tons is explicitly "that part of the energy which was not radiated outside the ball of fire". A blast-derived estimate is supposed to fall short of the radiochemical total.

The same explosion, estimated twice, from opposite ends of rigour

Enrico Fermi was at the test. He dropped scraps of paper as the blast front passed, measured how far they were carried, got about 2.5 m, and announced roughly ten kilotons within seconds of the shock arriving.5 Taylor used a published photograph, a self-similar solution and a season of algebra to get 16.8. Both are blast measurements, both fall below the radiochemical 21, and both are the same order of magnitude. That is the whole argument for the method: Fermi's back-of-envelope and Taylor's fluid dynamics land in the same place, so the envelope was enough for the decision that had to be made in the moment.

5Hangzhou: naming the bottleneck

Evacuating a city by plane or by car. The same flux formula answers both, and the bottleneck, not the vehicle, decides.

Dimensional analysis solved the blast wave because it had exactly one group. Most problems have more, and then you need move 2. The worked example is macroscopic and familiar: evacuate all of Hangzhou, about 107 people. The question sounds like a choice of transport. It is not.

By air

One airport. Under emergency loading a plane leaves every two minutes, and each carries about 102 people:

(86400 s/day) / (120 s/plane) ≈ 7×102 planes/day × 102 people ≈ 7×104 people/day (2)

For 107 people that is about 140 days. Too slow for an emergency, and note where the failure is. The aircraft is the fastest vehicle available and it is irrelevant, because the constraint is the number of runways.

By road

Count lanes instead. Roughly ten highways leave the city; in an emergency all lanes run outbound, so call it 102 lanes. A lane is a pipe with three numbers: how many people per vehicle, how fast, how closely spaced.

Flux = N v / L = (4 people × 20 m/s) / (40 m) = 2 people/s per lane (3)

With 102 lanes that is 1.7×107 people per day, so the city clears in about half a day. Same formula, opposite verdict, and the only thing that changed is the number of parallel pipes.

The move that matters

Now ask the obvious objection. Are there enough cars? What if people drive faster, or slower? Each of these is a fragile detail, and the way to kill all of them at once is to find what is actually fixed.

The spacing L is not a free choice. You leave a gap because you need time to react, so L = vτ with τ ≈ 2 s set by human reflexes. Substitute:

Flux = N v / L = N v / (vτ) = N / τ (4)

The speed has cancelled. Whatever you thought you knew about how fast the vehicle goes was never in the answer, because going faster forces you to leave a proportionally bigger gap. What remains is the occupancy and a fixed human reaction time.

The lesson, stated generally

When an estimate depends on a quantity you are unsure of, look for the relation that ties that quantity to something fixed, and substitute. If the uncertain quantity cancels, you never needed it. If it does not cancel, you have just learned that it is one of the few things worth measuring. Either outcome is progress, which is why this move is worth making even when you expect it to fail.

Part 3

The scales

What stays constant when size changes, what does not, and the single most useful scaling in the course.

6Jump height, and the wall at τ = L/v

Muscle energy scales with body volume, so jump height is size-independent. The time available to deliver it is not, and that is where the interesting biology lives.

How does jump height scale with animal size? Set the energy stored in muscle equal to the height gained. Muscle is a fixed volume fraction fm of the body, and it does a fixed work per unit volume w, so the available energy is Em = fm w L3. The mass is m = ρL3. Set them equal:

ρL3 g h = fm w L3   ⇒   h = fm w / (ρ g),   independent of L (5)

The L3 cancels on both sides, and jump height turns out not to depend on size at all. Only the muscle fraction and the quality of the muscle matter. Putting in fm ≈ 0.15 and w ≈ 105 J/m3 gives h ≈ 1.5 m, which is the right answer for an athlete's centre of mass.

This argument is a hundred years old, and Haldane credited it to Galileo

J. B. S. Haldane's 1926 essay On Being the Right Size puts it exactly: "people still believe that if a flea were as large as a man it could jump a thousand feet into the air. As a matter of fact the height to which an animal can jump is more nearly independent of its size than proportional to it."8 His figures are a flea at about two feet and a man at about five. He opens the point with "although Galileo demonstrated the contrary more than three hundred years ago", pointing back to the square-cube law in the Two New Sciences of 1638. Note also his hedge, "more nearly independent than proportional": his own two numbers differ by a factor of 2.5, and he is telling you what an order-of-magnitude claim actually claims.

Now the time

If h is the same for everyone then so is the takeoff speed, v = √(2gh) ≈ 3 m/s. But the animal has only its own body length in which to reach that speed, so the time available is

τ ≈ L / v (6)

and unlike h, this one depends on size, linearly. A human has 0.34 s, a cat 0.07 s, a flea 0.7 ms. Fast muscle needs roughly 0.1 s to shorten by half its length, so somewhere between the cat and the flea the animal runs out of time.

The same statement in power terms is sharper. Delivering 105 J/m3 in 0.7 ms demands 1.5×108 W/m3, and muscle tops out near 2×105. The flea needs a thousand times the power density muscle can produce.

The resolution is that small jumpers do not power the jump at all. They contract muscle slowly against an elastic element, load it over hundreds of milliseconds, hold it with a latch, and release it in under a millisecond. The spring, not the muscle, sets the power. Energy is conserved and power is amplified, because power is energy over time and the spring shortens the time.

Haldane had the constraint and missed the mechanism

His sentence carries the caveat "provided it can be developed quickly enough in the small animal", which is equation (6) in words, in 1926. But his explanation for why a flea does not clear a man's height is that insect muscle "appears to be less efficient". That is the wrong answer, and the right one is more interesting: the flea is not using muscle as an engine, it is using it as a winch. Getting the constraint right and the mechanism wrong is the normal outcome of a good estimate, and it is what makes the estimate worth doing. It tells you exactly which question to go and ask.

One number here is borrowed and flagged

Haldane's flea figure of two feet is high compared with published measurements, which are species-dependent and generally smaller. The primary sources are recorded in literature/NEEDED.md and have not been read, so this page quotes the figure as Haldane's and does not assert a corrected one. The scaling argument does not depend on it.

7Heat, and t ≈ x²/κ

Fourier's law, conservation, one equation, and the scaling that survives: double the size, quadruple the time. This is the most useful line in the course.

Heat flux is proportional to the local temperature gradient, and energy is conserved. Those two facts give the heat equation in four lines.

J = −K ∇T   (Fourier)     ΔE = Cv δT   (storage) (7)
δx Cv δT = [J(x) − J(x+δx)] δt   ⇒   ∂T/∂t = κ ∇²T,   κ = K/Cv (8)

Two material constants have collapsed into one. Now take the order-of-magnitude step: replace each derivative by the variation over one characteristic scale, ∂/∂t → 1/t and ∂²/∂x² → 1/x². The temperature cancels and

T/t ≈ κ T/x²   ⇒   t ≈ x²/κ (9)

Only one functional form survives, and it is quadratic in size. Every diffusive process in this course obeys it, with a different letter in place of κ: heat, molecules, momentum, even the spread of a mutation. Learn this one line.

Calibrating κ, and getting it wrong

Move 3 says to fix the constant from something you have personally experienced. The traditional anchor for this lecture is a potato: 10 cm across, ten minutes to cook, so κ ≈ (0.1)²/600 = 1.7×10−5 m²/s. It is a lovely move and the anchor is wrong.

Check it against the definition. A potato is mostly water, and water's conductivity, density and heat capacity are all textbook numbers:

κwater = K/(ρcp) = 0.60 / (1000 × 4180) = 1.4×10−7 m²/s (10)

a factor of 116 smaller. The anchor claimed a potato cooks in ten minutes, and by conduction it does not: equation (9) with the true κ and 3 cm to the centre gives about 100 minutes, which is what a baked potato actually takes. The original anchor was a memory of boiling chopped potato, where the distance is a centimetre and the answer really is about ten minutes.

What that does to the moon

The payoff of the potato was supposed to be the moon. With the bad κ and a 1737 km radius, equation (9) gives about 6×109 years, tantalisingly close to the 4.57 billion years the solar system has existed, and the lecture concluded that the moon should be nearly but not quite cooled. With rock's real diffusivity, κ = 1.0×10−6 m²/s, the answer is 9×1010 years, twenty times the age of the solar system.

So the conclusion inverts. Conduction cannot have cooled the moon, and the right question is how deep the cooling reached. Turn equation (9) around and ask what thickness has had time to cool:

d = √(κ t) = √(1.0×10−6 × 1.4×1017) ≈ 390 km (11)

which is the outer 22 per cent of the radius. That is a real and checkable prediction about the moon, and it is closer to the truth than the original.

This is the method working, not failing

A wrong anchor produced a wrong conclusion, and the error was caught by a routine that costs thirty seconds: compare your calibrated constant against the material property it is supposed to be. Move 1 is a theorem and cannot betray you. Move 2 is a judgement and can. Move 3 is an empirical claim, and an anchor you have never checked is a number you invented. Always check the constant, and always quote it, so that whoever reads your estimate can check it too.

8Life at low Reynolds number

Below ten microns, inertia does not exist. Everything you know about swimming, coasting and stirring is wrong there, and the estimate that shows it is one line.

The last physics of the lecture is the one that most damages your intuition, and it is the bridge to lecture 2. The Reynolds number compares inertial to viscous forces:

Re = ρvL/η (12)

For a bacterium, ρ = 103 kg/m3, v ≈ 30 µm/s, L ≈ 1 µm, η = 10−3 Pa·s:

Re ≈ (103)(3×10−5)(10−6) / 10−3 = 3×10−5 (13)

against roughly 106 for a human swimming. Purcell put it as "about 10−4 or 10−5. For these animals inertia is totally irrelevant. We know that F = ma, but they could scarcely care less."9

The consequence he draws is the one worth remembering. Stop pushing a bacterium and ask how far it coasts. Balancing its momentum against Stokes drag gives a stopping distance of about 0.03 Å, and Purcell's own figure is 0.1 Å, in 0.6 microseconds. Either way it is a fraction of an atom.

One more of Purcell's estimates carries straight into lecture 2. Compare the time to move something a distance l by stirring, l/v, against the time to move it by diffusion, l²/D. The ratio is lv/D. Purcell introduces it, notes that "I'm sure this ratio has someone's name but I don't know the literature", and calls it the stirring number. It is the Péclet number. A cell of a micron with D ≈ 100 µm²/s and any plausible internal flow has lv/D < 1, so stirring a bacterium is pointless, and diffusion has already done the job.

Using it

What the method is for once you leave physics, and what you do this week.

9Null models

In biology an estimate is a null hypothesis. It says what plain physics would do with no regulation at all, and the gap is where design lives.

Everything so far was physics, where the estimate is the answer. In biology the estimate is rarely the answer, and that is precisely what makes it valuable. You compute what the system would do if nothing were regulating it, and then you compare.11 Three outcomes, and all three are useful.

Table 2. What to conclude from the gap between the estimate and the observation.
OutcomeWhat it meansExample
Physics is far too slowSome mechanism must exist that you have not accounted for. Go find it.A protein diffusing down a 1 m axon needs 3×102 years, so motors exist.
Physics is fast enoughNo regulation is needed for this. Stop looking, and do not build a model of it.Mixing a bacterium takes 10 ms, so a bacterium is well stirred by doing nothing at all.
The system sits at the boundNothing left to explain by design; physics already forbids doing better. This is the strongest kind of result.The Berg–Purcell limit on how precisely a cell can read a concentration.10

The third row is the one people forget, and it is worth being honest that it undercuts the framing of the other two. Sometimes biology is at the physical limit, and then there is no gap and no design to find. Berg and Purcell showed that a chemotactic bacterium measures concentrations about as accurately as any device of its size could, given that the molecules arrive by diffusion. When an estimate comes out equal to the observation, that is not a failed null model. It is the discovery that the organism is up against physics.

The ten-questions game

Ten questions, three minutes each, answers to within a factor of ten. What is the mass of all the RNA in a human cell? How many ATP molecules are in a bacterium at any instant? How long is one transcription cycle? How many metabolic reactions fit in one cell? The method is always the same: name the scale, name the bottleneck, then answer. We do one live, and the room rates it 0 to 3 on the course rubric. That single act converts the rubric from a table into a shared standard.
