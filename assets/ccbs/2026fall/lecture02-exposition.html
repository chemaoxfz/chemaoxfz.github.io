The cell in numbers, and the two reactions that follow

A full tutorial on lecture 2. How to count what is in a cell, how to turn a size into a speed, how to write a reaction so that its rate law is honest, and how to bound that rate from first principles. Everything is derived rather than quoted, and the point of all of it arrives in section 22.

There are three documents for this lecture and they do different jobs. The core page is the spine, and it is what happens in the room. The lineage essay is the scholarship: where these ideas came from, what their authors actually said, and where the retelling has drifted. This is the third one. It is the document you read afterwards, slowly, if you want to be able to do this yourself rather than recognise it when someone else does it.

It assumes almost nothing. If you can multiply powers of ten and you remember what a derivative is, you have enough. Everything else is built here. It is longer than it needs to be for an exam and about the right length to actually learn something.

Part 0

Orientation

What you should be able to do at the end, and the only three moves you need to get there.

1What this is

Four abilities, stated concretely enough that you can check whether you have them.

By the end of this you should be able to do the following without looking anything up.

  1. Convert between counts and concentrations in a cell, in your head. Someone says "there are about 50 copies of that transcription factor". You say "so about 80 nanomolar, which is around its dissociation constant, so it is operating in the interesting regime".
  2. Turn a size into a speed, and a speed into a time. Someone shows you a compartment. You say how long it takes to mix, and therefore whether treating it as well stirred is legitimate.
  3. Look at a reaction diagram and know what it is claiming. If the arrow is elementary you write the rate law immediately. If it is not, you say so, and you say what would have to be measured.
  4. Bound a rate. Given any two molecules, compute the fastest they could possibly react, and compare a claimed number against it.

The fifth thing is not an ability but a piece of knowledge, and it is the one the rest of the course is built on: binding equilibrates thousands of times during one catalytic turnover. Section 22 derives that and section 23 shows what it forces.

2The three moves

Every estimate in this document is one of these, or two of them stuck together.

Lecture 1 introduced order-of-magnitude reasoning on physical problems. Here is the compressed version, because you are about to use it eleven times.

Move 1 · Bound it

Replace the quantity you want by something you can compute that is definitely bigger, or definitely smaller. A bound is often easier than an estimate and it is always more honest. Example ahead: section 5 bounds the number of proteins in a cell by assuming the cell is entirely protein.

Move 2 · Calibrate on something you have seen

If a law has one unknown constant, one observation of any system fixes it, and then it applies to every other system. Example ahead: section 10 fixes kBT/6πηk_{B}T/6\pi \eta from a particle under an 1827 microscope and then uses it on a protein.

Move 3 · Scale

Once you have one case and a scaling law, you have every case. Example ahead: section 11 gets the mixing time of a neuron from the mixing time of a bacterium and nothing else, because τ∝x2\tau \propto x^{2}.

There is a fourth thing that is not a move but a discipline: carry the units. Most errors in this material are unit errors, not conceptual ones, and section 18 is a whole section on the single conversion that trips everyone.

Part 1

Counting

Five estimates that between them fix every concentration you will meet this term.

3A cubic micron is a femtolitre

The single most useful identity in quantitative cell biology, and the conversion that falls out of it.

Start from the definition of a litre: 1 L=10−3 m31\,\mathrm{L} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}^{3}. A cubic micron is (10−6 m)3=10−18 m3(10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m})^{3} = 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{m}^{3}. So

1 μm3=10−18 m3=10−18/10−3 L=10−15 L=1femtolitre1\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3} = 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{m}^{3} = 10^{-18} / 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{L} = 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{L} = \pf{1 femtolitre}(1)

An E. coli is about that size. Measured properly it runs from 1.5 fL when growing slowly to 4.4 fL in rich medium, with the width holding near 1.26 µm and the length doing the changing.1 Use 1 fL when you want a round number and remember that it is the small end.

Now the conversion. A concentration is molecules per volume divided by Avogadro's number. One molecule in one femtolitre is

c=1NA÷10−15 L=16.02×1023÷10−15 L=1.66×10−9 M=1.7 nMc =\frac{1}{\mathrm{NA}} \div 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{L} =\frac{1}{6.02\times 10^{23}} \div 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{L} = 1.66\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{M} = \pf{1.7\,\mathrm{nM}}(2)
Learn this one

One molecule per bacterium is 1.7 nanomolar. Ten copies is 17 nM. A thousand copies is 1.7 µM. A million copies is 1.7 mM. You will use this more often than any other number in the course, and it is worth being able to produce it without thinking.

4How heavy is a protein

Two ranges, one trick, and a number you should carry for the rest of your career.

We want the mass of a typical protein, and neither ingredient is a single number. Amino acids run from glycine at 75 Da to tryptophan at 204 Da. Proteins run from about 50 residues to about 5000. Both spreads are multiplicative, which means the arithmetic mean is the wrong average.

The log-average, and why

If a quantity spans orders of magnitude, average its logarithm. The estimate from two endpoints aa and bb is the geometric mean ab\sqrt{ab}. The reason is that you are equally uncertain about each decade, not about each unit, so the middle of your uncertainty is the middle of the exponent range. Taking (a+b)/2(a+b)/2 instead would put your estimate near the top of the range, because the top dominates a sum.

Check the trick on the amino acids, where we know the answer. Two endpoints give 75×204=124\sqrt{75\times 204} = 124 Da. The geometric mean over all twenty is 133 Da. The arithmetic mean is 137 Da. All three agree, which is the sign of a well-behaved distribution, and it tells you the trick is not doing much work here.

One correction, and it matters. A residue inside a chain has lost a water molecule to the peptide bond, so subtract 18 Da:

mresidue≈133−18≈110 Dam_{\mathrm{residue}} \approx 133 - 18 \approx \pf{110\,\mathrm{Da}}(3)

Now protein length, where the trick does do work. Two endpoints give 50×5000=500\sqrt{50\times 5000} = 500 residues. The measured abundance-weighted mean for E. coli is 300 residues, and for eukaryotes 400.2 The estimate is high by a factor of 1.7. That is a completely typical order-of-magnitude outcome and you should not be disappointed by it. Use the measured value:

mprotein=300×110 Da=33000 Da=33 kDam_{\text{protein}} = 300 \times 110\,\mathrm{Da} = 33 000\,\mathrm{Da} = \pf{33\,\mathrm{kDa}}(4)
Why the log-average was still worth doing

Because you will meet quantities where nobody has measured the mean. The point of checking a trick on a case with a known answer is to learn how much to trust it on cases without one. Here the answer is: within a factor of two, which is exactly what this course promises and never more.

5How many are there

Do it wrong first. The wrong answer is instructive and it is the one most courses teach.

A cell of 1 fL at the density of water weighs about a picogram. Bacteria are slightly denser than water, around 1.1 g/mL, so call it 1.1 pg. Divide by the mass of one protein:

N≤1.1×10−12 g/33000×1.66×10−24 g=1.1×10−125.5×10−20=2×107N \le^{1.1\times 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{g}/_{33 000 \times 1.66\times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g}} =\frac{1.1\times 10^{-12}}{5.5\times 10^{-20}} = \pf{2\times 10^{7}}(5)

Stop and look at what you just assumed. You divided the entire wet mass by the mass of a protein, which is only correct if the cell is made of nothing but protein. It is not. It is mostly water. Equation (5) is move 1, a bound, and it is a legitimate and useful one as long as you say so.

To turn it into an estimate, put in what a cell is actually made of. About 70% of the wet mass is water, so the dry mass is 30%. Protein is about 55% of the dry mass, with RNA at 20% and DNA at 3%. Therefore

cp=1.1×0.30×0.55=0.18 g/mL of proteinc_{p} = 1.1 \times 0.30 \times 0.55 = \pf{0.18\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{mL}} \text{ of protein}(6)

and the measured value for E. coli is 0.24 g/mL.2 Either one gives

N=cpmprotein=3 to 4 million proteins per femtolitreN =\frac{c_{p}}{\mathrm{mprotein}} = \pf{3 \text{ to } 4 \text{ million proteins per } femtolitre}(7)
The five-fold error, and who else makes it

The bound in (5) is about five times the truth, and the whole error is the water. This is not a pedantic point. Milo went through the published proteome-wide censuses and found that several of them report totals three to ten times below the correct estimate, and argued they should be renormalised against it.2 An estimate whose job is to catch an experiment is worth more than one whose job is to agree with it.

6The concentration ladder

Three rungs. A dissociation constant means nothing until you know which rung it sits next to.

E. coli has about 4400 protein-coding genes. Spread 3.5 million molecules over them and the mean is around 800 copies per species, which by equation (2) is about 1.4 µM. Real copy numbers span from a handful to tens of thousands, but micromolar is the centre.

Metabolites are three decades higher, and this has been measured directly. Bennett and colleagues quantified over a hundred metabolites in glucose-fed E. coli and found a total pool near 300 mM, dominated by a few compounds, with glutamate alone at 96 mM.3

7Is a cell crowded

Two volume budgets, and the asymmetry between information and machinery.

Volume follows from mass through the partial specific volume of protein, 0.73 mL/g, which converts to 1.21 Å³ per dalton. So a 33 kDa protein occupies about 40 nm³. If it were a sphere, its radius would be

Rmin=(3V/4π)1/3=0.066⋅M1/3 nm=0.066×330001/3=2.1 nmR_{\mathrm{min}} = (3V/4\pi )^{1/3} = 0.066 \cdot M^{1/3}\,\mathrm{nm} = 0.066 \times 33000^{1/3} = \pf{2.1\,\mathrm{nm}}(8)

That formula, with M in daltons, is Erickson's, and it is worth memorising because it turns any molecular weight into a size.4 Two warnings. It is a minimum: a real protein carries a hydration shell and is not spherical, so the radius that governs its motion through water is 20 to 40 percent larger. And it applies to compact globular proteins, not to fibrous ones.

Now the budget. Three and a half million proteins at 40 nm³ each is 1.4×108 nm3=0.14 μm31.4\times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{nm}^{3} = 0.14\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}, so protein alone is about 15% of the cell volume. Add RNA and DNA and the total macromolecular mass concentration is around 0.26 g/mL. The cell is not a dilute solution.

Both of those numbers are low, and for different reasons

Zimmerman and Trach measured this directly, on E. coli cytoplasm, in exponential and stationary phase.19 Two of their numbers are worth putting beside the two just derived.

Mass concentration. They get 0.3 to 0.4 g/mL, with their own experimental value at 0.34 g/mL of RNA plus protein, or about 0.35 including DNA. The 0.26 derived above is low by a factor of 1.3. That is inside what an order-of-magnitude estimate promises, and the direction is the informative part: the estimate started from a protein concentration and added RNA and DNA by eye, and eyeballing them undercounts.

Volume fraction. They get an occupancy of about 0.43 mL per mL of cytoplasm. The 15% above is low by a factor of three, and this one is not an arithmetic slip. It is a different quantity wearing the same name.

Which volume you meant

The 15% used 0.73 mL/g0.73\,\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{g}, the partial specific volume of protein. That is the volume the protein's own atoms occupy, and it is the right number for converting mass to displaced water.

Excluded volume is a different question. A second protein cannot approach closer than contact, so each molecule denies its neighbours a shell around itself, and irregular objects do not pack to fill space. Zimmerman and Trach back out the specific volume of an equivalent hard sphere and get 1.2 to 1.3 mL/g1.2 \text{ to } 1.3\,\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{g}, roughly 1.7 times the anhydrous value. Multiply their 0.34 g/mL0.34\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{mL} by that and the 0.430.43 occupancy follows.

So there are two right answers. About 15% of the cell is protein matter, and about 43% of it is unavailable to the next protein. Crowding arguments need the second. Mass-balance arguments need the first. Quoting one where the other belongs is the most common error in this corner of the subject, and it is a factor of three.

Compare the genome. The E. coli chromosome is 4 641 652 base pairs. B-form DNA rises 0.34 nm per base pair and is 2 nm across, so

L=4.64×106×0.34 nm=1.6 mm,  V=π(1 nm)2×0.34 nm×4.64×106=0.005 μm3L = 4.64\times 10^{6} \times 0.34\,\mathrm{nm} = \pf{1.6\,\mathrm{mm}}, \,\, V = \pi (1\,\mathrm{nm})^{2} \times 0.34\,\mathrm{nm} \times 4.64\times 10^{6} = \pf{0.005\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}}(9)
The asymmetry

The genome is 1600 times longer than the cell and takes up half a percent of it. The proteome is made of things a few nanometres across and takes up fifteen percent. Information is long and cheap. Machinery is small and expensive. That asymmetry is why a cell can afford to carry instructions for far more proteins than it expresses at once, and it is the physical reason regulation of expression exists at all.

9Why ⟨x²⟩ grows like t

Derived from a coin flip in six lines. No probability theory needed.

A molecule in water is hit from all sides by other molecules. Model that as a walker that takes a step of fixed length ℓ\ell every τ\tau seconds, left or right with equal probability. After nn steps its position is

xn=ℓ(s1+s2+⋯+sn),  si=±1 with equal probabilityx_{n} = \ell (s_{1} + s_{2} + \dots + s_{n}), \,\, s_{i} = \pm 1 \text{ with equal probability}(10)

The average of xnx_{n} is zero, because every step is as likely to go one way as the other. That is not useful. Square it first:

⟨xn2⟩=ℓ2⟨(∑si)2⟩=ℓ2⟨∑isi2+∑i≠jsisj⟩\langle x_{n}^{2}\rangle = \ell^{2} \langle (\sum s_{i})^{2}\rangle = \ell^{2} \langle \sum_{i}s_{i}^{2} + \sum_{i\ne j}s_{i}s_{j}\rangle(11)

Each si2=1s_{i}^{2} = 1, so the first sum is nn. Every cross term ⟨sisj⟩\langle s_{i}s_{j}\rangle with i≠ji \ne j averages to zero, because the two steps are independent and each is equally likely to be +1+1 or −1-1. So

⟨xn2⟩=nℓ2=(t/τ)ℓ2≡2Dt,   with  D≡ℓ2/2τ\langle x_{n}^{2}\rangle = n\ell^{2} = (t/\tau )\ell^{2} \equiv 2Dt, \,\, \text{ with } \, D \equiv \ell^{2}/2\tau(12)
What just happened

The root mean square displacement grows like t\sqrt{t}, not like tt. A random walker is not slow, it is differently slow: to go twice as far takes four times as long. Every counterintuitive fact in the rest of this part is that one square root. And the diffusion coefficient is not a fundamental constant, it is a combination ℓ2/2τ\ell^{2}/2\tau of a step size and a step time, which is why it depends on the particle and the fluid.

In three dimensions the same argument applies to each axis independently, so ⟨r2⟩=6Dt\langle r^{2}\rangle = 6Dt. For order-of-magnitude work we drop the factor and write τ≈x2/D\tau \approx x^{2}/D, which overestimates by six. That is fine here and it would not be fine in a paper.

10Stokes-Einstein, and the calibration

One formula gives DD for anything, and one 1827 observation fixes its only constant.

Equation (12) defines DD but does not tell you its value. For that you need the physics of the balance between thermal kicks and viscous drag. Einstein's relation says the diffusion coefficient is the mobility times the thermal energy, D=μkBTD = \mu k_{B}T, where the mobility is the velocity per unit applied force. For a sphere of radius aa in a fluid of viscosity η\eta, Stokes' law gives the drag as F=6πηavF = 6\pi \eta av, so μ=1/6πηa\mu = 1/6\pi \eta a and

D=kBT6πηa  ⇒  D∝1/aD =\frac{k_{B}T}{6\pi \eta a} \,\,\Rightarrow \,\, \pf{D \propto 1/a}(13)

The scaling is the useful part and it is worth saying out loud: a particle ten times smaller diffuses ten times faster. Not a hundred times, not the square root, exactly ten.

Calibrating on Brown's microscope

Everything in (13) except the radius is one constant, kBT/6πηk_{B}T/6\pi \eta. One observation of any particle fixes it. Take the founding one.

In 1827 Robert Brown watched particles from Clarkia pulchella pollen jiggle in water. He was not watching the pollen grains, which are 100 µm across and do not visibly move. He was watching organelles ejected from burst grains, and the spherical ones, spherosomes, have a modal radius of 0.5 µm.5 Such a particle moves about 0.74 µm in one second. Inverting ⟨∣x∣⟩=0.802Dt\langle |x|\rangle = 0.80\sqrt{2Dt}:

D=(0.74/0.80)2/2=0.43 μm2/s ⇒ kBT/6πη=D⋅a=0.21 μm3/sD = (0.74/0.80)^{2} / 2 = 0.43\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s} \,\Rightarrow \, k_{B}T/6\pi \eta = D\cdot a = \pf{0.21\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s}}(14)

The true value at 25 °C is 0.245 µm³/s. A qualitative description from 1827, with no measurement in it, gets a fundamental constant to 13%. That is move 2, and it is the whole reason order-of-magnitude reasoning works.

Now divide by the radius of a protein, 2.1 nm from equation (8):

Dprotein=0.21 μm3/s÷0.0021 μm=100 μm2/s in waterD_{\text{protein}} = 0.21\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}/\mathrm{s} \div 0.0021\,\mu\mathrm{m} = \pf{100\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}} \text{ in water}(15)

13Where mass action comes from

Derive it from collisions rather than asserting it, because the derivation tells you when it fails.

Consider two species, A and B, in a volume VV, with NAN_{A} and NBN_{B} molecules. Suppose that whenever one A and one B come within a distance aa of each other, they react with some fixed probability. How often does that happen?

Fix attention on one particular A. It sweeps out some volume per unit time as it moves, call it σ\sigma, with units of volume per time. Any B whose centre lies in that swept volume gets hit. The number of B molecules per unit volume is NB/VN_{B}/V, so this one A is hit at a rate

rate for  oneA=σ⋅NB/V\text{\text{rate for } one} A = \sigma \cdot N_{B}/V(16)

There are NAN_{A} of them, and each is hit independently, so the total number of collisions per unit time is σNANB/V\sigma N_{A}N_{B}/V. Divide by VV to express the flux as a concentration per unit time and write [A]=NA/V[A] = N_{A}/V:

v=σ[A][B]≡k[A][B]v = \sigma [A][B] \equiv k[A][B](17)
Three things this derivation tells you that the textbook statement does not
  1. The rate constant is a swept volume per unit time. That is why its units are M−1s−1M^{-1}s^{-1}, and section 17 computes exactly that volume.
  2. The product form comes from independence. If A and B are not independently distributed, because they are tethered, or confined to a membrane, or the cell is not well mixed, then (17) is wrong. Section 11 is what licenses independence inside a bacterium.
  3. The exponents come from how many molecules must meet at once. Two molecules give a product of two concentrations. That is what "elementary" means, and it is why the exponents in an elementary rate law are the stoichiometric coefficients.
1864

Guldberg and Waage stated the law in Christiania in 1864, and their statement is more careful than the version usually attributed to them. They wrote that the force is proportional to the product of the masses "provided each is raised to a particular exponent", explicitly allowing arbitrary exponents, and separately that the action is inversely proportional to the volume, which is what makes it a law about concentrations.7 What they did not say is when the exponents equal the stoichiometry. That took another twenty years and it is the entire content of the next section.

14Elementary, composite, and four traps

The most important distinction in the course, and the four ways people get it wrong.

Definition

A reaction is elementary if the step as written is a single microscopic event: the listed reactants meet, and the listed products result, with nothing intervening. Drawn with a straight arrow. Its rate law is v=k∏i[Xi]αiv = k\prod_{i}[X_{i}]^{\alpha_{i}} with α\alpha the stoichiometric coefficients.

A reaction is composite if it summarises several steps. Drawn with a squiggly arrow, or in net-change form (X1,…,Xn)⇝(X1+γ1,…,Xn+γn)(X_{1},\dots ,X_{n}) \rightsquigarrow (X_{1}+\gamma_{1},\dots ,X_{n}+\gamma_{n}). No rate law follows.

The word people reach for is "molecularity" versus "order". Molecularity is how many molecules take part in one microscopic event, and it is a claim about mechanism. Order is the exponent you measure, and it is a claim about data. They coincide exactly when the step is elementary. Everywhere else they can differ, and often do.

Four traps

  1. Writing mass action under a squiggly arrow. "Transcription is G⇝G+MG \rightsquigarrow G + M, so the rate is k[G]k[G]." That happens to be nearly right, but not because of mass action: it is right because RNA polymerase is in excess and initiation is rate-limiting, and both of those are facts you would have to check. Change the growth condition and the rate law changes.
  2. Assuming a reversible arrow means detailed balance. Drawing A⇌BA \rightleftharpoons B asserts that both directions happen. It does not assert that the system reaches equilibrium, because the cell may be driving one direction with ATP. Lecture 7 is about exactly this.

The ATP is inside the squiggle. If a reaction as written seems to violate thermodynamics, look for what has been hidden.

Three worked examples, from a real paper each, done at increasing difficulty.

This is the exercise that transfers. Take a described biological mechanism and write it as a set of reactions with the arrows correct.

The usual two-line model is composite throughout:

Two ways to beat the bound, and one way to appear to

The bound assumes an unguided three-dimensional search. Break either assumption and you can go faster.

  1. Electrostatic steering. Put opposite charges on the two partners and the substrate falls in rather than wandering in. Barnase associates with barstar at 5×109 M−1s−15\times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1} despite needing a precise alignment.8 The catch, usually omitted: at physiological salt the charge is screened and these rates fall to about 10710^{7}.9
  2. Reduce the dimension of the search. A protein that binds DNA anywhere and then slides along it is searching a line, not a volume. The evidence is unambiguous: EcoRI methyltransferase reaches 5.1×107 M−1s−15.1\times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1} on a 14 bp substrate and four times that on a 429 bp substrate carrying the same site.8 Adding irrelevant DNA makes the target easier to find, which is impossible in three dimensions.
  3. Measure the wrong thing. The lac repressor's famous 1010 M−1s−110^{10}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1} is real, but it has been generalised into a claim about DNA-binding proteins that the data do not support: most protein-DNA associations sit within a factor of three of 10810^{8}, which is the ordinary limit for a small target on a large molecule.9

Facilitated diffusion, and how to recognise it

The search is not three-dimensional. The repressor binds DNA non-specifically anywhere along its length, then slides in one dimension until it reaches the operator or falls off. A 1D search over a large effective target beats a 3D search for a small one, because the protein no longer has to find the operator directly. It has to find the chromosome, which is enormous.

The signature is testable and it is unmistakable. More DNA around a target should make the target easier to find. For a 3D search that is impossible: extra flanking DNA is inert and the rate cannot change. For a 1D search it is automatic, because flanking DNA is extra antenna. EcoRI methyltransferase reaches 5.1×107 M−1s−15.1\times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1} on a 14 bp substrate and about four times that on a 429 bp substrate, for the identical recognition site.8

And the correction the story usually needs

Facilitated diffusion is real, and the generalisation drawn from it is not. Halford and Marko went through the record and found that the early lac experiments used DNA carrying several binding sites and a filter assay that could not resolve which one was occupied. Later footprinting did reproduce 101010^{10} for lac, and Gal repressor and IHF are above 101010^{10} too. But the majority of measured protein-DNA association rates sit within a factor of three of 108 M−1s−110^{8}\,\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-1}, which is exactly equation (27).9 Electrostatic steering is real and is largely screened away at physiological ionic strength.

So the honest statement has three parts, and most retellings keep only the middle one. The bound is sound. A few systems beat it by changing the dimension of the search. "DNA-binding proteins are all impossibly fast" is folklore.

A cell cannot change the concentration of a stable protein faster than it can grow. Not "slowly". Not "in about a generation". Exactly one doubling time, and no amount of transcription helps, because the problem is the removal and not the production. For an E. coli at 40 minutes, halving an enzyme level takes 40 minutes. Even the fastest imaginable route is bounded: 80% of E. coli mRNA half-lives are between 3 and 8 minutes,11 so nothing transcriptional acts in under a few minutes.

Step 4. Compare. Expression: 2400 seconds. The PEP pool: 0.12 seconds. The ratio is 2×1042\times 10^{4}. Even the slowest pool in Table 6 is a hundred times too fast for expression to track.

The conclusion, and it is forced

A cell that regulated metabolism only through expression would empty or overflow its pools thousands of times before the first new enzyme arrived. So a faster regulatory layer is not a design choice, it is a requirement. Look at the timescale ladder in figure 8 and everything fast enough lives in the binding layer.

Now stop short of the usual next sentence, which is “therefore allostery”. The argument forces a layer that moves on the metabolite timescale. It does not say which mechanism occupies it, and the measurement says the answer is mostly not allostery. Hackett and colleagues measured enzymes, metabolites and fluxes across 25 steady-state yeast cultures and partitioned the flux variability across conditions.15 Metabolites beat enzymes by more than two to one, which is what the argument requires. But the metabolite carrying the leverage is mostly the substrate, acting through the ordinary rate law, and for reactions that change net direction across conditions allostery contributes nothing at all. Its share rises only on strongly forward-driven steps.

That repair makes the argument stronger, not weaker. A substrate pool turns over in about a second, so it is inside the gap this section opened. Control through the ordinary rate law is the default and it is free, needing no regulatory apparatus at all, only an enzyme that is not saturated. Allostery is the expensive option, bought on the irreversible committed steps where concentration alone will not do, which is exactly where Umbarger found it in 1956.

And they were found, nine years before anyone made the argument

In 1956 Umbarger showed that isoleucine inhibits the first enzyme of its own biosynthetic pathway, and Yates and Pardee showed the same for pyrimidines.12 Neither product resembles the substrate of the enzyme it shuts down, so the inhibition could not be competition for the active site: there had to be a second site. Monod, Wyman and Changeux named it allostery and gave it a two-state equilibrium model in 1965.13 That the model contains no rate constants at all is not a simplification. It is equation (29).

Five steps you can apply to a system nobody has told you about, and the map of the rest of the term.

  1. Count. Volume, copy number, concentration. Put every species on the ladder in Table 1. This takes ten minutes and it changes what questions are worth asking.
  2. Time it. Mixing time from τ=x2/D\tau = x^{2}/D. Turnover time from pool over flux. Response time from equation (32). Put them on one logarithmic axis, as in figure 8. Gaps in that axis are where the architecture is.
  3. Bound it. Compute 4πDa4\pi Da for the pairs that matter. Anything claimed above it is wrong or is a mechanism.
  4. Check the arrows. For each reaction, elementary or composite? If composite, what would have to be derived or measured?
