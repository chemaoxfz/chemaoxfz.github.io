The reactor: chemical reaction networks

Notation, rates, mass action, and the equation that turns a soup of reactions into a machine — with the diffusion-limited on-rate derived to the last boundary condition.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · week 2 · core page · first draft (built from the 2025 scribe note) · blocks 1–2 Tue 09-08 + blocks 3–4 Thu 09-10

Week 2 is vocabulary and the first equation. Everything later — dynamics (week 3), control (week 4), stochasticity (week 5), energy (week 6), computation (week 7), industry (week 8) — is stated in the language fixed here: what a reaction is, how fast it can go, and how a network of them becomes a dynamical system. This first draft is built directly on the 2025 scribe note of the same lecture (Jiahe Wang, Yiqiao Deng, Zhi Zhou), whose detailed derivation of the diffusion-limited on-rate is the anchor derivation of the course: it is the first place where a biological quantity (the fastest possible reaction rate) is derived from physics, with boundary conditions, and every later derivation is judged against it.

Each block = one 30-minute session taught by one student, followed by the presenter's own TA-approved 5-minute quiz (it must test that block's core point, and anyone who got the session should solve it in ~30 seconds). The four blocks are coordinated by the week's students into one coherent week; the 15-minute addendum closes the Thursday session.

Block 1 · Tuesday

Notations

What a reaction is, and the two arrow vocabularies the course uses.

1How a cell does chemistry

Cellular changes happen through chemical reactions, ultimately governed by diffusion kinetics — at constant temperature and pressure.

A cell changes its state by reactions: molecules meet, decide, and become other molecules. Three properties frame everything. First, cellular chemistry happens at roughly constant temperature and pressure, so free energies, not heats, are the currency (that is week 6's background). Second, the rate at which molecules can meet is set by diffusion — which is why block 2's derivation is the course's first rate estimate.1

Third — the feature that matters most for this week — reactions in cells are catalyzed. Thermodynamically favorable reactions can sit for years on their own (spontaneous glucose degradation, peptide-bond cleavage, ATP hydrolysis), and enzymes accelerate them by factors of 1010 or more. The consequence is a clean separation of two questions, and the course keeps them apart deliberately: whether a reaction can proceed (thermodynamics — week 6) versus how fast it does (kinetics — this week).

2Elementary vs composite

Straight arrows are mechanisms; squiggly arrows are bookkeeping for the net change.

Table 1. The two reaction notations (from the 2025 scribe note).
TypeArrowMeaningExamples
Elementarystraight →implies the reaction mechanism, all key players included; it is an irreducible step — you cannot break it down further, or it is useless for your concernE+S ⇌ C → E+P; Gene+RNAP ⇌ C₀ → C₁ → … → Cₙ → Gene+RNAP+mRNA
Compositesquiggly ⇝may have a hidden mechanism — only the net change of several stepsS ⇝ P; Gene ⇝ Gene+mRNA; (S,P) ⇝ (S−1,P+1)

The third notation is the one the scribe names explicitly: the net-change form, (X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ) → (X₁ + P₁, …, Xₙ + Pₙ), written on molecule counts rather than species symbols. It is the notation that makes the accounting of block 3 automatic, because it registers only who lost and who gained what.

3Four biological examples

The same two arrows, four real systems — and the habit of asking which one a diagram really is.

The 2025 scribe lists four examples worth having on the wall:1

  1. Cell replication: N ⇝ 2N (or N ⇝ N+1) — composite; the mechanism is a whole cell cycle.
  2. Photon doubling in a laser: photon + excited atom → 2 photons + ground atom — elementary in the interesting direction, and one of the few systems where the "elementary" step is genuinely one event.
  3. Diffusion through a passive membrane channel: Aout → Ain — elementary as written (it is a single crossing), but the rate hides transport physics.
  4. ATPase pumping H⁺ out: H⁺in ⇝ H⁺out — composite, and energy-driven: this is exactly the kind of arrow that week 6 (energy) asks about.
The habit to install now

When anyone writes a rate law, ask: is that arrow elementary? If it is composite, the rate law must be derived (week 3's Michaelis–Menten) or measured — never assumed from the arrowhead.

Block 2 · Tuesday

Rates

How fast a reaction can happen: the diffusion-limited on-rate, derived — the anchor derivation of the course.

4Two steps: binding, then catalysis

An enzymatic reaction is binding plus catalysis; the fastest possible rate is the association rate, which is governed by diffusion.

An enzyme and its substrate collide, bind, and then the enzyme's atoms orient the substrate into the product. Two steps, one rate ceiling. The fastest reaction rate is limited by the association step — react immediately upon association — and that association is diffusion-limited, hence the name: diffusion-limited on-rate.1 The scheme:

E + S ⇌ C ⇝ E + P (1)
What "fast" means, in the end

The diffusion limit sets kon ≈ 10⁹ M−1s−1. At 1 mM substrate, one enzyme gets ~10⁶ encounters per second. But most enzymes are far slower — bottlenecked by catalysis at 10–10² reactions per second. Keep both numbers; the gap between them is the whole subject of enzyme engineering, and the answer to most "why isn't this reaction faster?" questions.

5The diffusion-limited on-rate, derived

Fick's law in spherical symmetry, two boundary conditions, one integration chain — and 10⁹ s⁻¹M⁻¹ falls out.

This is the anchor derivation. Set up: an enzyme at the origin, substrate diffusing in a steady state; the enzyme acts as an absorbing sphere of radius a. Step 1 — flux at the surface. At a point on the enzyme surface, Fick's first law:

j(a) = D ∂C/∂R |R=a,   roughly ≈ D C∞/a (2)

Step 2 — conservation gives the profile. In spherical symmetry, steady state means the flux through any sphere is the same: 4πR² j = const ≠ 0. In the steady state of Fick's second law, ∂C/∂t = D∇²C, the angular derivatives vanish and

(1/R²)·d/dR (R² dC/dR) = 0 ⇒ R² dC/dR = A ⇒ C(R) = −A/R + B (3)

Step 3 — boundary conditions. Absorbing surface: C(a) = 0; far away: C(∞) = C∞. So B = C∞, A = aC∞:

C(R) = C∞(1 − a/R) (4)

Step 4 — the gradient at the surface, the flux, the rate. ∂C/∂R|a = C∞/a, so j = D C∞/a, and the total rate is flux × area:

J = j·4πa² = 4πDa C∞ = kon C∞,   kon = 4πDa (5)

Step 5 — the number. With D ≈ 10² µm²/s for a small molecule, a ≈ 1 nm, and the unit conversion for a molecule-per-liter concentration:

kon = 4π · 10² µm²/s · 1 nm · (6×10²³/mol) · (1 L / 10¹⁵ µm³) ≈ 10⁹ s−1M−1 (6)

Note what the derivation does for you: it turns "how fast can a reaction be?" into a Newtonian boundary-value problem — a physical question, forced by the math to have one answer. If a claimed on-rate exceeds 10⁹, it is wrong, and you can say why.

6The units, checked

j = D ∂c/∂R, dimension by dimension — the way every estimate in this course should be checked.

The scribe includes the dimensional check of the flux law, and it is the template for every future estimate:1

Table 2. Dimensional verification of j = D ∂c/∂R.
QuantityDimensions

The habit generalizes: before believing a rate, check its units; before believing a scale, check its exponents. (The same table, with t ≈ x²/κ and J = 4πDaC∞, is what gives week 1's and this week's numbers legs.)

Block 3 · Thursday

Mass action

The one law that turns arrows into equations — and the accounting that holds even when you know nothing about the mechanism.

7A generic reaction, and the accounting

γ = β − α; every time a reaction fires it changes species j by γⱼ·v.

Write a generic reaction — elementary (→) or composite (⇝), n species, reactants αⱼ, products βⱼ:

α₁X₁ + … + αₙXₙ → β₁X₁ + … + βₙXₙ,   γⱼ = βⱼ − αⱼ (7)

The net-stoichiometry γⱼ is the only thing the accounting needs. Every time the reaction fires with flux v, species j changes by γⱼ·v. This is the net-change notation of block 1 made algebraic.

The accounting statement (say it out loud)

The equation of a chemical network is pure bookkeeping: where molecules went. It holds always, for elementary and composite reactions alike, and it depends only on γ — not on α, not on β. You can write it even if you know nothing about the mechanism. What you cannot do without kinetics is close the system: that is exactly what the next section supplies.

8The law of mass action

For an elementary reaction, the flux is k times the product of reactant concentrations raised to their stoichiometries.

Kinetics supplies the flux. For an elementary reaction, the law that organizes all reaction kinetics:

v(x) = k · X₁α₁ X₂α₂ ··· Xₙαₙ = k xα (8)

The rationale is the collision picture: the rate is proportional to how often reactants meet, which scales as the product of their densities. Example from the scribe: E + S → C with rate constant K has rate = K·E·S — and if the step is the elementary binding of an association-limited reaction, then that K is exactly kon ≈ 4πDa from block 2: the same quantity, one derived from physics, one assumed from the arrow. That agreement is the reason the course calls (5) an anchor: the law of mass action's constants are not free — block 2's physics caps them.

9The CRN rate equation

dx/dt = Γv = f⁺ − f⁻: a network's dynamics, split into production and consumption — the form every later week starts from.

Combine accounting (7) with kinetics (8). For m reactions, n species, the network obeys1

dxⱼ/dt = Σi γij vi = Σi: γ>0 γijvi − Σi: γ<0 |γij| vi = f+j(x) − f−j(x)

in matrix form

dx/dt = Γ v(x) = Γ Λk xα = f+(x) − f−(x) (9)

with Γ the n×m net-stoichiometry matrix and Λk the diagonal matrix of rate constants. Three immediate consequences:

  • Conservation. Any vector c with cTΓ = 0 is conserved; closed networks have such quantities — the accounting's invariant.
  • Steady states. Γv(x*) = 0. Note the split: structure (Γ) and kinetics (v) each constrain the fixed point separately, which is why week 7 can say surprising things about what a network's shape alone allows.
  • What (9) is not. It is a proper subset of arbitrary dynamical systems. Positivity (the orthant is forward-invariant), polynomiality and parameter uncertainty are the structural rules week 3 encodes as [P], [CP], [PU].
Week 2 quiz ideas (presenters, pick one)

(a) Given the scheme E+S ⇌ C → E+P, write Γ and v for the elementary steps and name the one that is composite. (b) A claimed on-rate of 10¹¹ M⁻¹s⁻¹ — why is it wrong? (c) Which species are conserved in a dimerization 2A ⇌ A₂ and what does (9) say about their sum? Each is 30 seconds for someone who got the block.

Block 4 · Thursday

Worked models

Three ODEs that carry the course, and the toolkit that solves them.

10Three ODEs that carry the course

Production–degradation, the TXTL circuit, and the S-shaped bistable — every later week is a variation on these.

The 2025 handwritten notes develop the gallery in order:2

ẋ = μ − x (10)

Production–degradation (the scribe's Exp 4). Fixed point x* = μ; relaxation with timescale 1. Nothing else happens — and that "nothing else" is the null model of every gene-expression circuit.

dM/dt = kMG − γMM,   dP/dt = kHM − γPP (11)

The TXTL-style circuit — transcription (with inducible rate) and translation, both diluted/degraded. Two variables, one matrix; its stability analysis is week 3's first exercise.

ẋ = f+(x) − x,   f+ an S-shaped (Hill-type) production term (12)

The bistable construction. With an S-shaped production, (12) has three fixed points: two stable, one unstable. This is the toggle switch's skeleton — and week 3 will say exactly how the S-shape can arise from a CRN (cooperativity, or the saturation/bottleneck/ultrasensitivity regimes of one binding reaction).

11The simulation toolkit

Deterministic solvers first; the CRN zoo next; Gillespie in week 5.

"Let's see" means "let's solve" from here on. Deterministic ODEs (any stiff-aware solver; the 2025 course's scribes and code are published with each lecture) for weeks 2–4; the Gillespie algorithm enters in week 5 when copy numbers get small. The zoo to keep on hand: birth–death (10), dimerization 2A ⇌ A₂ (the first nonlinearity and a conservation check on (9)), autocatalysis, the enzyme module (1), and the inducible/repressible genes of (11). Every model in the zoo should be written as (9) first: Γ, then v.

12What networks can do

The teaser that opens week 3 — and the reason this week's vocabulary is worth the effort.

With (9) in hand, the course's first big question appears: what can a network do? The CRN equation is a constrained dynamical system, so its behaviors are restricted — but within the restrictions lie bistability, oscillations, adaptation, computation. The lecturer's addendum this week states the question cleanly: week 3 will map what (9) can draw (phase portraits, stability, time-scale separation), week 4 will ask how a network adapts, week 5 how it behaves when the counts are small, and week 7 what it computes. Every one of those questions starts with the same two sentences: write Γ; write v.
