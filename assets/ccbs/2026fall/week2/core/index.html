The reactor: chemical reaction networks

Notation, rates, mass action, and the equations that turn a soup of reactions into a machine.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · week 2 · core page · 4 student blocks

Week 2 is vocabulary and the first equation. Everything the course later says about dynamics (week 3), control (week 4), stochasticity (week 5), energy (week 6), computation (week 7) and industry (week 8) is stated in the language fixed here: what a reaction is, how fast it goes, and how a network of them becomes a dynamical system. The 2025 lecture covered this in one sitting1; the four blocks of week 2 are the same material, one point per block.

Blocks and sessions

Each block = one 30-minute session taught by one student, followed by the presenter's own TA-approved 5-minute quiz (it must test that block's core point, and anyone who got the session should solve it in ~30 seconds). The four blocks are coordinated by the week's students into one coherent week; the 15-minute addendum closes the week, in Thursday's session.

Block 1

Notations

What a reaction is, and the two arrow vocabularies the course uses.

1How a cell does chemistry

Changes in cells happen via chemical reactions, whose kinetics are ultimately governed by diffusion.

A cell changes its state by reactions: molecules meet, decide, and become other molecules. Two facts set the frame. First, cellular chemistry happens at roughly constant temperature and pressure — so free energies, not heats, are the currency, and equilibrium questions (week 6) are the right background. Second, the rate at which molecules can meet is set by diffusion — which is the very first rate estimate in the course (block 2) and by which every enzyme must abide.

The base notation is simple: E + S ⇌ C → E + P is a mechanism — the straight arrow means the reaction actually proceeds through that collision. Kinetic detail is what distinguishes an elementary reaction from a summary. 1

2Elementary vs composite

Straight arrows are mechanisms; squiggly arrows are bookkeeping for net change.

Elementary reaction

One step, straight arrow: E + S ⇌ C. The arrow is the mechanism; stoichiometry and rate law follow from the arrow.

Composite reaction

A summary of several steps, squiggly arrow: S ⇝ P (or, tracking counts, (S,P) ⇝ (S−1, P+1), mRNA ⇝ mRNA+1). Only the net change is claimed; the mechanism is not.

The distinction runs the whole course.1 An elementary reaction's rate law is forced by the arrow (block 3). A composite reaction's rate law must be measured or derived from the steps — Michaelis–Menten (block 4) is the first derived rate law; Hill functions and ultrasensitive fronts (week 3) are composite behaviors built from elementary steps. Whenever a claim says "the rate is…," the first question to ask is: is that arrow elementary?

Block 2

Rates

How fast can a reaction actually happen: the diffusion limit, and the catalytic reality below it.

3The diffusion-limited encounter

The fastest possible bimolecular reaction is set by diffusion: k_on ≈ 10⁹ s⁻¹M⁻¹ — every enzyme starts here and can only go slower.

Estimate the upper bound on a bimolecular rate. Put a sphere of radius a (the enzyme or molecule) in a sea of substrate at concentration C. Molecules diffuse; any molecule that reaches the surface reacts. The flux at the surface is the diffusive flux J = D ∂C/∂R; solving the boundary-value problem on the sphere gives the classic result1

J = 4πDaC∞ = konC∞,    kon = 4πDa ≈ 109 s−1M−1 (1)

with D the diffusion constant of the substrate and a the reaction radius. The number itself is the lesson: no bimolecular association in a cell can beat 109 M−1s−1; real rates sit at or below it, and when a claimed on-rate exceeds it, the claim is wrong. With C ~ 1 mM, this says an enzyme can receive up to ~106 encounters per second — the full unit chain is worth doing once by hand: (109 s−1M−1)(10−3 M) = 106 s−1.

4Catalysis and the real rates

Enzymes receive millions of encounters per second and convert tens per second: catalysis, not diffusion, is the bottleneck.

An enzyme that could react with every encounter would be a perfect enzyme — but even the fastest enzymes turn over at 10–102 reactions per second. The gap of ~104–105 is catalysis: binding must be specific, the catalytic step has to happen, products must leave. This one OoM fact, plus the encounter limit, sets the definition of "fast" the course will use: 1

Units are the answer

Every rate estimate lives or dies on its unit chain. Practice the chain until it is reflexive: M−1s−1 × M = s−1, volume 1 µm³ = 10−15 L, mass 1 pg = 10−12 g.

Block 3

Mass action

The one law that turns arrows into equations.

5The law of mass action

The rate of an elementary reaction is k times the product of reactant concentrations raised to their stoichiometries.

For an elementary reaction with reactant stoichiometry αi (the exponent vector), the law of mass action says

vi = ki xαi = ki ∏ xjαij (2)

The law is the "central organizing law of reaction kinetics": rate ∝ how often reactants collide, which scales as the product of their densities. It is what the course means by a well-mixed assumption — week 5's master equation relaxes it.

6The CRN rate equation

A whole network is dx/dt = Γv: the stoichiometric matrix times the flux vector. Conservation and steady states follow.

Collect species x, reactions with fluxes v, net stoichiometry γj = βj − αj (products minus reactants), and the network becomes1

dx/dt = Γ v(x) = Γ Λk xα = f+(x) − f−(x) (3)

where Λk is the diagonal matrix of rate constants. Two consequences are immediate. First, conservation: the left-null space of Γ contains the conserved quantities; if the network is closed, Σx stays put. Second, steady states: Γv(x*) = 0 — the equation that week 3 will analyze for stability. Note also what (3) is not: it is a subset of an arbitrary dynamical system ẋ = f(x). Polynomiality, positivity (the cone x ≥ 0 is forward-invariant) and parameter uncertainty constrain what a cell can realize — this is the structural rules story of week 3, block 3.

Block 4

Worked models

Three models that carry the course: production–degradation, the TXTL circuit, and the derived Michaelis–Menten law.

7Production, degradation, bistability

One ODE is biology's harmonic oscillator: everything later is a variation on it.

Start with the workhorse1 — protein production at rate k, degradation at rate λ:

dP/dt = k − λP (4)

Steady state P* = k/λ; relaxation with timescale 1/λ. Add a second layer — the TXTL-style circuit (transcription with inducible rate, translation with dilution),

dM/dt = kMG − γMM,   dP/dt = kHM − γPP (5)

— and the machinery for week 3 is assembled: two variables, one matrix, and a qualitative question (when is the fixed point stable?). The third example of this block is the construction of a 1D bistable system, ẋ = f+(x) − x with an S-shaped production term — the general shape behind every toggle switch, and the bridge to week 3's bifurcation language.

The block's exercise

Simulate (4) and (5); then add a Hill term plus a delay-less S-shape and find the bistable window by hand: f+(x) = β xn/(Kn+xn). Where (in β) does the middle fixed point appear? This is the same computation week 3 does with phase portraits.

8Michaelis–Menten from rates

The first derived rate law: three elementary steps, one quasi-steady assumption, one effective law.

The canonical composite: E + S ⇌ C → E + P. Under the quasi-steady-state assumption on the complex, the binding equilibrium gives C = ES/K with K = (koff+kcat)/kon, and the quadratic solves to1

C = ½ [ (Etot+Stot+K) ± √((Etot+Stot+K)² − 4EtotStot) ] (6)

with rate v = kcatC. The two branches of (6) pick out the two dominance regimes of the reaction — substrate-excess and enzyme-excess — and the transition between them is exactly the kind of thing week 3 handles as time-scale separation and week 7 sees in binding-based computation. The deep point: a composite reaction's rate law is derived, not assumed, and this derivation is the template.

9The simulation toolkit

Deterministic solvers first; the CRN zoo next; the Gillespie jump goes with week 5.

For the rest of the course, "let's see" means "let's solve" — deterministic ODEs with any stiff-capable solver (scipy, ROSENBROCK-style) for weeks 2–4, and the Gillespie algorithm (week 5) when copy numbers get small. The zoo to keep on hand: birth–death (4), dimerization 2A ⇌ A₂ (the first nonlinearity and the start of unit-problems in stoichiometry), autocatalysis, the MM module (6), and the repressible/ inducible gene expressions of (5). During this block, each student should bring one CRN simulation with a number they can defend: that is the format of the week's exercises and of every later week.

Week 2 extension micro-essay

One of: (a) estimate + verify the turnover rate of your favorite enzyme against the 109 limit; (b) derive the rate law of a composite reaction of your choice and show which regime it lives in; (c) build a CRN whose steady state is a conserved quantity the naive observer wouldn't see. Same format: one example, one number made by code, one source.
