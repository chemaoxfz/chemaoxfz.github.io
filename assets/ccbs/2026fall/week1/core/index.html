Order of magnitude: numbers can say a lot

Week 1 of CCBS 2026. Estimate fast, estimate robustly, and the world starts to look constrained — from evacuating a city to mixing a metabolite inside a cell.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · week 1 · core page · lecturer-taught · blocks 0–3 · tue 09-01 (0–1) + thu 09-03 (2–3)

This page is the lecture. It is also the exemplar: every week after this one produces a page like it — a core digest of the week's one lecture, split into four 30-minute blocks (week 1: an introductory block 0 plus three content blocks), each block teachable as a single session and quizzed by its presenter. Block 0 tells you how the course works. Blocks 1–3 are the first content lecture, Order-of-Magnitude (OoM) reasoning: the skill the whole course leans on, because every later question — how fast a reaction can be, whether noise matters, how much energy regulation costs — is a numbers question first.

How to read this page

Blocks are labeled with their session. Each section is one teachable unit of a block: a claim, its derivation, and an estimate. Numbers are bookkept as the lecturer's notes and the 2025 scribes had them; where the handwritten notes and the scribe differ by a factor of 2–3, the order of magnitude is the claim (that is the point of the lesson). Claims are cited to the 2025 lecture note and its scribe note at the bottom.

Block 0 · Tuesday

The course

Rules, the three schools, the cycle, the session anatomy, and how excellence is measured.

1What this course is

Physics, system, industry: three schools asking three questions about life, and one course whose tools aim at all three.

CCBS reads a cell as a machine you can reason about quantitatively. Three schools frame the reasoning, each asking its own question. Physics asks what life is as an object: sizes, rates, energies, and the constraints they impose (weeks 1 and 6). System asks how life works as a machine: reaction networks, dynamics, feedback, stochasticity, computation (weeks 2–5 and 7). Industry asks how life could be useful as a tool: design a protein, engineer a metabolism, grow a machine (week 8). Each school has its own origins, its own analysis style, and its own goals — the course's promise is that you will leave able to reason about any biological problem with all three at once.

Nothing needs prior background classes — only "an exuberant love for biology," which is mandatory. What the course does demand is a different relationship to material than you may be used to: in 2026 the raw material for learning is available to everyone — an AI agent can digest a paper, write first-pass code, and draft a figure on demand — so the course moves the emphasis to what an agent cannot do: understand, defend, and communicate.

2The 2026 operating cycle

Input → learn → output, run weekly, with an agent in the loop and the audience as the judge.

ask AI to digest and write → understand and use it to work and iterate → present to others to communicate

Every week runs this loop. Input: the lecturer's core page and its pointers, posted a week ahead. Learn: digest it with an agent — then check every claim against the core, and make the numbers with code rather than quoting them. Output: the teaching students present (one block each), the class answers comprehension quizzes, and every non-teaching student posts a short extension micro-essay: one example, one scenario, one opinion, one perspective, one argument — traceably sourced, not a summary. Conflicting positions are welcome (the TAs assemble the week's micro-essays into the week's extension-by-audience page, keeping the conflicts visibly in conversation).

The four commandments (research-with-AI)
  • AI is our minion, not our peer. Own every claim, or do not use it. If you cannot explain it, it is vapor.
  • AI is a cyber-arm. Attach it to the work you care about most, not the chores you dislike. If an idea is active in your mind, put it into the loop before it cools.
  • Your input–output bandwidth is the bottleneck — not the model's power. Specification in, comprehension out.
  • Be above AI on everything you think about. Generic AI output is the floor, not the ceiling. Know the generic answer before claiming your own expertise.
Tools you will actually use

The Westlake AI Campus (hpc.westlake.edu.cn/#/ai-campus) hosts the university's internal model deployments (DeepSeek, GLM, and more) — use these as your agents. The research-essay (idea-to-essay) skill is the reference pipeline for every digest: idea → thesis → literature sweep with verified full texts → numbers from code → a single self-contained HTML page. The course submissions repo (ccbs-2026-submissions) collects each week's exposition, quizzes, and extension micro-essays.

3The anatomy of a session

30 minutes of teaching, 5 minutes of quiz, 15 minutes of addendum; a two-session week — blocks 1–2 Tuesday, blocks 3–4 Thursday.

The course runs Tuesdays and Thursdays, 10:40–12:15 (two 95-minute sessions per week, E10-305): blocks 1–2 on Tuesday, blocks 3–4 on Thursday. From week 2, each block is one point of the lecture, taught by one student as a 30-minute session, immediately followed by a 5-minute quiz written by that session's presenter and approved by the TAs before class — approval means it tests the block's core point, and anyone who got the material solves it in about 30 seconds. The class and the lecturer rate each session 0–3 (next section). If the lecturer has to step in and teach because the quality did not hold, that is the designed fallback: the material is covered anyway, and the group retakes a condensed version with the TAs. The week's Thursday session ends with the lecturer's 15-minute addendum: gaps, the thing that matters most, take-aways.

Block 1 · Tuesday

The method

Why order-of-magnitude reasoning is a way of seeing connections — and the city-size example that makes the method obvious.

5What OoM reasoning is

Numbers can say a lot, but only when they are robust; details are fragile, orders of magnitude are not.

The world is connected. Observations that seem unrelated can be deeply constrained — the size of the Earth was estimated from a solar eclipse, centuries before anyone stood on a pole. The instrument that sees such connections is order-of-magnitude reasoning: estimate the quantity, keep only the power of ten, and let the constraint do the arguing.1

Order of magnitude

An estimate right to within a factor — we keep 1 or 10, not more. The claim is of the form "it's about 106 per second," never "approximately 1.3×106."

Two properties make OoM the right first tool. First, it is fast: do it in your head, mid-conversation. Second, and more important, it is robust: the factors that dominate an estimate are usually few, so the answer survives small errors in every detail — while a precisely written answer suggests a precision it does not have. The lecture's shorthand for this: details are fragile; orders of magnitude are not. The practice of the skill is the seeing: the goal is not the answer, it is the constraint that becomes visible once numbers are put in.

6Hangzhou: 10⁷ people out

Evacuating a city by plane or by car — the same flux formula answers both, and the bottleneck, not the vehicle, decides.

The first worked example is macroscopic and familiar: evacuate all of Hangzhou (~107 people). The question is framed as a choice of transport, but the method is: name the bottleneck.

Option 1 — airplane

One airport. Emergency loading: a plane leaves every ~2 minutes, so on the order of 103 minutes per day divided by 2 — about 5–7×102 planes per day. A plane carries ~102 people:

2 min/plane → ~5×102 planes/day → ~102 people/plane → ~5×104 people/day (1)

For 107 people: 107 / 5×104 ≈ 2×102 days. Too long for an apocalypse — and note the reason: the airplanes are fast, the airport is not. The bottleneck is the number of runways.

Option 2 — cars

Count the lanes instead. Roughly ~10 main highways out of the city; an emergency state makes all lanes one-directional; typical 4 lanes per way, 8 per highway (~102 lanes total). Now a lane is a pipe with three numbers: speed, spacing, capacity —

Flux = N·v/L = (4 people/car × 20 m/s) / (40 m/car) = 2 people/s per lane (2)

102 lanes × 2 people/s × 3.6×103 s/h × 24 h/day ≈ 2×107 people/day — the city evacuates in about a day. The same formula (2) that condemned the airport saves the highway; the difference is the number of parallel pipes.

The actual bottleneck: τ, the reaction time

Now the deeper move. Is the number of cars a limit? Could be — but does it matter? Rewrite the lane flux in terms of the safety spacing: the gap between cars is not a design choice; it is a consequence of a fixed human reaction time τ, through L = vτ:

Flux = N·v/L = N/τ,   with τ fixed by human reflexes (3)

Once flux is N/τ, the vehicle disappears from the arithmetic — walking at 1 m/s with 2 m spacing gives the same people-per-second. The conclusion, which is exactly the lecture's first lesson about scaling: the constraint is the human reaction time, and it is the same for every vehicle; the geometry of the problem, not the technology, decides the answer. So: in an emergency, walk on the highway.1

The method, in three steps

1) Set up the formula with the quantities you can estimate (flux = Nv/L). 2) Find the bottleneck by scaling the geometry (one airport vs 100 lanes). 3) Replace fragile details with the fixed quantity (τ) and rewrite — the vehicle drops out, and what survives is the constraint.

Block 2 · Thursday

The numbers

Scaling laws and the cell's ledger: what stays constant, how much stuff a cell has, and why crowding is a design constraint.

7Jump heights and the t = L/v limit

h ∝ M⁰: muscle energy scales with body volume, so jump height is size-independent — but the time to deliver it is not.

Second example: how does jump height scale with animal size? A student jumps ~40–60 cm above their reach; an athlete, a bit more — within humans, no change across orders of magnitude. Now the dimensional argument.1 Energy to reach height h: Eg = mgh. Energy stored in muscle: muscle is a fixed volume fraction fm of the body, and body mass scales as volume, m ∝ L³, so Em ∝ L³ fm ∝ m fm. Set equal, cancel:

mgh = Em ∝ m fm ⇒ h ∝ M0 fm (4)

Jump height is independent of mass, volume, or size — only the muscle fraction matters, as long as the same muscle mechanism is used. Data roughly confirm: rat ~50 cm, flea ~25 cm, cat ~100–150 cm: same order of magnitude.

The time limit that drives fleas to cheat

Since h is fixed, the takeoff speed v = √(2gh) is the same for all of them (~3 m/s). What changes is how long the body has to deliver that speed: τ ∝ L/v — the energy is released over roughly the animal's own length.

Table 3. The release-time limit (v ≈ 3 m/s).
AnimalLτ = L/vFast muscles (~0.1 s half-contraction)
human1 m≈ 0.3 sfine
cat0.2 m≈ 0.1 spushing the limit
flea2 mm≈ 1 msimpossible

The flea's resolution, per the lecture: the body is too slow, so the energy is stored in the shell — bent shells release in microseconds. And one more estimate closes the question: does air resistance matter? Drag is Fd ≈ ½ Cd ρa v² L²; over the stopping length ~L it wastes FdL ~ ρav²L³ against total energy ~ ρmL³v² — so drag matters only when the jump length reaches ~ρm/ρa ≈ 10³ body lengths. A flea jumps ~200 body lengths; it is safe.1

The fragile part

The conclusion (4) is a scaling law and it is robust. The mechanism ("muscle energy ∝ volume") is the fragile part: a different mechanism (shells, tendons, springs) breaks the scaling — which is exactly what the flea's case demonstrates. When you present a scaling result, name the assumption that made it, and what would break it. That is what the week's quiz will test.

8The cell in numbers

The ledger every later lecture draws on: volumes, masses, concentrations, crowd ing — built from three estimates and one unit convention.

OoM reasoning turns inward, to the cell — the domain of the whole course. Three estimates structure everything: size, mass, count. Start with the length ladder 1: an atom is ~1 Å (10−10 m), a cell ~1 µm (10−6 m), a human ~1 m. The ladder itself is the first lesson: a bacterium is to a human what an atom is to a bacterium.

Size and volume

Table 4. The length ladder and cell sizes.

atom1 Å = 10−10 mthe bottom of our ladder
E. coli1 µmour standard reference

human1 mthe top of our ladder
1 µm³ = 10−18 m³ = 10−15 L = 1 fL   ✓   1 pg = 10−12 g (5)

Two consequences of the volume in (5). First, a pellet of saturated E. coli culture is ~1 µL for ~109 cells — 1 mL of culture medium is ~10−3 cells by volume, less dense than our classroom. Second, in this volume, "one molecule per cell" is already a concentration: 1/10−15L / 6×1023 ≈ 10−9 M = 1 nM. This single conversion is the seed of week 5: at 1 nM, discreteness is a property of the system.

Mass per molecule: the log-average trick

A glucose/nucleotide is ~3×102 Da (size ~ (3×10²)1/3 ≈ 6 Å). An amino acid is ~102 Da — because the true range is ~60–200 Da, and the average over a log scale is √(60×200) ≈ 10². The aside is worth stopping on: orders of magnitude are the right thing to average when quantities span them.

How many proteins per cell?

  • Protein length: lower bound ~50 aa, upper bound ~5×10³ aa → geometric mean √(2×105) ≈ 300 aa ≈ 30 kDa.
  • 1 pg / 30 kDa ≈ 2×107 protein molecules per E. coli — the upper bound if the whole cell were protein. Reality: 103–104 per protein type.

Volume: the genome is small, proteins are not

The genome: 5×106 bp × 0.3 nm/bp ≈ 103 µm of DNA — long enough to need folding ≥103-fold to fit. Its volume, though, is ~1.5×10−3 µm³ ≈ 1% of the cell. Proteins: 300 aa × ~100 Å³ × 107 molecules ≈ 0.3 µm³ — 20–40% of the cell's volume. The asymmetry is the lesson: the genome is information (small volume, long length), the protein is machinery (large volume, crowded). Crowding puts a cap on how many components you can pack, and it is one of the reasons cells are not dilute solutions.1

Take-away of block 2

Three numbers do most of the work: 1 fL (the volume), 30 kDa (the typical protein), 1 µM ≈ 1/fL (the regulator scale). Everything else in this course — 10⁹ encounter rates, 10⁻³/s degradation, 1 nM switch thresholds — is these three numbers times a scaling law.

Block 3 · Thursday

The scales

Heat and diffusion do one thing everywhere: t ≈ x²/κ. It is the single most useful scaling in the course.

9Heat: from the equation to t ≈ x²/κ

Fourier's law, energy conservation, one PDE, and the scaling that survives: double the size, quadruple the time.

Third example: heating a potato — and then, with the same constant, the cooling of the moon. The derivation, in four steps.1

Step 1: the flux law

Heat flux is proportional to the local temperature gradient (Fourier):

J = −K ∇T,   ∇T ≈ δT/δx (6)

with K the thermal conductivity. Stored energy per volume per degree: ΔE ≈ Cv δT.

Step 2: energy conservation on a slab

In a segment of length δx, the net influx changes the internal energy:

δx Cv δT = (Jx−1 − Jx+1) δt ⇒ ∂T/∂t = κ ∇²T,   κ = K/Cv (7)

Step 3: the scaling

Drop the partial derivatives (that is legitimate: the scaling is a statement about the units of one step):

T/t ≈ κ T/x² ⇒ t ≈ x²/κ (8)

The heat equation's two parameters, K and Cv, collapse into one — κ, the heat diffusivity — and only one functional form survives: quadratic in size.

Step 4: calibrate κ with a potato, then cool the moon

A 10 cm potato takes ~10 min to cook through:

κ ≈ x²/t = (10−1 m)² / 600 s ≈ 1.5×10−5 m²/s (9)

Now the moon (L ≈ 2×106 m):

t ≈ x²/κ = 4×1012 / 1.5×10−5 ≈ 3×1017 s ≈ 1010 years (10)

with ~3×107 s/year. The solar system is ~4.6×109 years old — so the estimate says the moon should be nearly, but not quite, cooled. Reality: the moon is fully cooled. The discrepancy is also the lesson: the moon's molten core, early on, enabled convection — a much faster transport mechanism than conduction — and the estimate's failure mode tells you the mechanism. A calibrated constant plus a scaling law gives you a prediction that is quantitatively close and mechanistically honest about where it is wrong.

10Diffusion and the mixing of a cell

Brownian motion is a random walk: τ ≈ x²/D with D ∝ 1/r — and the mixing times that follow run the whole course.

The same random-walk logic, in biological currency. Brown (1827) watched pollen jiggle under a microscope with no apparent force. Estimate its size: visible under a 1800s microscope, not visible to the naked eye → ~10 µm (eukaryotic-cell scale). Its motion: visible jiggling over seconds (~1 µm on seconds timescale), but not significant net movement over minutes → to move one body length (10 µm) takes about an hour:

Dpollen ≈ x²/τ = (10 µm)² / 3600 s ≈ 10−2 µm²/s (11)

Now for the scaling that turns this into biology. Diffusion is a balance between the thermal kicks (∝ L², since it scales with the surface being hit) and viscous drag (F ∝ η L v), so mobility per force is v/F ∝ L−1, and Einstein's relation gives

D = μ kBT,   μ ∝ v/F ∝ L−1 ⇒ D ∝ 1/L (12)

A protein is ~1 nm (from (300 aa)1/3-ish size scalings), 104-fold smaller than pollen, so Dprotein ≈ 100 µm²/s. Then the mixing times, which the rest of the course quotes:

Table 6. τ = x²/D for a protein (D ≈ 100 µm²/s): the size scaling of mixing.
CompartmentSizeτWhat it means

The axon row is the payoff: since τ ∝ x², mass transport by diffusion dies at large distances, and the existence of axonal transport (and vesicles, and motors) is predicted by this scaling rather than assumed. That is what a null model is for.

11Null models, and the ten-questions game

In biology, an OoM estimate is a null hypothesis: how far does plain physics take you before any design is needed?

The last point of the lecture is the mode in which OoM reasoning is used in biology: not to calculate, but to generate null hypotheses. Before asking why a cell regulates something, estimate what plain physics would do without regulation: "would two molecules at 1 µM meet often enough?" — about 106 encounters per second per enzyme, because kon ≈ 4πDa ≈ 109 M−1s−1 (that estimate, derived properly, is week 2's first derivation). "How long to transport a protein across a neuron?" — forever; therefore motors exist. "Is this state stable?" — before the ODE, ask: what is the timescale? A naked reaction with a 100 ms timescale is going nowhere unless something — catalysis, concentration, localization — does the work. The number says what needs explaining, and the null hypothesis says the explanation is cheap.

The ten-questions game

The block's exercise, in the demo format: ten questions, three minutes each, answers to within a factor of ten. Sample: what is the mass of all the RNA in a human cell? How many ATP molecules in a cell at any instant? How long is one transcription cycle? How many metabolic reactions fit in one cell? Method, always: name the scale, then the bottleneck, then the answer. One of these is done live in the demo; the class rates it 0–3 on the rubric; the remaining nine are the discussion material.
