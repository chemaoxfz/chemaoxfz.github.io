Order of magnitude: numbers can say a lot

Week 1 of CCBS 2026. Estimate fast, estimate robustly, and the world starts to look constrained — from evacuating a city to mixing a metabolite inside a cell.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · core page · lecturer-taught (week 1) · blocks 0–3

This page is the core for week 1: everything a student needs before build discussion. This page spans the first week's two sessions — blocks 0–1 on Tuesday 09-01, blocks 2–3 on Thursday 09-03. Block 0 tells you how the course works. Blocks 1–3 are the content of the first lecture, Order-of-Magnitude (OoM) reasoning, taught now and used for the rest of the course: what a reaction costs, whether noise matters, how fast a cell can respond — those questions are all OoM questions first.

Block 0

The course

Rules, the three schools, the cycle, and the anatomy of every session.

1What this course is

Physics, system, industry: three schools asking three questions about life, and this course's tools aim at all three.

CCBS reads a cell as a machine you can reason about quantitatively. Three schools frame the reasoning, and each asks its own question. Physics asks what life is as an object: sizes, rates, energies, and the constraints they impose. System asks how life works as a machine: reaction networks, dynamics, feedback, stochasticity, computation. Industry asks how life could be useful as a tool: design a protein, engineer a metabolism, grow a machine.

Nothing in the course needs prior background classes — only "an exuberant love for biology," which is mandatory. What the course does ask is that you treat skills and numbers as things you can build, because in 2026 the raw material for learning is available to everyone: an AI agent can digest a paper, write first-pass code, and produce a draft figure on demand. What it cannot do is understand, defend, and communicate. That part is yours, and it is the whole course.

2The 2026 operating cycle

Input → learn → output, run weekly, with an agent in the loop and the audience as the judge.

ask AI to digest and write → understand and use it to work and iterate → present to others to communicate

Every week runs this loop. Input: the lecturer's core page and the pointers on it. Learn: you digest it with an agent, check every claim against the core, and make numbers with code rather than quoting them. Output: the teaching students present (30 minutes per block), the whole class answers a comprehension quiz per block, and every non-teaching student posts a short extension micro-essay. The extension micro-essay is not a summary: one example, one scenario, one opinion, one perspective, one argument, traceably sourced. Conflicting positions are welcome — the TAs assemble the week's micro-essays into one extension-by-audience page, conflicts kept visibly in conversation.

The four commandments (research-with-AI)
  • AI is our minion, not our peer. Own every claim, or do not use it.
  • AI is a cyber-arm. Attach it to what you care about, not the chores you dislike.
  • Your input–output bandwidth is the bottleneck. Specify well, comprehend fully.
  • Be above AI on everything you think about. Generic output is the floor, not the ceiling.

3The anatomy of a session

30 minutes of teaching, 5 minutes of quiz, 15 minutes of addendum; the lecture that gets rated is the lecture that gets quizzed.

From week 2, each week has four blocks. Each block is one point of the lecture and is taught by one student in a 30-minute session, immediately followed by a 5-minute quiz written by that session's presenter and approved by the TAs before class (approval means: it tests the block's core point, and anyone who got the material can solve it in about 30 seconds). The class and the lecturer rate every session 0–3: 3 excellent, 2 the audience got it, 1 the key ideas landed but details vanished, 0 the lecturer steps in and teaches it instead — that fallback is a design feature, not a failure. Blocks 1–2 are taught in Tuesday's session and blocks 3–4 in Thursday's (both 10:40–12:15); each block is followed by its quiz, and the week's Thursday session ends with the lecturer's 15-minute addendum: gaps, the thing that matters most, take-aways.

Block 1

The method

OoM reasoning: fast, robust estimates; order-of-magnitude is the unit, not the answer.

4What OoM reasoning is

Numbers can say a lot, but only when they are robust; details are fragile, orders of magnitude are not.

The world is connected. Observations that seem unrelated can be deeply constrained — the size of the Earth was estimated from a solar eclipse, centuries before anyone stood on a pole. The instrument that sees such connections is order-of-magnitude reasoning: estimate the quantity, keep only the power of ten, and let the constraint do the arguing.1

Order of magnitude

An estimate that is right to within a factor — typically we keep 1 or 10, not more. The claim to make is of the form "it's about 106 per second," never "approximately 1.3×106."

Two properties make OoM the right first tool. First, it is fast: you can do it in your head, mid-conversation. Second, and more important, it is robust: the factors that dominate an estimate are usually few, so the answer survives small errors in every detail — while striking a precise answer suggests precision it does not have. Details are fragile; orders of magnitude are not. The lecture's heading for this is 1, 10.1

5Hangzhou: 107 people out

Evacuating a city by plane or by car — the same flux formula answers both, and scaling tells you cars win.

The first worked example is macroscopic: evacuate all of Hangzhou (~107 people). Option one, airplane. The bottleneck is the airport — one plane at a time. One plane every two minutes holds 300 people, so 1

2×105 people/day ≈ 60·24 × 300/2 (1)

and the whole city takes roughly 50 days. Option two, cars. Count the lanes: about 10 highways, ~10 lanes each in an emergency → 100 lanes. Each lane is a flux pipe:

Flux = N·v / L = (#people per car × speed) / (distance between cars) (2)

With 4 people per car, 20 m/s, and 40 m between cars, one lane moves 2 people per second — again ~2×105 per day, but per lane, so 100 lanes move ~2×107 people per day: the city evacuates in about a day.1

The lesson

The same arithmetic (1) appears twice with opposite conclusions; the difference is the bottleneck, found by scaling the geometry, not by refining numbers. And once the flux is written as N/τ — N people per car over τ, a reaction time that nature fixes — the answer stops depending on the vehicle: walking on the highway at 1 m/s with 2 m spacing evacuates the city too.

Block 2

The numbers

Scaling laws and the cell's ledger: what stays constant, what changes, and how much stuff a cell actually has.

6Jump height does not care

h ∝ M⁰: muscle energy scales with body volume, and the height reached is independent of animal size.

Second example: how does jump height scale with animal size? Energy to reach height h: Eg = Mgh. Energy stored in muscle: the muscle volume is a fixed fraction of the body, so Em ∝ L³ ∝ M. Equate, cancel:1

M g h = Em ∝ M fm ⇒ h ∝ M0 fm (3)

Jump height is independent of size (for the same muscle mechanism). Data fit: rat 50 cm, flea 25 cm, cat 150 cm. But the time to take the jump scales as τ ∝ L/v — the body must deliver its stored energy over its own length at a fixed speed. Human: ~0.3 s, fine. Cat: pushing the limit. Flea: ~0.6 ms — too fast for muscles.1 The resolution: the flea stores energy in its shell first. Air resistance only bites when the jump length reaches ~10³ body lengths, since the energy lost to drag relative to takeoff energy scales as ρa/ρm per body length — a flea jumping 10³ body lengths is where drag starts to matter.

A warning built into the example

The conclusion (3) is a scaling law, robust and worth remembering. The mechanism ("muscles store energy ∝ volume") is the fragile part. When a student presents a scaling result, the audience should be able to name the assumption that made it — and what would break it. That is exactly what the week's quiz question will test.

The ledger every later lecture will draw on: volumes, proteins, concentrations, and the rates that follow from them.

OoM reasoning turns inward, to the cell — the domain of the whole course. The base units first:1

Block 3

The scales

Diffusion and heat do one thing everywhere: t ≈ x²/D. It is the single most useful scaling in the course.

8Heat: t ~ x²/κ

From the heat equation to "a potato takes ~10 minutes"; the scaling t ∝ x² is the deliverable.

Third example: heat. Flux J = −K∇T, heat capacity per volume cv; energy balance gives ∂T/∂t = κ∇²T with κ = K/cv. Dimensional analysis of that equation gives the only scaling that matters:1

t ≈ x² / κ (4)

Check the potato: κ ≈ 1.5×10−6 m²/s, x ~ 5 cm → about 10 minutes to cook through. The same scaling says a molten Earth takes ~1010 years to cool — which may be one of the reasons the moon's interior is still partly hot. The functional form t ∝ x² is the point: double the size, quadruple the time. Nothing else about the mechanism — K, cv, geometry details — survives in the scaling.

9Diffusion and mixing

Brownian motion is a random walk: τ ≈ x²/D, D ∝ 1/r; the mixing time of a cell follows immediately.

Same random-walk logic, biological currency. A molecule of radius r in fluid buys mobility independent of its mass through drag, Fdrag ≈ η r v, so the diffusion constant shrinks with size: D ∝ 1/r — protein D ≈ 100 µm²/s, metabolite D larger. Mixing is a random walk, so 1

τmix ≈ x² / D (5)

For a metabolite crossing a 1 µm bacterium: τ ~ (10−6)²/103 ≈ 1 ms. For a protein covering a 1 µm cell: ~0.01–0.1 s. The estimates carry a message used all course long: inside a bacterium, diffusion mixes small molecules almost instantly — so spatial gradients are the exception, and the cell is best described as a well-mixed reactor. Turbulence there is none; the molecular jiggle is the mixing.

10Null hypotheses for biology

OoM in biology starts from the null model: how far can diffusion, dilution and mass action take you before any design is needed?

When OoM reasoning enters biology, it becomes a null-hypothesis generator: before asking why a cell regulates something, estimate what plain physics would do without regulation.1 "Would two molecules at 1 µM meet often enough?" — about 106 encounters per second per enzyme, since kon ≈ 4πDa ≈ 109 M−1s−1 (that estimate, with its boundary-value derivation, is week 2's first block). A naked reaction with a 100 ms timescale is going nowhere unless something else — catalysis, concentration, localization — does the work. So the OoM drill ends the way it began: the number says what needs explaining, and the null hypothesis says the explanation is cheap.

The ten-questions game

Worked example of the format: ten questions, three minutes each, answers to within a factor of ten. What is the mass of all RNA in a human cell? How many ATP molecules per cell at any instant? How long does a transcription cycle take? How many metabolism reactions fit in one cell? The method is always: name the scale, then the bottleneck, then the answer.
