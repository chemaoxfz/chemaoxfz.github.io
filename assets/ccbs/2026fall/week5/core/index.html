The stochastic reactor

Under a femtoliter, regularity is a lucky guess: the master equation, exact sampling, and what noise actually does to a cell.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · week 5 · core page · 4 student blocks

Week 2's "well-mixed reactor" was a mean-field claim; week 5 qualifies it. A protein at 1 µM in a 1 fL cell is roughly one molecule per cell — copy numbers that small make the law of large numbers a bad friend. The week's arc: from the deterministic ODE to the chemical master equation (block 1), to exact sampling with Gillespie (block 2), to the moment analysis that quantifies noise (block 3), to the phenomena that follow: burstiness, multimodality, extinction, and the subtle word ergodicity (block 4).

Blocks and sessions

The 2025 notes open this material with the Repressilator; the noise half of lecture 5 and the first half of lecture 6 are merged here into four blocks. Each is one 30-minute student session with its TA-approved quiz.

Block 1

The counting regime

When randomness is a property of the design, not a measurement error.

1Why noise becomes physics

At ~1 molecule per femtoliter, the mean-field picture loses its guarantee — and the Repressilator's fluctuations stop being a curiosity.

Start from the numbers of week 1: a 1 µM regulator over a 1 fL volume is order one molecule per cell. When a cell has a handful of copies, every event — transcription, binding, degradation — is a discrete jump, and averages over a cell population hide the single-cell truth that gene expression is bursty and noisy.1 The lecture's opening example is the Repressilator (Elowitz & Leibler): an engineered oscillator whose single-cell trajectories show substantial fluctuations — the deterministic equations predict oscillation; the stochastic system does not stay on that trajectory, and the fluctuations are not error, they are the system.

2The chemical master equation

The state is a probability distribution; the master equation moves probability between counts.

Let pn(t) be the probability of n copies. For the birth–death process (production rate k, degradation rate μ per molecule) the master equation is the account book of probability flow:2

dpn/dt = k(n+1)pn+1 − knpn + μpn−1 − μpn (1)

Each term: probability leaving state n (rightward/leftward), and entering it from the neighbors. General form: a linear ODE on an infinite-dimensional space — the deterministic ODE is recovered as the mean and becomes exact only in the large-volume limit; the distribution is the full object. There is no escaping the discreteness here: states are integers, and "n = 0" is a special state (it can absorb probability but not produce it) — the seed of extinction, below.

Block 2

Sampling

Exact realizations of the master equation: the Gillespie algorithm.

3The Gillespie algorithm

Two random draws, one exact trajectory: when does the next reaction occur, and which reaction is it?

At state x, each reaction i has a propensity ai(x) (its rate law, in molecules-per-time units). The total propensity a0 = Σai; the inter-event time is exponential,3

τ ~ Exp(a0),   reaction j chosen with probability aj/a0 (2)

Advance time by τ, apply j, repeat. The derivation walks through all of it: the probability that no reaction happens in dt, the exponential waiting time for each reaction, and the competition-weighted selection. The essential virtue: the algorithm is exact — it samples the master equation's distribution with no approximation, which is why it is the reference method to check everything else (moment closure, tau-leaping, ODE limits) against.

4Exactness, speed, and what it costs

Exactness comes at a price — one event at a time. That's the lesson, and the reason approximations exist (week 5's quiz loves the "exact" word).

The exact algorithm is event-by-event, so a cell at 10⁵ molecules and ms timescales samples 10⁶+ events per second — expensive. Approximations, in order of faithfulness: tau-leaping (bundle many events per step), moment closure (block 3: track the first moments, close them approximately), and the LNA (block 3's systematic Gaussian approximation). For the course, the practise rule is: Gillespie when counts are small (regulators); ODEs when they're large (metabolites); moment equations when all you need is the noise level.

Block 3

Moments

You don't need the whole distribution to answer "how noisy is this cell?" — sometimes just the first two moments.

5Mean = variance: Poisson universality

For a birth–death process in steady state the distribution is Poisson: mean equals variance, CV = 1/√λ.

Solve the steady state of (1) for the crude birth–death process: the distribution is Poisson with rate λ = μ/k.3

P{X = k} = λke−λ/k!,   ⟨X⟩ = Var(X) = λ,   CV = 1/√λ (3)

Two consequences to keep. First, the Poisson reference: if the measured variance exceeds the mean, the process is not Poisson — something (bursting, feedback, noise propagation) is at work; this is precisely the Fano factor F = Var/⟨X⟩ and its departure from 1. Second, the size of noise in biology's currency: CV ~ 1/√λ — noise goes down as copy number goes up, which is itself a design constraint: cells that need precision buy copies.

6Linear noise analysis

Linearity in the noise: the covariance of a linear-noise system obeys a Lyapunov equation, Mη + ηMᵀ = D.

The general machinery: write the mean-field ODE, add small deterministic noise; the propagator matrix M comes from the Jacobian of the deterministic system, the noise matrix D from the net stoichiometric fluxes, and the steady-state covariance η solves the Lyapunov equation2

Mη + ηMᵀ = D (4)

This is block 3's workhorse: any CRN with a stable fixed point gets a systematic noise level, without simulation. (The 2025 notes develop the index-heavy version and end mid-example — treat the margins: the classic pitfalls are cross-correlation terms and the sign conventions on the stoichiometric matrix; the homework in this block is exactly to carry (4) through one example twice: by hand and by simulation.)

Block 4

Phenomena

Noise is not a disturbance to robustness; it is the material of which switches, extinction and singular cells are made.

7Burstiness and the Fano factor

Gene expression arrives in bursts: the distribution is not Poisson, the variance exceeds the mean — and single cells are bimodal.

Genes fire in pulses: mRNAs are born in bursts and translated in bursts, so a single-gene reporter's histogram is heavier-tailed than Poisson. The Fano factor (above) is the diagnostic; in the lecture's examples, mean ≈ variance is replaced by F > 1 — an immediate quantitative statement that the "noise" is bursty, not diffusive.3 And because the production rate itself is a state (gene on/off), the effective description of a gene is a two-level input filtered by degradation — the bursts' size and duration are design knobs, and they parameterize how much noise a cell tolerates before a design changes.

8Multimodality, extinction, ergodicity

Three consequences of discreteness: multi-stability becomes multimodality, zero is an absorbing risk, and time averages betray single cells.

  • Multimodality. A bistable deterministic system has two attractors; a stochastic one has two peaks in its stationary distribution — and the rate of switching between them is finite. The toggle switch from week 3, in single cells, samples both states (week 7's fate decisions exploit exactly this).
  • Extinction & the absorbing zero. When n hits 0 in the master equation, the production term cannot bring it back — extinction is a real thermodynamic risk in small volumes, and the quasi-steady "leak" terms that ODE models carry are precisely the ones that hide it.
  • Ergodicity — time averages vs population averages. A single cell that switches between states over time averages differently from a population snapshot; ergodicity is the assumption that the two agree, and when a system is slowly switching they don't. The design consequence: cross-sectional (single-cell fixed) data and longitudinal (single-cell tracked) data are different instruments, and measures of "noise" depend on which one you used.
The week's exercise

Implement Gillespie for the toggle switch with Hill-2 repression; measure the switching rate as you tune the leakage; compare the stationary distribution with the LNA's prediction; and find where the LNA is wrong. One script, one honest paragraph on where the approximation failed.
