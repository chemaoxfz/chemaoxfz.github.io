Which growth objective rewards an allocation?

Compare division rate, harvested biomass, accumulated product and recovery while retaining the cost of making the machinery.

CCBS · Lecture 14 audience extension
Editorial synthesis of eight Week 7 student contributions · prepared with Codex assistance · 3 November 2026

Growth is a rate, but many biological and engineering questions concern an amount produced over time. The core derives growth from a cell's investment in its own machinery. The audience asks which investment is rewarded when the objective, environment or readout changes.

We begin with harvest and a finite production deadline. We then include the growth cost paid before a nutrient improvement, the material cost of maintaining precursor inventory, and the competing resource costs of two metabolic routes. Finally, we ask whether the observed cell-count ratio measures the intended change in growth.

The examples use declared illustrative parameters. Names link to accepted submissions. Their questions are distinguished from the additional derivations, numerical checks and connecting prose in this editorial synthesis.

1Choose the objective before selecting a growth rate

There is no single best allocation without a stated objective. Shunkang separates useful output from protein cost and damage. A weighted objective can compare those quantities, but its weights are part of the specification. They are not automatically a universal utility function used by a cell.

Biomass harvest provides a concrete example. Siheng Wang asks whether the fastest dividing chemostat population also yields the most biomass per hour. Let substrate concentration be cc, incoming concentration cinc_\mathrm{in}, biomass concentration XX, dilution rate DD and biomass yield YY. Assume the Monod law

λ(c)=λmaxcKs+c,X˙=[λ(c)−D]X,c˙=D(cin−c)−λ(c)XY.\lambda(c)=\lambda_\mathrm{max}\frac{c}{K_s+c},\qquad \dot X=[\lambda(c)-D]X,\qquad \dot c=D(c_\mathrm{in}-c)-\frac{\lambda(c)X}{Y}.

On the positive steady branch, growth equals dilution. Solving the balances gives

c∗=KsDλmax−D,X∗=Y(cin−c∗),0<D<Dwash:=λmaxcinKs+cin.c^*=\frac{K_sD}{\lambda_\mathrm{max}-D},\qquad X^*=Y(c_\mathrm{in}-c^*),\qquad 0<D<D_\mathrm{wash}:=\lambda_\mathrm{max}\frac{c_\mathrm{in}}{K_s+c_\mathrm{in}}.

The desired output is harvested biomass per volume per time, PB=DX∗P_B=DX^*. Increasing DD raises the per-cell growth rate on this branch but eventually leaves too little biomass in the vessel. Differentiating the harvest expression gives the unique interior maximum

Dopt=λmax(1−KsKs+cin).D_\mathrm{opt}=\lambda_\mathrm{max}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{K_s}{K_s+c_\mathrm{in}}}\right).

For λmax=1 h−1\lambda_\mathrm{max}=1\,\mathrm h^{-1}, Ks=1K_s=1, cin=9c_\mathrm{in}=9 in common substrate units and Y=0.5Y=0.5 biomass units per substrate unit, washout occurs at D=0.9 h−1D=0.9\,\mathrm h^{-1}. Maximum harvest occurs at about 0.6838 h−10.6838\,\mathrm h^{-1}. The biomass concentration there is 3.419 and harvest is 2.338 in the corresponding units. Product concentration, economic value and operating cost would define different objectives.

Optimize the requested amount or flux. Do not substitute maximum cellular division rate for maximum biomass harvest.

2Account for the time left to produce a product

A finite deadline can favor growth before production. Xinyu Wu derives the comparison in a two-phase model. Begin with biomass B0B_0. Before a switch time τ\tau, biomass grows at μ0>0\mu_0>0 and makes no product. After the switch, biomass grows at 0≤μ1<μ00\leq\mu_1<\mu_0 and produces at rate qB(t)qB(t), with constant q>0q>0.

For a fixed harvest time TT and μ1>0\mu_1>0, integrating product formation gives

P(T;τ)qB0=eμ0τeμ1(T−τ)−1μ1,0≤τ≤T.\frac{P(T;\tau)}{qB_0}=e^{\mu_0\tau}\frac{e^{\mu_1(T-\tau)}-1}{\mu_1}, \qquad 0\leq\tau\leq T.

Delaying production creates more biomass but leaves less production time. Differentiating this expression shows that an interior optimum leaves a production interval

sopt=1μ1log⁡μ0μ0−μ1,τopt=max⁡(0,T−sopt).s_\mathrm{opt}=\frac1{\mu_1}\log\frac{\mu_0}{\mu_0-\mu_1},\qquad \tau_\mathrm{opt}=\max(0,T-s_\mathrm{opt}).

At μ0=0.5 h−1\mu_0=0.5\,\mathrm h^{-1}, μ1=0.25 h−1\mu_1=0.25\,\mathrm h^{-1} and T=6 hT=6\,\mathrm h, the optimum switches at 3.227 hours. It gives P/(qB0)=20.086 hP/(qB_0)=20.086\,\mathrm h, compared with 13.927 hours for immediate production. The gain is about 44.2% under the same model and deadline.

The boundary cases identify the mechanism. If production stops growth completely, μ1=0\mu_1=0, then sopt=1/μ0s_\mathrm{opt}=1/\mu_0 by the continuous limit. If production carries no growth penalty, μ1=μ0\mu_1=\mu_0, immediate production is optimal. Nutrient exhaustion, changing product yield and a finite switch response would require different balances.

Include the deadline and the cost of production in the objective. Derive the switch from the full accumulated output, including boundary cases.

3Include the growth cost paid before reserve machinery is used

A strategy can sacrifice balanced growth and still gain more biomass during a short favorable interval. Fei Wang shows the possibility with a phenomenological lag model. Over a cycle of length TT, a population with post-lag rate λ\lambda and lag duration LL has logarithmic growth G=λmax⁡(T−L,0)G=\lambda\max(T-L,0).

Compare assigned pairs (λ,L)=(0.8 h−1,2 h)(\lambda,L)=(0.8\,\mathrm h^{-1},2\,\mathrm h) and (0.72 h−1,0.5 h)(0.72\,\mathrm h^{-1},0.5\,\mathrm h). Their logarithmic gains are equal at 15.5 hours. At 12 hours, the second strategy produces about 1.323 times as much biomass from the same start. This calculation compares given rates and lags. It does not yet explain why a reserve of protein would create them.

Machinery accounting supplies that missing connection. Zixuan Wang uses the core's two-sector model. Let ϕR\phi_R be the ribosomal protein fraction, fRf_R the fraction of new protein assigned to ribosomes and MM total protein mass:

λ=min⁡[σϕR,k(1−ϕR)],ϕ˙R=λ(fR−ϕR),M˙=λM.\lambda=\min[\sigma\phi_R,k(1-\phi_R)],\qquad \dot\phi_R=\lambda(f_R-\phi_R),\qquad \dot M=\lambda M.

Before a nutrient improvement, take σ=3 h−1\sigma=3\,\mathrm h^{-1} and k=0.6 h−1k=0.6\,\mathrm h^{-1}. A lean allocation ϕR=1/6\phi_R=1/6 grows at 0.5 per hour. A reserve allocation ϕR=0.3\phi_R=0.3 grows at 0.42 per hour. Each maintains its composition by setting fR=ϕRf_R=\phi_R. If equal masses spend HH hours in these conditions, the reserve population enters the improvement with a mass disadvantage e−0.08He^{-0.08H}.

After the improvement, set k=3 h−1k=3\,\mathrm h^{-1} and fR=1/2f_R=1/2 for both. Both trajectories remain translation-limited while approaching one-half ribosomal allocation. Their mass gains from the instant of improvement are exactly 1+2ϕR(0)(e1.5t−1)1+2\phi_R(0)(e^{1.5t}-1). Hence the ratio of reserve to lean mass, including the earlier cost, is

Mreserve(t)Mlean(t)=e−0.08H1+0.6(e1.5t−1)1+13(e1.5t−1)⟶1.8e−0.08H.\frac{M_\mathrm{reserve}(t)}{M_\mathrm{lean}(t)} =e^{-0.08H}\frac{1+0.6(e^{1.5t}-1)}{1+\tfrac13(e^{1.5t}-1)} \longrightarrow1.8e^{-0.08H}.

The ratio increases after the improvement but has a finite upper limit. A finite catch-up time exists only when H<log⁡(1.8)/0.08≃7.35 hH<\log(1.8)/0.08\simeq7.35\,\mathrm h. After a four-hour wait, the limiting advantage is about 1.307. After eight hours, the limiting ratio is only 0.949. Faster recovery then never repays the earlier loss.

Mori and colleagues connect ribosomal reserve to faster recovery through theory and nutrient-upshift measurements. That work supports the need to compare the cost during poor conditions with the gain during a finite favorable interval. The numerical example above has its own simplified capacities and initial conditions.1

Start the comparison before the reserve is useful. Integrate growth over the whole history and check whether the later gain can repay the earlier cost.

4Retain the machinery needed to replenish precursor inventory

A stationary precursor-to-protein ratio still requires precursor production as protein mass grows. Lipei Fang examines how this requirement changes the growth optimum and its interpretation. The current exposition retains that finite inventory. Here we use it to quantify the departure from the small-pool result.

Let a=MA/Ma=M_A/M be precursor amount in protein-mass equivalents divided by protein mass. Metabolic supply is k(1−ϕR)k(1-\phi_R) per unit protein mass. Translation is limited by both ribosomes and precursor processing, with capacities σϕR\sigma\phi_R and kAak_Aa. At the interior growth optimum, these capacities equal λ\lambda, while the precursor balance requires k(1−ϕR)=λ(1+a)k(1-\phi_R)=\lambda(1+a). Eliminating ϕR\phi_R and aa yields

λσ+λk+λ2kkA=1.\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}+\frac{\lambda}{k}+\frac{\lambda^2}{kk_A}=1.

The three terms are protein requirements for translation, supply of new protein and replenishment of precursor inventory. The last term is extra metabolic machinery. It is not the precursor stock itself counted as protein.

A dimensionless comparison makes the correction transparent. Define the small-pool optimum λ0=kσ/(k+σ)\lambda_0=k\sigma/(k+\sigma), the rate ratio r=λ/λ0r=\lambda/\lambda_0 and Γ=λ02/(kkA)\Gamma=\lambda_0^2/(kk_A). The budget becomes r+Γr2=1r+\Gamma r^2=1, with positive solution

r=21+1+4Γ.r=\frac2{1+\sqrt{1+4\Gamma}}.

For k=2,σ=3,kA=8k=2,\sigma=3,k_A=8 per hour, the optimum is 1.108 per hour instead of 1.2. The ribosomal fraction is 0.369. The precursor-to-protein ratio is 0.138, while the additional metabolic protein requirement is 0.0767. These quantities have different meanings even though all are dimensionless except the growth rate.

The ribosome line does not identify the entire mechanism. At this optimum, ϕR=λ/σ\phi_R=\lambda/\sigma still holds. Measuring only λ\lambda and ϕR\phi_R therefore does not separate unknown kk and kAk_A. A precursor-pool measurement supplies an additional constraint through a=λ/kAa=\lambda/k_A. A finite precursor pool alone also does not create a nonzero ribosomal intercept in this model.

Separate precursor inventory from the protein cost of replenishing it. Identify which additional observable is needed to distinguish the capacities behind a growth law.

5Compare carbon efficiency with protein efficiency

A route that saves carbon can lose when protein becomes limiting. Leo Sin studies this tradeoff through two routes contributing useful fluxes Jr,Jf≥0J_r,J_f\geq0. For this comparison, both fluxes use the same output unit. Let carbon costs be cr<cfc_r<c_f and total protein costs be pr>pfp_r>p_f. With carbon budget CC and available protein fraction FF, maximize

J=Jr+Jf,crJr+cfJf≤C,prJr+pfJf≤F.J=J_r+J_f,\qquad c_rJ_r+c_fJ_f\leq C,\qquad p_rJ_r+p_fJ_f\leq F.

The costs must include whichever machinery the model assigns to a route. If uptake itself costs protein, comparing only downstream enzyme costs can give the wrong ordering of pr,pfp_r,p_f. The objective here is useful flux. Equating it with growth would require an explicit conversion and additional biomass requirements.

At low carbon supply, the carbon-efficient route alone gives Jr=C/crJ_r=C/c_r. Its protein requirement reaches the budget at C=Fcr/prC=Fc_r/p_r. At high supply, the protein-efficient route alone gives Jf=F/pfJ_f=F/p_f and requires C=Fcf/pfC=Fc_f/p_f. Between these thresholds, solving the two active constraints gives the mixed optimum.

An editorial example uses normalized costs cr=1,cf=3,pr=2,pf=1,F=1c_r=1,c_f=3,p_r=2,p_f=1,F=1. The complete solution is

(Jr,Jf)={(C,0),0≤C≤1/2,((3−C)/5,(2C−1)/5),1/2≤C≤3,(0,1),C≥3.(J_r,J_f)=\begin{cases}(C,0),&0\leq C\leq1/2,\\ ((3-C)/5,(2C-1)/5),&1/2\leq C\leq3,\\ (0,1),&C\geq3. \end{cases}

In the mixed region, the weighted sum of carbon and protein costs is J=[(Jr+3Jf)+2(2Jr+Jf)]/5≤(C+2)/5J=[(J_r+3J_f)+2(2J_r+J_f)]/5\leq(C+2)/5. The proposed solution attains that bound, proving optimality. The low- and high-supply bounds follow directly from carbon and protein, respectively.

Basan and colleagues tested a proteome-allocation explanation of acetate overflow in E. coli, including measured costs of energy production. That evidence motivates comparing carbon and protein efficiency. The two-route calculation above isolates the tradeoff without claiming to fit every overflow phenotype.2

Compare complete resource costs per unit of the same output. Demonstrate which budget limits each regime and certify the proposed optimum.

6Measure the growth change rather than its accumulated consequence alone

An endpoint cell-count ratio can differ even when two populations undergo the same fractional growth-rate reduction. Xiaomeng Zhu uses the growth-rate inhibition metric to expose this observation problem. Assume equal initial counts N0N_0 within each treated-control pair and exponential net growth over the interval:

Ncontrol=N0eλ0t,Ntreated=N0eλ1t,NtreatedNcontrol=e(λ1−λ0)t.N_\mathrm{control}=N_0e^{\lambda_0t},\qquad N_\mathrm{treated}=N_0e^{\lambda_1t},\qquad \frac{N_\mathrm{treated}}{N_\mathrm{control}}=e^{(\lambda_1-\lambda_0)t}.

Suppose treatment halves the net growth rate in both populations. If the untreated doubling times are 24 and 48 hours, their treated-control count ratios after 48 hours are 0.5 and about 0.707. Those different endpoint ratios do not imply different fractional rate inhibition.

Hafner and colleagues define a normalized growth-rate inhibition value, conventionally written GR\mathrm{GR}, from the relative logarithmic count increases. For a growing control and a positive observation interval,

GR=2log⁡(Ntreated/N0)/log⁡(Ncontrol/N0)−1=2λ1/λ0−1.\mathrm{GR}=2^{\log(N_\mathrm{treated}/N_0)/\log(N_\mathrm{control}/N_0)}-1 =2^{\lambda_1/\lambda_0}-1.

Both examples give 2−1≃0.414\sqrt2-1\simeq0.414. This number is not a surviving-cell fraction. In the stated model, a value between zero and one describes reduced positive net growth. Zero describes no net population growth. Negative values describe net population decline. Time-dependent responses require time-resolved interpretation rather than assuming a single rate throughout.3

The next growth comparison should therefore declare its history and readout together. For two allocations, record initial biomass, the duration of each environment, protein composition and the final output that matters. Test whether a measured recovery advantage survives both the prior cost and the chosen observation metric. That is the point at which an allocation law becomes a prediction of useful performance.

Keep initial counts and control growth in the analysis. Distinguish reduced accumulation from reduced net growth rate and from a claim about cell survival.
