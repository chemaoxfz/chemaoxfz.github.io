The energy of regulation

Binding energies, detailed balance, and Markov chains: what a cell can know and what it must pay for.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · week 6 · core page · 4 student blocks

Weeks 2–5 described what networks do; week 6 asks what they cost. The week's arc runs from the thermal scale that governs binding (block 1), over the bookkeeping that distinguishes equilibrium from driven systems (block 2), through Markov chains as the mathematics of both discrete states and random-walk timing (block 3), to the information-theoretic consequence: specificity is bought with energy, and there are hard limits on how precisely a cell can sense and control (block 4).

Blocks and sessions

The 2025 material splits across lecture 6 (half B: equilibrium physics of bioregulation) and lecture 7 (statistical mechanics of regulation and Markov chains). Each block is one 30-minute student session with its TA-approved quiz.

Block 1

Statistical mechanics

Everything binding-shaped is a Boltzmann weight: the probability of a state is e⁻ᵝᴱ over the partition function.

1The Boltzmann scale of regulation

p(x) ∝ e^(−βE) with β = 1/k_BT: one k_BT is the exchange rate of thermal noise, and 2–3 k_BT per hydrogen bond is the currency of specificity.

The fundamental distribution of a constant-temperature system: p(x) ∝ e−βE, β = 1/kBT. Two scales follow. Thermal noise is k_BT — at room temperature, the energy of a few kT is what a hydrogen bond contributes, and the notes' working heuristic is 2–3 k_BT per hydrogen bond, translating directly into an about-ten-fold binding preference per bond pair.1 The rule of thumb to internalize: a difference of a few k_BT in binding energy is a ten-fold change in binding probability. That is how a cell "programs" affinity with sequences: not by engineering exact constants, but by allocating hydrogen bonds.

2The energy model of a regulated gene

Transcriptional regulation as a sum of microstates: free (weight 1), bound to polymerase (S_tot/K_0-style weights), bound to a repressor — with energies that add per contact.

The standard machinery: enumerate states of the promoter (free / polymerase-bound / repressor-bound — with weights from binding affinities and energy differences), compute the partition function, and read off the probability of expression. The lecture's worked examples include the free-state weight of 1 (reference), the bound-state weights proportional to Stot/K0-type ratios, and the multiplicative energy terms of a two-site binding (zero/one/two bound states with their e−Δε factors).1 The lecture's "Review of procedure" table is exactly the accounting the students should be able to do blind: for a promoter with given binding energies, write the weights, normalize, and read the expression probability.

From here the MWC model is one more layer over the same bookkeeping — a few binding states plus an interaction (cooperativity) term — and the notes work it as the second example, alongside the classical Michaelis–Menten enzymatic regulation as example one, and gene expression under repression as example three.2

Block 2

The ledger

Equilibrium does not do work; driven cycles do. The bookkeeping rule is detailed balance.

3Detailed balance and the cycle condition

In equilibrium, every cycle is balanced: k₂₁k₃₂k₁₃ = k₁₂k₂₃k₃₁ — no net circulation, no work done.

Detailed balance in a three-state cycle says the probability flow around the loop cancels:2

k21k32k13 = k12k23k31 (1)

When the condition holds, the system sits at thermodynamic equilibrium: no net circulation, no free energy dissipated, and the stationary distribution is the Boltzmann one. When it fails (an ATP-driven cycle, a molecular motor, a proofreading device), the system is driven — it can do work, and it can also regulate better than equilibrium allows. The worksheet tests: given a set of rates, check (1); if it fails, find the energy source.

4What energy buys

Driven systems buy the one thing equilibrium cannot: accuracy beyond the Boltzmann limit — and the notes' worked example quantifies it.

The lecture's quantitative example (the gene-activation accounting with eε_A − ε_I = 10 and 100× tighter binding in one state) produces an active probability of about 0.9: the calculation is a clean demonstration of how energy differences and affinity ratios compose into a regulatory phenotype.2 The deeper statement for the block: a kinetically proofread device — one that hydrolyzes ATP to reject a wrong match — achieves an error rate that an equilibrium device at the same affinities cannot, and the margin is paid for in energy.1 This is the entry to block 4's information limits.

Equilibrium is never free

An equilibrium device has no net flux and makes no net energy cost, but its accuracy is bounded by the Boltzmann weight difference. Anything that claims better accuracy than e−Δε/k_BT is either driven or wrong. That — not "energy is expensive" — is the real reason cells burn ATP.

Block 3

Markov chains

Discrete states, one stochastic step at a time: the mathematics of both regulation and transport.

5Chains, transition matrices, stationary distributions

A CTMC is a master equation; a discrete chain is a transition matrix; the stationary distribution solves the balance equations.

Markov chains are the same jump-process mathematics as the CME of week 5, read differently. In continuous time (CTMC) the master equation dp/dt = Qp is the same object as equation (1) of week 5; in discrete time the transition matrix πPt+1 = πPt repeats. The stationary distribution solves the balance equations, and the finest version is detailed balance (1) per pair of states: πiqij = πjqji. The lecture's summary slide covers exactly this line: CTMC master equation → steady state → detailed balance → hitting & first-passage times.3

6Hitting times and first passages

How long before a chain gets somewhere? One linear system: Q^j τ^j = 0 with boundary conditions, and often a closed form.

The first-passage problem: the expected time to first reach a set of states solves a linear system, Q(j)τ(j) = 0 with boundary values, and the notes carry the general recurrence plus the worked rotational example (the qij/|qii| structure and the "wait" annotation: the expected time stays put — a standard random-walk result) and the finite-state projection remark for solving the CME by chain methods.1 Compute: small systems by hand (two or three states), bigger ones by sparse linear algebra. And note the two face of this in the course: the same computation, run on a gene-state chain, gives switching/first-expression times — the bridge from block 3 to the stochastic events of week 5.

Block 4

Information

Accuracy costs; and there are limits on the rate and precision of what a cell can sense.

7Specificity is paid for

Kinetic proofreading is the canonical example: "super precise" specificity, bought with driven cycles, and its error rate is a power of the discrimination factors.

Hopfield's proofreading device: an enzyme discriminates correct from incorrect substrates by a factor f per "proofreading" round; each round costs energy (e.g. one ATP); the error rate after two rounds scales as f−2 rather than f−1, and every extra factor of ten in accuracy costs a factor of ten in energy. The notes state the endpoint directly: proofreading is what makes the ribosome's ~10⁻⁴ error rate thermodynamically unexplainable at equilibrium — and therefore the argument that specificity must be paid for.1

8Limits on what a cell can know

Two classical bounds — the Berg–Purcell timing bound and the energy-accuracy tradeoff — as the "go beyond" of the week.

The block ends facing outward. The Berg–Purcell limit: a sensor integrating a concentration for T seconds has measurement precision bounded by

δc/c ≈ 1/√(D a c T) (2)

— the timing/length/energy constraints that bound chemotaxis accuracy; and the energy–accuracy tradeoff of proofreading above. Both are statements of the same deep claim this whole day makes: a cell's information is budgeted, and the budget is denominated in energy and time. That is why the "physics school" is the course's first school, and why the industry week (8) has to pay the bills.
