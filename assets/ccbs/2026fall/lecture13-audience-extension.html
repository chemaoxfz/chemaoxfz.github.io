Which metabolic constraints limit improvement and recovery?

Distinguish the value of more capacity, the limits of a feedback architecture and recovery from a finite disturbance.

CCBS · Lecture 13 audience extension
Editorial synthesis of one Week 6 and three Week 7 student contributions · prepared with Codex assistance · 3 November 2026

A better steady optimum does not establish a safer transient. Extra substrate can raise an optimal flux while leaving the regulation problem untouched. Stronger feedback can reduce a steady offset while making a disturbance harder to survive. The core develops these different descriptions of a metabolic machine. The audience asks which constraints belong to each claim.

We first calculate the value of relaxing a resource bound. We then hold the metabolic plant fixed and change the coordinates, sensing restriction and controller. Finally, we restore nonlinear rates and compare two histories that end at the same demand. The progression separates a steady optimization from a local control calculation and a finite-trajectory test.

The numerical choices are illustrative. Names link to accepted submissions. Equations and figures have been independently checked for this editorial synthesis.

1Price an active resource constraint before increasing capacity

Additional capacity has value only relative to an objective and its currently limiting constraints. Peng Su makes the local value explicit. Consider a normalized biomass flux μ≥0\mu\geq0 that consumes two carbon units and one nitrogen unit per biomass unit. Uptake capacities are UC,UN>0U_C,U_N>0:

max⁡ μsubject to2μ≤UC,μ≤UN,μ≥0.μ∗=min⁡(UC/2,UN).\max\ \mu\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad 2\mu\leq U_C,\quad \mu\leq U_N,\quad\mu\geq0. \qquad \mu^*=\min(U_C/2,U_N).

Within a region where the same bound limits growth, define the uptake-bound shadow prices λC=∂μ∗/∂UC\lambda_C=\partial\mu^*/\partial U_C and λN=∂μ∗/∂UN\lambda_N=\partial\mu^*/\partial U_N. They are (1/2,0)(1/2,0) on the carbon-limited side and (0,1)(0,1) on the nitrogen-limited side. Thus

Δμ∗=λCΔUC+λNΔUN\Delta\mu^*=\lambda_C\Delta U_C+\lambda_N\Delta U_N

is exact for changes that remain in either one of those regions. At UC=10,UN=8U_C=10,U_N=8, one extra carbon unit raises the optimum by one half. Extra nitrogen does nothing. At UC=20,UN=8U_C=20,U_N=8, the roles reverse.

The boundary has no unique ordinary derivative. The dual problem minimizes λCUC+λNUN\lambda_CU_C+\lambda_NU_N over nonnegative prices satisfying 2λC+λN≥12\lambda_C+\lambda_N\geq1. At the positive boundary UC=2UNU_C=2U_N, every pair

0≤λC≤12,λN=1−2λC0\leq\lambda_C\leq\frac12,\qquad \lambda_N=1-2\lambda_C

attains the same dual optimum. These pairs give supporting upper bounds on changes in the concave optimal-value function. They do not assign one derivative to every direction across the kink. Increasing only carbon at the boundary gives no improvement. Increasing both capacities in the stoichiometric ratio does.

The linear rule is a property of the optimized model. A cell has not thereby been shown to measure or compute those dual prices. Larger linear programs can also have several optimal flux distributions. Sensitivity of the objective does not establish a unique internal route or the time needed to change enzymes. Flux balance analysis deliberately omits that intracellular recovery trajectory.1

Name the constrained quantity and objective before assigning a shadow price. Use the price within its valid region and examine directional changes at a boundary.

2Preserve the input and sensor when changing plant coordinates

A coordinate change preserves a control problem only when the actuator and observation change with it. Haobo Zheng applies this test to the core's ATP-investment model. Let xI,xATPx_I,x_{\mathrm{ATP}} be small deviations of the two scaled totals on the normalized metabolic clock. Open upstream inhibition into an independent input uu:

ddtˉ(xIxATP)=(−κ1+g2κ−1−2g)(xIxATP)+(−11)u,y=xATP.\frac{d}{d\bar t}\begin{pmatrix}x_I\\x_{\mathrm{ATP}}\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}-\kappa&1+g\\2\kappa&-1-2g\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}x_I\\x_{\mathrm{ATP}}\end{pmatrix} +\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\end{pmatrix}u, \qquad y=x_{\mathrm{ATP}}.

Here κ=1.1\kappa=1.1 and g=0.3g=0.3. A positive inhibitory action reduces investment flux. It therefore reduces intermediate production and immediately spares ATP, giving the two signs in the input vector. The output remains the ATP deviation.

A transformed coordinate makes the unstable plant easier to compare with a familiar second-order system. Define

θ=xI+xATP,ω=κxI−gxATP.\theta=x_I+x_{\mathrm{ATP}},\qquad \omega=\kappa x_I-gx_{\mathrm{ATP}}.

Since the transformation determinant is −(κ+g)≠0-(\kappa+g)\ne0, it is invertible. Transforming all three parts of the system gives

θ′=ω,ω′=κθ−(1+κ+2g)ω−(κ+g)u,y=κθ−ωκ+g.\theta'=\omega,\qquad \omega'=\kappa\theta-(1+\kappa+2g)\omega-(\kappa+g)u, \qquad y=\frac{\kappa\theta-\omega}{\kappa+g}.

The coordinate θ\theta is a sum of pool deviations. It is not a physical mechanical angle. The mathematical analogy helps only if the transformed sensor is retained. ATP-only proportional feedback u=hyu=hy restricts two possible state gains to the ray

u=hθθ+hωω,(hθ,hω)=hκ+g(κ,−1).u=h_\theta\theta+h_\omega\omega,\qquad (h_\theta,h_\omega)=\frac{h}{\kappa+g}(\kappa,-1).

With two independently available state gains, the second-order characteristic polynomial is

s2+[1+κ+2g+(κ+g)hω]s+[(κ+g)hθ−κ].s^2+[1+\kappa+2g+(\kappa+g)h_\omega]s +[(\kappa+g)h_\theta-\kappa].

Both coefficients must be positive. For the stated parameters, stability requires hθ>0.7857h_\theta>0.7857 and hω>−1.9286h_\omega>-1.9286. Restricting those gains to the ATP-only ray gives the narrower interval 1<h<2.71<h<2.7. The upper limit is therefore tied to this observation and feedback restriction. Independent gains would require the corresponding additional state information and realizable control action.

Transform the state, input and sensor together. Compare controllers only after stating which measurements and gain combinations they can use.

3State the controller class before interpreting a feedback limit

Adding memory changes the controller's stability conditions rather than removing the need for them. Álvaro asks how the glycolytic restriction changes beyond proportional feedback. Keep the same ATP measurement and let

u=hxATP+kIz,z′=xATP,kI>0.u=h x_{\mathrm{ATP}}+k_I z,\qquad z'=x_{\mathrm{ATP}},\qquad k_I>0.

The added state is a signed integral coordinate. At a finite equilibrium it forces the measured ATP deviation to zero. The resulting local characteristic polynomial is

s3+(2.7−h)s2+[1.1(h−1)−kI]s+1.1kI.s^3+(2.7-h)s^2+[1.1(h-1)-k_I]s+1.1k_I.

The cubic Routh–Hurwitz test requires positive coefficients and the product of the first two nonleading coefficients to exceed the constant term. Simplifying those conditions gives

1<h<2.7,0<kI<1.1(h−1)(2.7−h)3.8−h.1<h<2.7,\qquad 0<k_I<\frac{1.1(h-1)(2.7-h)}{3.8-h}.

Thus the added integral action can remove a constant-input equilibrium error inside a smaller two-parameter stability region. For example, h=2,kI=0.1h=2,k_I=0.1 is stable in this linear model. At h=2.6h=2.6, kI=0.05k_I=0.05 is stable while kI=0.2k_I=0.2 is not. Neither conclusion supplies a molecular implementation or guarantees actuator capacity.

Broader performance bounds likewise need a specified input-output problem. Chandra, Buzi and Doyle analyze glycolytic autocatalysis through its unstable plant and delayed ATP payoff. Their frequency-domain limitations retain a plant, actuation channel and measured output while allowing a class of controllers. A scalar gain boundary and a sensitivity-integral constraint are different results. Changing the available sensor or comparing a different disturbance-response quantity changes the question.2

Separate an equilibrium identity, a controller-specific stability region and a broader input-output limitation. State the actuator, measurement and admissible controller class for each.

4Test nonlinear recovery under the actual demand history

A stable final operating point can coexist with a dangerous finite excursion. Chengwei Jin asks whether delaying the increase in demand can change the recovery. Restore the core's illustrative nonlinear investment and payoff rates:

vˉ1=2qˉATP1+qˉATP2h,vˉ2=2κqˉI1+qˉATP2g,qˉI′=vˉ1−vˉ2,qˉATP′=−vˉ1+2vˉ2−wˉ.\bar v_1=\frac{2\bar q_{\mathrm{ATP}}}{1+\bar q_{\mathrm{ATP}}^{2h}},\qquad \bar v_2=\frac{2\kappa\bar q_I}{1+\bar q_{\mathrm{ATP}}^{2g}}, \qquad \bar q_I'=\bar v_1-\bar v_2,\quad \bar q_{\mathrm{ATP}}'=-\bar v_1+2\bar v_2-\bar w.

Bars retain the core's common concentration scale and metabolic clock. Use h=2.6,κ=1.1,g=0.3h=2.6,\kappa=1.1,g=0.3 and start at (qˉI,qˉATP)=(1/κ,1)(\bar q_I,\bar q_{\mathrm{ATP}})=(1/\kappa,1) under demand one. Compare an immediate demand step to 1.224 with a linear increase from one to 1.224 over ten time units.

A supply-capacity check explains why the example is demanding. At steady state, both pathway fluxes must equal demand. Differentiating the investment rate gives its maximum at

qˉATP,max=(2h−1)−1/(2h),vˉ1,max⁡≃1.22580.\bar q_{\mathrm{ATP,max}}=(2h-1)^{-1/(2h)},\qquad \bar v_{1,\max}\simeq1.22580.

The final demand lies just below that capacity. The two positive ATP equilibria are about 0.73872 and 0.77890. Direct Jacobian evaluation identifies the lower as a saddle and the higher as locally stable. Existence of the higher equilibrium does not put every initial trajectory in its basin.

The two input histories select different trajectories. The immediate step crosses an illustrative ATP threshold of 0.6 at normalized time 14.517 and continues downward. Integration stops at ATP 0.2 to remain within the positive concentration domain. The ten-unit ramp stays above the 0.6 threshold over the checked trajectory and approaches the stable ATP level 0.77890. Independent integration methods agree on both outcomes.

The ramp has a cost that the endpoint comparison must retain. Relative to the immediate step, it postpones (1.224−1)10/2=1.12(1.224-1)10/2=1.12 normalized units of ATP demand during the first ten units of time. The example therefore does not show a free improvement in efficiency. It shows that the timing of required work can change a constrained trajectory.

A next test should fix both the final demand and the allowed delay in total work. Compare admissible demand histories while enforcing a declared ATP floor and the same kinetic model. That would connect Peng's constrained optimum to a finite-time control objective, while retaining the sensing restrictions exposed by Haobo and Álvaro.

Check capacity, local stability and the full finite trajectory separately. Include delayed work in the comparison when a gentler input history improves recovery.
