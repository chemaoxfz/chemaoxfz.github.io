What does adaptation preserve when the experiment changes?

Distinguish an invariant endpoint from an invariant trajectory, an achievable control action and a constrained statistical mean.

CCBS · Lecture 10 audience extension
Editorial synthesis of six Week 5 student contributions · prepared with Codex assistance · 19 October 2026

Adaptation is a claim about a specified output under a specified perturbation. The core shows why an accumulated error must vanish at a finite steady state, then examines biological implementations and their stability. The audience papers ask what changes when we alter the experiment around that identity: controller parameters, memory lifetime, input scale, actuator capacity or observation noise.

We answer in three parts. First, identify the value to which the output returns and how leakage changes it. Next, test whether scaled inputs preserve the whole response and whether the actuator can reject the disturbance at all. Finally, identify the statistical quantity constrained when observations and reactions fluctuate. The exposition supplies the controller derivations, while the lineage traces their sources.

Names link to accepted, commit-pinned submissions. Parameters and simulations in this chapter are illustrative unless explicitly described as published observations. The additional derivations and regenerated figures are editorial synthesis.

Part I

Specify the return value before testing robustness

Rejecting a disturbance does not imply rejecting changes to the reference that defines the desired output.

1Separate disturbance rejection from changes to the reference and sensor

A controller can preserve its return value against one perturbation and move it under another. Lipei Fang makes this distinction explicit by varying parameters inside the adaptation mechanism. The first task is to name which parameters a robustness claim holds fixed.

Recall the antithetic controller's difference variable. Let Z1,Z2Z_1,Z_2 be its two nonnegative controller concentrations, let Z=Z1−Z2Z=Z_1-Z_2, and let XX be the controlled output concentration. Reference production has rate μ>0\mu>0, and sensor production has rate θX\theta X, with θ>0\theta>0. The common removal term cancels in the difference:

Z˙=μ−θX.\dot Z=\mu-\theta X.

A finite controller steady state therefore requires X∗=μ/θX^*=\mu/\theta. A constant disturbance to the plant can leave this value unchanged if the full closed system still reaches a feasible stable equilibrium. Changing reference production or sensor calibration changes the value itself. Its logarithmic sensitivities are

∂log⁡X∗∂log⁡μ=1,∂log⁡X∗∂log⁡θ=−1.\frac{\partial\log X^*}{\partial\log\mu}=1,\qquad \frac{\partial\log X^*}{\partial\log\theta}=-1.

This is the scope of the antithetic identity, not a universal sacrifice of robustness to every controller parameter. The annihilation constant does not appear in the ratio, although it can affect the dynamics and whether the required steady state exists. Briat, Gupta and Khammash explicitly separate the reference and measurement reactions from these other controller functions.1

A methylation balance gives a second concrete example. Let a∈(0,1)a\in(0,1) be receptor activity and mm a continuous methylation coordinate. Consider constant methylation rate VR>0V_R>0 and activity-dependent demethylation with capacity VB>0V_B>0 and dimensionless half-saturation parameter KB>0K_B>0:

m˙=VR−VBaKB+a.\dot m=V_R-V_B\frac{a}{K_B+a}.

Here the input disturbance is a change in attractant concentration, and the output is activity. At a finite methylation steady state, balancing the two processes gives

a∗=KBVRVB−VR,0<a∗<1⟺0<VR<VB1+KB.a^*=\frac{K_BV_R}{V_B-V_R},\qquad 0<a^*<1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0<V_R<\frac{V_B}{1+K_B}.

The inequality is an activity feasibility condition. An actual receptor also needs enough modification capacity to reach the required methylation state. Neither condition is supplied merely by canceling the steady derivative.

The return value is sensitive to the enzyme-rate parameters. Define Sp=∂log⁡a∗/∂log⁡pS_p=\partial\log a^*/\partial\log p, holding the other parameters fixed. Direct differentiation gives

SVR=VBVB−VR=1+a∗KB,SVB=−VBVB−VR,SKB=1.S_{V_R}=\frac{V_B}{V_B-V_R}=1+\frac{a^*}{K_B},\qquad S_{V_B}=-\frac{V_B}{V_B-V_R},\qquad S_{K_B}=1.

Experiments in bacterial chemotaxis illustrate the distinction. Alon and colleagues varied CheR abundance and measured tumbling responses to attractant. Adaptation precision remained within experimental error over the reported variations, while the steady tumbling frequency and adaptation time changed. Their behavioral readout is not numerically identical to the receptor activity in Figure 1. It nevertheless makes clear why adaptation precision and the baseline value are separate measurements.2

Correlated parameter variation can preserve a ratio. Across preparations that each satisfy X∗=μ/θX^*=\mu/\theta, the exact identity log⁡X∗=log⁡μ−log⁡θ\log X^*=\log\mu-\log\theta implies

Var⁡(log⁡X∗)=Var⁡(log⁡μ)+Var⁡(log⁡θ)−2Cov⁡(log⁡μ,log⁡θ).\operatorname{Var}(\log X^*)= \operatorname{Var}(\log\mu)+\operatorname{Var}(\log\theta) -2\operatorname{Cov}(\log\mu,\log\theta).

This equation concerns diversity in parameters and steady values across preparations. It is not the intrinsic fluctuation variance of one cell's instantaneous output. A common multiplicative change in both parameters preserves their ratio, while independent changes generally do not.

Test disturbance rejection with the reference and sensor held fixed. Then vary those parameters separately to determine how the adapted value itself is specified.

2Turn memory leakage into an explicit steady-error budget

Leaking memory requires a nonzero error to maintain an opposing action. Siheng Wang turns this statement into a calculable tolerance. Qian and Del Vecchio identify molecular dilution as a source of leaky integration and analyze how faster controller reactions can reduce its effect.3

Use a dimensionless deviation model to isolate the mechanism. The output xx is its deviation from the desired value zero. A constant disturbance dd drives the output upward. The controller state zz provides an opposing action. Let k>0k>0 be the integration coefficient and λ≥0\lambda\geq0 the memory-leak rate:

x˙=d−x−z,z˙=kx−λz.\dot x=d-x-z,\qquad \dot z=kx-\lambda z.

The variables are signed deviations, not individual nonnegative molecular counts. Time is measured in units of the uncontrolled plant's relaxation time. The model abstracts the action of a controller without specifying its biochemical realization.

At a constant-disturbance steady state, solve d=x∗+z∗d=x^*+z^* and kx∗=λz∗kx^*=\lambda z^*. Thus

x∗=λk+λd,z∗=kk+λd.x^*=\frac{\lambda}{k+\lambda}d,\qquad z^*=\frac{k}{k+\lambda}d.

Ideal memory, λ=0\lambda=0, supports a nonzero controller action with zero output error. Leaky memory continually loses that action, so a persistent error supplies the replenishment. With k=0.5k=0.5, λ=0.15\lambda=0.15 leaves 23.1% of the uncontrolled constant-disturbance error.

The residual formula needs a stability check before it predicts a long-time observation. The characteristic polynomial is

s2+(1+λ)s+(k+λ).s^2+(1+\lambda)s+(k+\lambda).

Both nonleading coefficients are positive for k>0,λ≥0k>0,\lambda\geq0, so both roots have negative real part. Every equilibrium in this particular linear model is asymptotically stable. Additional delays, actuator dynamics or nonlinear biochemical constraints would require another stability calculation.

A tolerated residual becomes a quantitative memory requirement. To keep the steady error at most 5% of the uncontrolled response, require

λk+λ≤0.05⟺λ≤k19.\frac{\lambda}{k+\lambda}\leq0.05 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lambda\leq\frac{k}{19}.

With k=0.5k=0.5, the leak rate must be at most 0.0263. The corresponding unforced memory half-life, t1/2=log⁡2/λt_{1/2}=\log2/\lambda, must be at least 26.3 plant relaxation-time units. This is a steady-error requirement. It does not bound the transient peak in Figure 2.

Convert a steady-error tolerance into a leak-to-integration ratio, then check stability separately. Keep the resulting memory lifetime in the same time units as the plant.

Part II

Specify which input changes the controller can accommodate

Returning to one endpoint is weaker than preserving a whole trajectory. Both require an attainable controller state and action.

3Test fold-change detection through a symmetry of the whole response

Exact adaptation does not imply identical responses to proportional inputs. Leo Sin asks when the entire activity trace depends on the fold change rather than the absolute ligand level. Shoval and colleagues define fold-change detection as equality of the full output trajectories for proportional positive input histories, starting from their corresponding adapted states.4

A receptor model reveals the needed input coordinate. Let L>0L>0 be free attractant concentration and let receptor activity be

a(L,m)=11+eF(L,m),F(L,m)=ϵ0−gm+Nlog⁡1+L/Kd,I1+L/Kd,A.a(L,m)=\frac1{1+e^{F(L,m)}},\qquad F(L,m)=\epsilon_0-gm+ N\log\frac{1+L/K_{d,I}}{1+L/K_{d,A}}.

The dissociation constants Kd,I<Kd,AK_{d,I}<K_{d,A} describe ligand binding to inactive and active receptor states. The positive parameters NN and gg set the ligand and methylation contributions to the activation exponent. The constant ϵ0\epsilon_0 fixes its offset. Increasing ligand lowers activity, while increasing methylation raises activity. Here mm is a continuous coordinate with an arbitrary reference offset. A negative coordinate does not mean a negative number of methyl groups.

Close the same methylation balance as in section 1 around this activity. For fixed ligand, ∂a/∂m=ga(1−a)>0\partial a/\partial m=ga(1-a)>0, while ∂m˙/∂a<0\partial\dot m/\partial a<0. The feedback therefore restores the feasible activity a∗a^*, provided the required methylation coordinate is available. For the calculation below, the coordinate has no imposed end stops. Finite modification capacity would restrict the range further.

Fold-change detection requires more than this endpoint condition. In the affinity window Kd,I≪L≪Kd,AK_{d,I}\ll L\ll K_{d,A}, the ligand contribution is approximately Nlog⁡(L/Kd,I)N\log(L/K_{d,I}). Write the resulting idealized exponent as

Fideal(L,m)=F0−gm+Nlog⁡(L/Lref),F_\mathrm{ideal}(L,m)=F_0-gm+N\log(L/L_\mathrm{ref}),

where Lref>0L_\mathrm{ref}>0 is a reference concentration and F0F_0 collects the constant terms. Multiplying the entire input history by a factor r>0r>0 can now be canceled by a constant translation of the memory:

Lr(t)=rL(t),mr(t)=m(t)+Nglog⁡r,Fideal(Lr,mr)=Fideal(L,m).L_r(t)=rL(t),\qquad m_r(t)=m(t)+\frac Ng\log r, \qquad F_\mathrm{ideal}(L_r,m_r)=F_\mathrm{ideal}(L,m).

This cancellation also preserves the dynamics. Equal activity gives equal methylation rates because m˙\dot m depends only on activity. A constant translation has the same derivative. Starting with the translated adapted memory therefore produces identical activity at every time. The argument applies to entire positive input histories, not only isolated steps.

Finite affinities break the exact logarithmic symmetry. To show the size of the effect, choose N=6N=6, g=2g=2, Kd,I=0.01 μMK_{d,I}=0.01\,\mu\mathrm M and Kd,A=100 μMK_{d,A}=100\,\mu\mathrm M. Use VR=1,VB=4,KB=1V_R=1,V_B=4,K_B=1, so a∗=1/3a^*=1/3. Choose ϵ0\epsilon_0 so that m=2m=2 is adapted at Lref=1 μML_\mathrm{ref}=1\,\mu\mathrm M. These parameters define a teaching example, not a fit to bacterial measurements.

A comparison using absolute ligand level isolates why the logarithm matters. Replace the receptor exponent by

Flevel(L,m)=log⁡2+A(L−Lref)−g(m−2),A=N(1Kd,I+Lref−1Kd,A+Lref).F_\mathrm{level}(L,m)=\log2+A(L-L_\mathrm{ref})-g(m-2),\qquad A=N\left(\frac1{K_{d,I}+L_\mathrm{ref}}-\frac1{K_{d,A}+L_\mathrm{ref}}\right).

The coefficient A≃5.881 μM−1A\simeq5.881\,\mu\mathrm M^{-1} matches the ligand derivative of the finite-affinity exponent at the reference concentration. The same methylation feedback still restores a∗=1/3a^*=1/3. But doubling a larger ligand background now creates a larger change in the exponent, so the transient response depends strongly on the background.

The computed differences make “approximate” measurable. For backgrounds 0.1, 1 and 10 micromolar, the initial normalized responses a/a∗−1a/a^*-1 are −0.969, −0.975 and −0.961. The full traces differ by as much as 0.083 in this normalization. Below the affinity window, a doubling from 0.0005 micromolar gives only −0.177. The ideal logarithmic model instead gives identical traces, with initial value −0.977 for every background.

Fold-change detection does not uniquely identify integral feedback. Goentoro and colleagues show that an incoherent feedforward loop can also produce this property in a suitable kinetic regime. Its intermediate repressor retains information about past input levels. The absence of a feedback loop does not imply absence of memory.5

Test fold-change detection by overlaying complete responses from matched adapted states. Prove the input-and-memory symmetry, then determine the affinity and capacity range over which it applies.

4Solve the constrained action and test whether rejection is feasible

No control rule can remove material faster than its actuator allows. Álvaro Bátrez uses a small predictive-control problem to connect optimization with an explicit bounded feedback law. The calculation also separates infeasible demand from poor controller tuning.

Use discrete time labeled tt. The output y[t]y[t] is a normalized inventory deviation from its desired level zero. A constant unknown disturbance adds w≥0w\geq0 units per sampling interval, while the actuator removes u[t]u[t] units. Its capacity is umax>0u_\mathrm{max}>0:

y[t+1]=y[t]+w−u[t],0≤u[t]≤umax.y[t+1]=y[t]+w-u[t],\qquad 0\leq u[t]\leq u_\mathrm{max}.

Suppose the controller predicts one step ahead using an estimate w^[t]\widehat w[t]. Choose the current action to minimize squared predicted error:

u[t]=argmin⁡0≤u≤umax(y[t]+w^[t]−u)2=clip⁡(y[t]+w^[t],0,umax).u[t]=\underset{0\leq u\leq u_\mathrm{max}}{\operatorname{argmin}} \bigl(y[t]+\widehat w[t]-u\bigr)^2 =\operatorname{clip}\bigl(y[t]+\widehat w[t],0,u_\mathrm{max}\bigr).

The clipping operation limits its first argument to the stated interval. The unconstrained minimum sets the predicted next output to zero. If that action lies outside the interval, the nearest endpoint minimizes the same convex quadratic. No online numerical optimizer is needed for this one-step problem.

The previous transition supplies an exact disturbance estimate under noiseless observation and this plant model. For t≥1t\geq1,

w^[t]=y[t]−y[t−1]+u[t−1]=w.\widehat w[t]=y[t]-y[t-1]+u[t-1]=w.

Substituting the estimate gives an incremental proportional-integral form with unit coefficients:

u[t]=clip⁡(u[t−1]+(y[t]−y[t−1])+y[t],0,umax).u[t]=\operatorname{clip}\bigl(u[t-1]+(y[t]-y[t-1])+y[t],0,u_\mathrm{max}\bigr).

The proportional increment is y[t]−y[t−1]y[t]-y[t-1], and the integral increment uses the current observed error y[t]y[t]. The stored previous input is the applied, bounded action. The clipping remains part of the equation. This equivalence belongs to the specified plant, objective, estimator and coefficients. It does not identify every predictive controller or a biochemical antithetic controller with this law.

The failure under excessive disturbance follows without solving the controller. If w>umaxw>u_\mathrm{max}, then every admissible action obeys

y[t+1]−y[t]≥w−umax>0.y[t+1]-y[t]\geq w-u_\mathrm{max}>0.

Thus zero-error steady balance is impossible. In the example, error must increase by at least 0.3 per step. Bounding the stored applied input prevents that controller state from winding up without bound, but it cannot make an infeasible inventory balance feasible. Saturation behavior and adaptation feasibility are separate questions.

The boundary case also matters. At w=umaxw=u_\mathrm{max}, zero-error balance is possible, but the actuator has no spare capacity to remove an existing positive inventory error. Holding a target and recovering from a displaced state impose different requirements.

Solve the bounded action before interpreting a failed adaptation trace. Compare disturbance demand with actuator capacity, and retain the actual clipping in any equivalent feedback formula.

Part III

Specify the measured signal and the average being regulated

Differencing changes observation noise. Nonlinear reactions change how fluctuations contribute to a mean.

5Propagate sample covariance before choosing a differencing interval

Rapid differencing can amplify observation error even when the underlying signal changes smoothly. Haobo Zheng derives the cost for independent sample errors. The disturbance estimator in the previous section contains exactly such a difference of measured outputs, so its noiseless identity needs an observation model before practical use.

Let the measured sample be yobs[t]=y[t]+ϵ[t]y_\mathrm{obs}[t]=y[t]+\epsilon[t]. The index tt increases by one, and successive observations are separated by a physical interval Δt\Delta t. Assume zero-mean stationary measurement errors with variance σ2\sigma^2 and adjacent-sample correlation ρ(Δt)\rho(\Delta t). The finite-difference estimate and its measurement-error contribution are

y˙^[t]=yobs[t+1]−yobs[t]Δt,ϵD[t]=ϵ[t+1]−ϵ[t]Δt.\widehat{\dot y}[t]=\frac{y_\mathrm{obs}[t+1]-y_\mathrm{obs}[t]}{\Delta t},\qquad \epsilon_D[t]=\frac{\epsilon[t+1]-\epsilon[t]}{\Delta t}.

The estimate targets the secant slope over the interval. Any difference between that slope and an instantaneous derivative is a separate approximation error. Expanding the variance of the two sample errors gives the exact measurement-noise relation

Var⁡(ϵD)=Var⁡(ϵ[t+1])+Var⁡(ϵ[t])−2Cov⁡(ϵ[t+1],ϵ[t])Δt2=2σ2(1−ρ(Δt))Δt2.\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_D)= \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon[t+1])+\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon[t])-2\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon[t+1],\epsilon[t])}{\Delta t^2} =\frac{2\sigma^2(1-\rho(\Delta t))}{\Delta t^2}.

For independent samples with fixed variance, the derivative-error standard deviation is 2σ/Δt\sqrt2\sigma/\Delta t. With σ=0.01\sigma=0.01 output units, intervals of 1, 0.5 and 0.25 minutes give 0.0141, 0.0283 and 0.0566 output units per minute. Halving the interval doubles this noise level.

Correlation changes that conclusion quantitatively. An exponential covariance with correlation time tcorr>0t_\mathrm{corr}>0 has ρ=e−Δt/tcorr\rho=e^{-\Delta t/t_\mathrm{corr}}. At small intervals, the derivative-error standard deviation grows as Δt−1/2\Delta t^{-1/2}. A smoother covariance, ρ=e−Δt2/(2tcorr2)\rho=e^{-\Delta t^2/(2t_\mathrm{corr}^2)}, instead gives a finite limit σ/tcorr\sigma/t_\mathrm{corr}. These are distinct mathematical models of the sensor noise.

Neighboring difference estimates also share a sample. Even if the original errors are independent, ϵD[t]\epsilon_D[t] and ϵD[t+1]\epsilon_D[t+1] have covariance −σ2/Δt2-\sigma^2/\Delta t^2 and correlation −1/2. A long list of derivative estimates is therefore not a list of independent noise observations.

The appropriate experiment measures the sensor before tuning the controller around it. Estimate error variance and autocovariance at the intended exposure and sampling settings. Averaging over a different exposure can change both. The fixed-variance independent-sample result alone cannot establish whether a faster physical sensor will improve or worsen control.

Propagate the covariance of the actual measurements through the estimator. Keep sample noise, finite-difference approximation error and closed-loop stability as separate quantities.

6Separate nonlinear mean-rate bias from a controller's mean constraint

Zero-mean input fluctuations can shift a nonlinear reaction's mean rate. Peng Su adds this question to the adaptation discussion. It concerns the shape of the rate law around the mean input. It does not by itself determine which mean a complete feedback circuit regulates.

Let a positive fluctuating input be X=x(1+η)X=x(1+\eta), where x=⟨X⟩>0x=\langle X\rangle>0, ⟨η⟩=0\langle\eta\rangle=0 and ⟨η2⟩=CV2\langle\eta^2\rangle=\mathrm{CV}^2. Here CV\mathrm{CV} is the coefficient of variation. For a smooth positive instantaneous rate law v(X)v(X) and sufficiently small fluctuations, Taylor expansion gives

⟨v(X)⟩−v(x)v(x)≃x2v′′(x)2v(x) CV2.\frac{\langle v(X)\rangle-v(x)}{v(x)} \simeq\frac{x^2v''(x)}{2v(x)}\,\mathrm{CV}^2.

The linear fluctuation term averages to zero. The curvature term need not. This expansion assumes that the relevant rate can be evaluated at the instantaneous input. Hidden kinetic filtering or large excursions require a fuller dynamical model and a check of higher moments.

Reaction order makes the curvature coefficient interpretable without confusing it with a slope. Define the local logarithmic reaction order H(x)=dlog⁡v/dlog⁡xH(x)=d\log v/d\log x. Since v′=Hv/xv'=Hv/x, differentiating once more gives the exact identity

x2v′′v=H2−H+dHdlog⁡x.\frac{x^2v''}{v}=H^2-H+\frac{dH}{d\log x}.

A single reaction order does not determine the mean bias. Its change with input also matters. This is a direct connection to Lecture 11, where local reaction orders organize the linear response. Nonlinear averaging asks for one derivative beyond that local slope.

For an illustration, use a Hill rate v(x)=Vmaxxm/(Km+xm)v(x)=V_\mathrm{max}x^m/(K^m+x^m), with exponent m>0m>0 and half-activity concentration K>0K>0. Here mm is the Hill exponent, unrelated to the methylation coordinate used earlier. At x=Kx=K, the curvature coefficient is x2v′′/(2v)=−m/4x^2v''/(2v)=-m/4. Thus a coefficient of variation of 0.2 predicts a relative mean-rate shift of −1% for m=1m=1 and −4% for m=4m=4.

An ideal integral identity can still constrain a stationary mean in the presence of such nonlinear rates. For the antithetic controller without additional controller leakage, the common comparison reaction changes both controller species by the same amount. In a stochastic reaction model, its contribution therefore cancels in the expected difference:

ddt⟨Z1−Z2⟩=μ−θ⟨X⟩.\frac{d}{dt}\langle Z_1-Z_2\rangle=\mu-\theta\langle X\rangle.

Use random concentrations at fixed volume, or equivalently consistently scaled molecular counts. If a stationary distribution exists and the required expectation balances are finite, stationarity forces ⟨X⟩=μ/θ\langle X\rangle=\mu/\theta. The plant's nonlinear rates can change ⟨v(X)⟩\langle v(X)\rangle and the output distribution while this linear sensed mean remains fixed. Establishing stationarity and moment conditions is essential to the stochastic adaptation claim.1

A nonlinear sensor constrains a different average. If sensor production has rate h(X)h(X), the same cancellation gives ⟨h(X)⟩=μ\langle h(X)\rangle=\mu at stationarity. It does not generally give h(⟨X⟩)=μh(\langle X\rangle)=\mu. Memory leakage, an unavailable steady balance or a boundary that invalidates the expectation balance can change the conclusion again.

A useful next experiment measures both the controlled abundance and the sensor reaction output while changing the fluctuation amplitude. Keep the mean applied input and controller reference fixed. Test whether the mean abundance stays constant, whether the mean sensor rate stays constant, and whether the reaction output shifts as curvature predicts. Those observations distinguish a change in averaging from a failure of the proposed integral mechanism.

Name the random quantity inside the integral balance. Calculate nonlinear averaging separately, then determine whether the controller pins a mean abundance, a mean sensor response or neither.

Models and provenance

This chapter uses six accepted Week 5 contributions from repository snapshot 0ba6ab29, retrieved on 19 October 2026. The other four submissions appear in the Lecture 9 audience extension. Attribution identifies the submitted question or construction. The synthesis supplies the consistent formulas, scope qualifications and independent numerical checks.

Set-point derivatives were checked by independent finite perturbations. Leaky-controller trajectories were compared with matrix exponentials. Fold-change simulations were integrated with two numerical methods and tested against the ideal symmetry. Constrained actions were checked against the quadratic objective and inventory balance. Measurement-noise formulas were checked against independent sampled errors, including adjacent-difference correlation. Curvature was checked by finite differences and direct two-point averaging.
