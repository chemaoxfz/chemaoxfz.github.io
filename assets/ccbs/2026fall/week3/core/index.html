The shape of the reactor: dynamics and bioregulation

Flows, fixed points, time-scale separation — and the small library of behaviors a cell can actually build.

CCBS · CST 5034 · Fall 2026 · week 3 · core page · 4 student blocks

Week 2 turned reactions into equations; week 3 turns equations into a picture. The questions are: what can a network's trajectory do (1D, then 2D), what do the structural rules of chemistry forbid (Rule [P], [PU], [CP]), and what does time-scale separation buy you back. The payoff is a library: saturation, bottleneck, ultrasensitivity from one binding reaction, and the motif gallery — toggle, oscillators, feedback, pulse-fronts — that week 4 (control) and week 7 (computation) will build on.

Blocks and sessions

Block 1–2 = dynamics (from the 2025 lecture's §2.4–2.5); blocks 3–4 = time-scale separation and the building blocks (the 2025 lecture 3). Each block is one 30-minute student session with its TA-approved quiz.

Block 1

1D dynamics

One variable: it can approach, go away, or sit. That is all.

1Flows on a line

Fixed points f(x*) = 0; stable (●) vs unstable (○); anything else is decoration.

For ẋ = f(x) in one dimension, the whole analysis is: find zeros of f, classify them by the sign of f′. Two standard examples set the vocabulary:1

ẋ = −x ⇒ x(t) = x(0)e−t   (stable);    ẋ = x² ⇒ x(t) = x(0)/(1 − x(0)t)   (blow-up in finite time)

The second example is the first "details are fragile" object: trajectories escape to infinity at t = 1/x(0) — the answer depends on initial conditions in a way no linear system does. The morphological fact to internalize: on a line, trajectories either converge or escape — a 1D system can never "come back". Coming back requires a second dimension (centers, cycles).

The 1D limitation

Nothing that toggles, oscillates, or resets can be built in one dimension. Every decision to make in the course is ultimately about adding dimensions or time scales — which is why motifs come in pairs and why week 4's adaptation needs feedback plus memory.

2Bifurcations and the bistable construction

The saddle-node family ẋ = x² − r: no fixed points, one half-stable, two stable — and beyond it, multistability.

The canonical bifurcation is ẋ = x² − r: r > 0 gives two fixed points, r = 0 a half-stable one, r < 0 none. The lesson is not the equation but the habit: parameters move behaviors; the qualitative change is the bifurcation. Multistability sets in when the production term is S-shaped: ẋ = β xn/(Kn+xn) − x — three fixed points for the right β, and the cell gets a switch. That is the construction week 7 meets again as the toggle switch (mutual repression) and as multistable cell-fate circuits.2

Block 2

2D dynamics

Nullclines, linearization, and the four local shapes: node, spiral, saddle, center.

3Phase portraits and linearization

Draw ẋ = 0 and ẏ = 0; the crossings are fixed points; linearize and read the eigenvalues.

The method: nullclines (sets where one component of f vanishes) partition the plane into regions of known flow direction; intersections are fixed points. Linearize at a fixed point, A = ∂f/∂x(x*), and the resulting ẋ = Ax gives the local geometry:1 stable/unstable nodes (real eigenvalues, same sign), saddles (opposite signs), spirals (complex eigenvalues; in/out by sign of trace), and centers (purely imaginary — the mass-on-spring system mẍ + kx = 0, energy E = ½mẋ² + ½kx² conserved). Add friction and the center becomes a stable spiral: the same harmonic oscillator, damped.

A = ∂f/∂x|x*;   2D: tr A < 0, det A > 0 ⇔ Hurwitz (stable) (1)

4Stability criteria: Hurwitz in 2D

Local stability is a test on the matrix A: eigenvalues with negative real part, or the trace–determinant pair.

A is Hurwitz if every eigenvalue has Re λ < 0; then the fixed point is locally stable. In 2D the criterion collapses to the clean pair

tr A < 0  and  det A > 0 (2)

(the Routh–Hurwitz criterion). Note what is not covered: limit cycles — nonlinear oscillations with no fixed point — require a full Poincaré–Bendixson style argument (or a Lyapunov/bounding-box one). The repressilator (week 5's opening example) is exactly the failure of the linear test.3

Block 3

Time scales

Rules of the road: what CRNs must look like, and the separation-of-scales trick that escapes the rules.

5What CRNs may and may not do

Positivity ([P]), parameter uncertainty ([PU]) and constrained polynomials ([CP]) restrict which dynamical systems a chemical network can realize.

Equation (3) of week 2 was a subset of arbitrary dynamical systems. The constraints, as the lecture states them:1

  • Rule [P] — the state stays nonnegative: the positive orthant is forward-invariant. A system that crosses x = 0 is not a CRN.
  • Rule [PU] — biological parameters are uncertain. A circuit whose behavior depends on exact coincidence of rates is not reliably buildable.
  • Rule [CP] — the vector field is a constrained polynomial: each species' dynamics are sums of monomials with the reaction's own stoichiometry. Answers to "is ẋ = x − μ possible?" and "is the rotation matrix [[0,−1],[1,0]] possible?" are: not directly — which is precisely why composite steps and time-scale separation are the cell's workarounds.
A recurring quiz question

Name the rule a proposed circuit violates. If the answer is "none," ask which parameter coincidence it silently relies on.

6QSSA and the enzyme master derivation

Fast variables are slaved by slow ones: the complex C is quasisteady, and the resulting law comes straight from the algebra.

The time-scale separation argument: E + S ⇌ C → E + P with the complex fast relative to the substrate/product. Set dC/dt ≈ 0 (the quasi-steady-state assumption). The binding equilibrium gives 1

C = ES/K,   K = (koff + kcat)/kon,   C = ½[(Etot+Stot+K) ± √((Etot+Stot+K)² − 4EtotStot)] (3)

Two regimes emerge from the quadratic's two branches — substrate-excess and enzyme-excess — and the realized rate is a different function in each. The general principle: slow variables see fast ones at their quasisteady value. Gene expression uses the same trick (transcription fast, protein slow); week 6's equilibrium physics is its thermodynamic form; week 5's master equation is its stochastic one.

Block 4

Building blocks

One binding reaction, three behaviors: saturation, bottleneck, ultrasensitivity — the LEGO of bioregulation.

7One binding reaction, three regimes

Time-scale separation turns the same elementary binding reaction into three different composite behaviors, each dominant in its own region of concentration space.

This is the lecture's central trick, and its title is time-scale separation and archetypal behaviors of bioregulation: analyze a single binding reaction (with its catalytic conversion and turnover) in each dominance regime, and it produces Saturation, Bottleneck, and Ultrasensitivity as distinct composite behaviors.1

Why the course cares

Ultrasensitivity is how a cell builds a switch out of reversible chemistry; it is the entry point to bistability, and week 7 shows binding-based computation exploiting exactly these regimes (SAT/ultrasensitive fronts, titration).

8The LEGO of bioregulation

Beyond the three archetypes: Hill functions, competitive binding, and the design question — what can a cell build from a constrained set of blocks?

The homework of the 2025 lecture sets the LEGO question explicitly: the building blocks are the elementary reaction + its regimes; the bricks are Hill-type regulation, competitive binding, and the titration sponge; and the exercise is to say what a given combination does.1 The design discipline is stronger than it sounds: any circuit the students propose in later weeks can be checked — is the production term S-shaped enough, are the parameters in a dominance regime, is there a hidden parameter coincidence?

9What each motif buys

A one-definition tour of the gallery: toggle, oscillator, positive/negative feedback, feedforward, pulse-front — and the cost of each.

  • Toggle switch (mutual repression): two stable states, memory. Needs enough cooperativity (Hill ≥ 2) or it collapses — week 7's nullcline argument.
  • Negative feedback: reduces sensitivity, speeds relaxation, can oscillate with delay (repressilator-style; the linear test says stable — week 5's stochastic counterpoint).
  • Positive feedback: amplification and decision-making; costs bistability (and noise-latching, week 5).
  • Feedforward: the tool of week 4 — incoherent feedforward gives perfect adaptation; coherent feedforward gives pulse-detection and filtering.
  • Pulse/ultrasensitive front: time-scale separation as the miniaturization of a differentiation step.
The week's exercise

Take the two-gene toggle with Hill-2 repression; plot nullclines for three values of the leak parameter; identify where the middle intersection appears; then add a third gene and predict what your nullcline picture does. Any simulation is fair game.
