The adaptation biomachine: integral feedback, feedforward, and reaction order

Perfect adaptation is not a metaphor for integral control. It is integral control. And then biology produces a feedforward version that no circuit textbook would write.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · lecture 10 · core page · 2026-10-08 (Thu) 10:40–12:15 · students present, lecturer-backed

Holding an output constant against a persistent disturbance is the most-studied control problem in biology, and the answer is a theorem: perfect adaptation requires an integrator. This lecture derives that, finds the integrator in chemotaxis, and then shows the incoherent feedforward loop doing the same job by a route that only works because the parts are biochemical. The structural reason it works is reaction order, which sets up Lecture 11.

Part 1

The problem

Inputs, outputs, set-points, and why the obvious feedforward solution fails.

1Inputs, outputs, set-points

A controller has an input, a measure, a target, and a correction. The biology that does this is the cell's homeostatic machinery.

Look at any biological control problem the same way: disturbance (the environment changing), measurement (a sensor molecule), target (the concentration the cell wants), controller (what does the correction) and actuator (what the correction changes). The language is deliberately engineering-shaped: the claim of the biomachine program is that cells are machines in this precise sense — the question of what is governed, measured, and corrected is a biological one with a false-choice trap: too much plant-metaphor (nothing is "designed") or too little (no function at all).

2Why feedforward fails

The canonical discrete example: without memory, proportional and pure feedforward control leave an offset; only the integrator kills it.

The lecture's scalar example runs the whole argument in one line: a plant x[t+1] = x[t] + w + u[t] — the state is exactly the running sum of input w and control u. Feedforward set u = −w works only if w is known; feedback of a constant is ambiguous; the controller that actually cancels a persistent w is the one whose output is proportional to the accumulated error:1

u = kpy + ki∫y dt + kdẏ (1)

PID, in one line. The P term reacts, the D term anticipates, the I term memorizes. And for adaptation, only the I term matters: a system with perfect adaptation must contain a variable that is untouched by the steady input — an internal estimate of the disturbance. Remove the integrator and the steady state moves with the input; that is the difference between "adapt" and "resist".

Part 2

The integrator

Adaptation is integral feedback, and the internal model principle says why.

3Adaptation ⇔ integral feedback

If a system adapts perfectly to a persistent input, it contains an internal variable that integrates the error — and conversely.

The equivalence, argued in the lecture via the integrable-error argument: write the error e = y − y*. Perfect adaptation means e → 0 for any constant input. Consider ż = e — the error's integral. If the controller sees only y and the input w ranges over a continuum, then at steady state both e and ẋ disappear, and the only way the control law stays consistent for every w is that the controller carries an internal reference that is itself driven through the integrator. Compactly: no integrator, no perfect adaptation; integrator, adaptation. The Internal Model Principle is the theorem form of the same sentence: the controller must contain a model of the disturbance class it cancels — here, the constant (a pure integrator).1

4The Internal Model Principle

An electrical circuit implements the integrator with an inductor or capacitor — and that's exactly what biology cannot buy.

In electrical circuits the implementation is physical and cheap: a resistor gives proportional control (P), an inductor or a capacitor gives the integrating element (I). The lecture does the mapping explicitly: electric P = resistance; electric I = the charge/current integrator; D = inductance effects. The reason to spell this out is the next block's question: what is the biochemical analogue of an inductor? If it doesn't exist, biology must build the integrator some other way — and the constraints of lecture 4 (rules [P], [CP] — no negative entries in the vector field, no free parameters) say precisely what those other ways can look like.

5Rules [P]/[CP] against the integrator

A reaction network cannot implement the naive integral controller: the rules forbid it. Strategy 1: an antithetic (virtual) variable. Strategy 2: constrain yourself to a regime.

The obstacle, stated in the notes: chemical networks can only add, multiply, and degrade positive quantities (rules [P]/[CP]), so the pure difference-integral controller of (1) is not realizable with constant-rate parameters — the naive negative term cannot be made from positive physical chemistry at fixed rate constants. Two strategies follow.

  • Strategy 1 — the virtual variable (antithetic controller): add two species that mutually annihilate, z₁ + z₂ → ∅, one produced by the input, one by the output. The difference z₁ − z₂ is only expressible as annihilation, not as a negative rate, yet with ż = μ − x-type dynamics the pair is an integrator. This is the celebrated antithetic integral controller (the 2016/2019 Khammash-line designs flagged in the notes' margin).1
  • Strategy 2 — regime constraint: lock the system into a dominance regime (lecture 8: saturation/bottleneck/ultrasensitivity) so that the effective law is the one you want — parameter coincidence replaced by regime design.
Part 3

The twist

The incoherent feedforward loop, and where the engineering analogy holds and breaks.

6The twist: IFFL

Here comes the twist: the cell's canonical adaptation device is not a matched integrator at all — it's an incoherent feedforward loop, and no parameter matching is needed.

Milo and Shen-Orr (2002) found that the dominant adaptive motif in E. coli is the incoherent feedforward loop: an input activates two branches, one activating and one repressing the output, with the two branches tuned so their effects cancel in steady state.2 The lecture's worked example (the Saffer-style module) is explicit:1

ẋ₁ = k₁w − δx₁x₂,   ẋ₂ = k₂w − γx₂  ⇒  x₁* = k₁γ/(k₂δ) (2)

The steady-state output x₁* is a constant function of the input w — perfect adaptation — and it requires no parameter matching: the input appears in both branches at different powers and cancels identically. This is the headline claim of the day, and the line that differs from the textbook engineering answer: IFFL works in biology but not in traditional engineering — its adaptation is exact but its usability hinges on parameters the engineer would rather control — and the lecture's discussion of the matter is the week's debate material. (Compare: the antithetic controller adapts robustly to parameter coincidence; the IFFL adapts exactly but delicately.)

7Where the analogy holds

Analysis discipline transfers completely: identify the variable, the feedback, the set-point; the truth of controllers in cells is not decorative.

What transfers first-class: the analysis discipline. Declare the input/output/measurement/target; ask which controller class the circuit implements; predict the closed-loop behavior under a step input, then measure it. The chemotaxis system is the classic success: it is an integral controller (the methylation bundle being the memory), and the description organizes decades of experiments into one feedback diagram. The discipline also buys you immediate falsification: if a circuit adapts, someone must be able to identify the memory — the search becomes a biological question with a well-posed answer.

8Where it breaks

The analogy breaks where the medium differs: no current, no distances, no library of parts, no precision, and evolution as the designer.

Four mismatches, all of which the debate this block should engage:

  • No free parameters. An engineer tunes a resistor; the cell's parameters are set by evolution, with rule [PU] (uncertainty) as a constraint. The cell's "robust designs" are the ones that work across parameter space, not at a point.
  • No separation of sensing and actuation. Every sensor is in the control loop's physics — measurements consume molecules, and the "transducer" is the signal.
  • Cost. Every bit of control costs energy and time (lecture 7); the design objective is fitness, not set-point accuracy — often a trade-off (lecture 14's survival-vs-growth tradeoff is the same argument in the growth frame).
  • No designer. The phrase "the cell uses IFFL because…" is shorthand; the honest form is "the class of circuits that survive selection contains IFFLs".
