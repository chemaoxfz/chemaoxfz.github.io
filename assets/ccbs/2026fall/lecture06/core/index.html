Timescale separation: singular perturbation, QSSA, and the telegraph model

Fast and slow are not a convenience. Made rigorous by singular perturbation, they turn an intractable network into a small one, deterministically and stochastically.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · lecture 6 · core page · 2026-09-17 (Thu) 10:40–12:15 · students present, lecturer-backed

Every model in this course is a reduced model, and timescale separation is the reduction. Done casually it is the quasi-steady-state assumption you already met in Michaelis-Menten. Done rigorously it is singular perturbation, with an error bound and a condition for when it fails. The same idea then reduces a stochastic gene-expression model to the telegraph model, which is where bursting comes from.

  • The bridge: the telegraph model IS the stochastic QSSA of the same network, so the two halves of the lecture are one argument.
Part 1

The deterministic reduction

Fast enzymatic reactions, the quasi-steady-state assumption, and the derivation done properly.

1QSSA and the enzyme master derivation

Fast variables are slaved by slow ones: the complex C is quasisteady, and the resulting law comes straight from the algebra.

The time-scale separation argument: E + S ⇌ C → E + P with the complex fast relative to the substrate/product. Set dC/dt ≈ 0 (the quasi-steady-state assumption). The binding equilibrium gives 1

C = ES/K,   K = (koff + kcat)/kon,   C = ½[(Etot+Stot+K) ± √((Etot+Stot+K)² − 4EtotStot)] (3)

Two regimes emerge from the quadratic's two branches — substrate-excess and enzyme-excess — and the realized rate is a different function in each. The general principle: slow variables see fast ones at their quasisteady value. Gene expression uses the same trick (transcription fast, protein slow); lecture 7's equilibrium physics is its thermodynamic form; lecture 5's master equation is its stochastic one.

Part 2

The stochastic reduction

The same separation applied to gene expression: promoter states, bursts, and the Fano factor.

2Burstiness and the Fano factor

Gene expression arrives in bursts: the distribution is not Poisson, the variance exceeds the mean — and single cells are bimodal.

Genes fire in pulses: mRNAs are born in bursts and translated in bursts, so a single-gene reporter's histogram is heavier-tailed than Poisson. The Fano factor (above) is the diagnostic; in the lecture's examples, mean ≈ variance is replaced by F > 1 — an immediate quantitative statement that the "noise" is bursty, not diffusive.3 And because the production rate itself is a state (gene on/off), the effective description of a gene is a two-level input filtered by degradation — the bursts' size and duration are design knobs, and they parameterize how much noise a cell tolerates before a design changes.
