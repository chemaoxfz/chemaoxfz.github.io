The growth biomachine

How nutrients, ribosomes and productive investment determine the rate at which a cell builds itself.

CCBS · EST 5053 · Fall 2026 · Lecture 14 · 27 October · Core developed from the 2025 growth lecture and the lecturer’s 2026 narrative

Lecture 13 budgets the machinery. Lecture 14 explains how the machinery builds itself. An enzyme can process a substrate only while the enzyme exists. Making more enzyme uses ribosomes, amino acids and time. Making more ribosomes uses those same resources. Growth therefore couples the operation of the machine to the production of the machine.

We will build one model in stages. Each stage answers a question left open by the previous one. The route starts with the measured relation between nutrients and growth. It ends with an allocation problem that predicts both the growth rate and the composition of the growing cell. The optional directions at the end record the next questions about changing environments and populations.

The exposition gives the full derivations, solved transfers and a finite-precursor calculator. The lineage follows the experiments and modeling choices behind the argument.

The experiment that will test the argument

Both poor nutrients and a ribosome-targeting antibiotic can slow growth. Should the cell contain more ribosomal machinery or less? A growth curve alone cannot answer. The supply-and-production model below predicts opposite composition changes.

Part I

Use growth phenomenology to locate the missing mechanism

External conditions change growth. The internal composition tells us what must be explained.

1Growth requires the cell to produce its own machinery

The product is another functioning cell, with another set of catalysts.

A growing cell differs from a reaction mixture with a fixed inventory of enzymes. The reaction mixture can convert material while its machinery stays fixed. A growing culture increases its biomass and, during sustained balanced growth, its enzyme and ribosome inventories. Division distributes that enlarged material between descendants. A model of sustained growth must account for this reproduction of productive capacity.

FBA’s biomass reaction records a material requirement. In Lecture 13, an aggregate reaction consumes metabolites in specified proportions to represent the production of biomass. This is useful accounting. The aggregate reaction does not itself describe the many synthesis and assembly processes that produce an enzyme, a ribosome or a membrane. Basic FBA can impose bounds on the flux. A mechanistic explanation of those bounds asks how the catalysts are made and how their production competes for resources.

Protein production is a useful place to start because protein is a large part of biomass and much of the catalytic machinery. A classic composition compilation assigns about 55% of the dry mass of a reference E. coli cell to protein. RNA accounts for about 20.5%. These are condition-specific values, not constants shared by every cell. The figure states the reference growth conditions. [1]

We must also specify what is growing. Let NN be the number of cells, BB their total dry biomass and VcellsV_{\mathrm{cells}} their total cellular volume. These quantities have the same specific growth rate only when the average mass per cell and the biomass density remain constant. Nutrient shifts can break these equalities. We will first consider a population in a fixed environment with a stable size distribution and composition.

Before interpreting a “growth flux,” identify the material it produces and the assumptions that convert that flux into biomass, volume or cell-number growth.

2External nutrient limitation explains a growth curve but leaves capacity open

Monod’s phenomenological law supplies the historical and logical beginning.

Exponential growth specifies how a constant rate accumulates. If every cell contributes the same average rate of population increase, the increment is proportional to the number already present. Write the specific rate as λ\lambda, in inverse hours. Then

dNdt=λN,N(t)=N(0)eλt,tdouble=ln⁡2λ.\frac{dN}{dt}=\lambda N,\qquad N(t)=N(0)e^{\lambda t},\qquad t_{\mathrm{double}}=\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}.

The equation makes a quantitative prediction once the rate is supplied. For example, a one-hour doubling time means λ=ln⁡2≃0.69  h−1\lambda=\ln 2\simeq0.69\;\mathrm h^{-1}. A value of one doubling per hour is therefore different from a natural-log growth rate of one per hour. The rate itself is still unexplained: why does the same organism double more slowly on one medium than another?

Monod related the rate to the concentration of a limiting external nutrient. Let cc be that concentration. Let λmax⁡\lambda_{\max} be the limiting rate at high concentration in the specified setting, and let KcK_c be the concentration at which the rate is half that limit. The empirical relation is

λ(c)=λmax⁡cKc+c.\pf{\lambda(c)=\lambda_{\max}\frac{c}{K_c+c}}.

At low nutrient concentration, adding nutrient produces a nearly proportional increase in growth. At high concentration, growth saturates. Monod’s 1949 review presents this relation and discusses how the limiting rate varies with the nutrient used. The hyperbolic form is a phenomenological description of whole-cell growth. Its resemblance to a Michaelis–Menten law does not identify a single rate-limiting enzyme. [2]

Saturation directs attention inside the cell. Once the nutrient is plentiful, some internal process must limit sustained production. Changing the carbon source can change the enzymes and processing effort needed to make precursors. Inhibiting ribosomes can change the capacity to turn those precursors into protein. A fitted λmax⁡\lambda_{\max} can absorb either effect, but does not tell us which mechanism changed.

A historical progression

Use 1949 for Monod’s review of nutrient-dependent growth, and 1950 for the contemporary continuous-culture developments. The next question is experimental: how can we hold a culture at a controlled growth rate long enough to measure its composition? [2] [3]

Use a Monod curve to distinguish limitation by external concentration from the unresolved internal capacity represented by its maximum rate.

3A chemostat separates growth from removal

A constant culture density can conceal continuous biomass production.

Continuous culture gives a reproducible way to examine growth below the organism’s capacity. Fresh medium flows into a well-mixed vessel at volume flow rate FF. The same volume leaves, so the vessel volume VtankV_{\mathrm{tank}} stays fixed. Define the dilution rate D=F/VtankD=F/V_{\mathrm{tank}}. This is an experimental removal rate, not an intrinsic cellular growth rate. Novick and Szilard’s 1950 description of the chemostat explicitly uses this separation. [3]

The biomass balance compares growth with washout. Let XX be dry biomass per unit vessel volume. Let YY be a constant biomass yield per mass of consumed nutrient. Ignoring death and maintenance consumption gives

X˙=[λ(c)−D]X,c˙=D(cin−c)−λ(c)XY.\begin{aligned}\dot X&=[\lambda(c)-D]X,\\\dot c&=D(c_{\mathrm{in}}-c)-\frac{\lambda(c)X}{Y}.\end{aligned}

In the first equation, cells produce biomass at rate λ(c)X\lambda(c)X and the effluent removes it at rate DXDX. In the second, inflow replaces depleted nutrient and growth consumes nutrient. These are culture-level balances. They do not yet describe the enzyme and ribosome inventories inside a cell.

A nonzero steady culture must satisfy λ(c∗)=D\lambda(c^*)=D. The nutrient concentration adjusts until growth replaces what leaves. The culture is stationary as a vessel inventory, while the cells continue synthesizing biomass. The imposed dilution rate therefore lets us compare compositions across reproducible growth conditions.

Calculate the residual nutrient and the washout boundary

Substitute the Monod relation into the nonzero steady-state condition. Solving for the residual nutrient gives c∗=KcD/(λmax⁡−D)c^*=K_cD/(\lambda_{\max}-D). The nutrient balance then gives X∗=Y(cin−c∗)X^*=Y(c_{\mathrm{in}}-c^*). A physical nonzero culture requires positive biomass, so its residual nutrient must be below the feed concentration.

0<D<λ(cin),c∗=KcDλmax⁡−D,X∗=Y(cin−c∗).0<D<\lambda(c_{\mathrm{in}}),\qquad c^*=\frac{K_cD}{\lambda_{\max}-D},\qquad X^*=Y(c_{\mathrm{in}}-c^*).

At and above the feed-supported capacity, the washout state has X∗=0X^*=0 and c∗=cinc^*=c_{\mathrm{in}}. The threshold depends on the finite feed concentration. It is below λmax⁡\lambda_{\max}. At exactly zero flow, a unique continuous-culture steady state is no longer selected by these formulas.

In a steady chemostat, infer cellular growth from the known removal rate while keeping vessel stationarity distinct from the absence of cellular production.

4Composition reveals the machinery behind the growth rate

The observed variable is RNA per protein mass. The proposed mechanism is translation capacity.

Balanced-growth experiments turned growth rate into a question about cellular composition. Schaechter, Maaløe and Kjeldgaard varied the medium and temperature for Salmonella typhimurium in 1958. Cell mass and nucleic-acid content changed systematically with the physiological state. Their analysis suggested an approximately constant protein-synthesis rate per unit RNA across the examined nutrient conditions. This was an early mechanistic clue, not the exact later linear law for every species and condition. [4]

Later measurements made the RNA/protein relation more explicit in E. coli. Dennis and Bremer’s 1974 study found a linear RNA/protein relation above about 1.2 doublings per hour in their conditions. Scott and colleagues revisited the relation in 2010 and tested several ways to perturb growth. The measurements below are their untreated nutrient series, transcribed from the numerical supplement. [5] [6]

The nutrient series below uses exponentially growing batch cultures. Changing the medium sets different balanced physiological states. It does not impose those growth rates by adjusting chemostat dilution. The two methods control different experimental inputs. Scott’s samples were collected after at least three generations of unperturbed exponential growth. [6]

RNA is an informative proxy because much of bacterial RNA belongs to ribosomes. Let MM denote total protein mass and let r=MRNA/Mr=M_{\mathrm{RNA}}/M denote the measured RNA/protein mass ratio. We will model a ribosomal sector: ribosomal proteins together with the affiliated translation machinery. Its protein mass is MRM_R, and its share of the proteome is ϕR=MR/M\phi_R=M_R/M. RNA mass is not itself a protein fraction.

Comparing the model with RNA measurements requires a conversion. Scott’s supplement estimates ϕR≃ρr\phi_R\simeq\rho r with ρ≃0.76\rho\simeq0.76 grams of ribosomal-sector protein per gram of total RNA. That estimate combines the rRNA share of RNA, the protein-to-rRNA mass ratio of ribosomes and an allowance for affiliated proteins. Severe limitation can change these ratios. We therefore plot the measured RNA/protein observable directly and use the conversion as an explicit model assumption. [6]

The nutrient series supplies a relation to explain, not a control law. Higher growth is associated with a larger ribosomal sector over the studied range. The data do not yet say whether ribosomes cause the higher growth, whether nutrient processing permits more ribosomes, or how the cell chooses the allocation. The next construction separates those questions.

Translate an RNA/protein measurement into a statement about ribosomal machinery only after declaring the mass denominator, sector definition and proxy conversion.

Part II

Construct the growth law from production and allocation

First explain why ribosomes matter. Then explain why a cell cannot devote all its protein to ribosomes.

5Ribosomes produce the composition that determines future production

The synthesis allocation is a decision about new protein. The existing protein fraction is a state.

Begin with an intentionally incomplete self-replicating machine. Suppose all protein belonged to the ribosomal sector and precursors were always supplied. Let σ\sigma be the effective rate of protein production per unit ribosomal-sector protein mass. Its unit is inverse time. Then M˙R=σMR\dot M_R=\sigma M_R. Ribosomal machinery makes more ribosomal machinery, so its amount grows exponentially at rate σ\sigma.

This effective capacity includes the conversion from molecular elongation speed to mass production. A ribosome’s raw speed in amino acids per second is not numerically interchangeable with σ\sigma. Transcription, rRNA production, ribosome assembly, energy supply and precursor supply are not yet resolved. The first model assumes they support the stated effective capacity.

The amounts can represent a population total or one cell between divisions. Summing protein mass across a growing population gives a continuous production variable over many generations. The same balances can describe a single cell between division events. Division partitions the existing amounts between daughters. It does not itself create biomass.

Now allow ribosomes to make other proteins. In the two-sector construction, call the remaining protein mass MEM_E. We will give that sector a metabolic function in the next section. Total protein mass is M=MR+MEM=M_R+M_E. The existing metabolic-sector fraction is ϕE=ME/M=1−ϕR\phi_E=M_E/M=1-\phi_R. Let fRf_R be the fraction of new protein synthesis assigned to the ribosomal sector. The rest, 1−fR1-f_R, makes the other sector. With negligible protein turnover,

vtr=σMR,M˙R=fRvtr,M˙E=(1−fR)vtr,M˙=vtr,λM≡M˙M=σϕR.\begin{aligned}v_{\mathrm{tr}}&=\sigma M_R,\\\dot M_R&=f_Rv_{\mathrm{tr}},\qquad \dot M_E=(1-f_R)v_{\mathrm{tr}},\\\dot M&=v_{\mathrm{tr}},\qquad \lambda_M\equiv\frac{\dot M}{M}=\sigma\phi_R.\end{aligned}

The variable vtrv_{\mathrm{tr}} is total protein-production rate, measured in protein mass per time. The symbol λM\lambda_M names protein-mass growth explicitly. Under balanced composition it becomes the common growth rate λ\lambda.

The fraction dynamics tell us how allocation becomes composition. We need this equation because a changed synthesis program does not instantly replace the proteins already present. Differentiate the ratio ϕR=MR/M\phi_R=M_R/M. The denominator grows as well as the numerator:

ϕ˙R=M˙RM−ϕRM˙M=λM(fR−ϕR)=σϕR(fR−ϕR).\begin{aligned}\dot\phi_R&=\frac{\dot M_R}{M}-\phi_R\frac{\dot M}{M}\\&=\lambda_M(f_R-\phi_R)\\&=\sigma\phi_R(f_R-\phi_R).\end{aligned}

If fRf_R exceeds the current fraction, new protein is richer in ribosomal machinery than the current proteome, so the fraction rises. If fRf_R is smaller, the expanding non-ribosomal sector dilutes that fraction. This is a compositional change even when no ribosomal protein is destroyed.

At a positive balanced-growth state, allocation equals abundance. If λM>0\lambda_M>0 and the fraction is stationary, the fraction equation forces ϕR=fR\phi_R=f_R. Consequently λ=σfR\lambda=\sigma f_R. This explains why more ribosomal machinery can support more growth. It still predicts that allocating everything to ribosomes is best. The missing process is the production of their precursors.

Derive an abundance fraction from its production allocation by differentiating the mass ratio. Do not set allocation equal to abundance before imposing positive balanced growth.

6Metabolic enzymes supply the precursors that translation consumes

Nutrient quality enters through a process carried by another part of the proteome.

Give the non-ribosomal sector the job that the first model omitted. Let the metabolic sector of mass MEM_E import and convert nutrients into amino-acid precursors. Write its effective supply capacity as vsup=k(c)MEv_{\mathrm{sup}}=k(c)M_E. The parameter k(c)k(c) is precursor production per unit metabolic protein mass, expressed in protein-mass equivalents per time. At a fixed external nutrient condition, abbreviate it as kk.

Changing nutrient identity can change this supply capacity. A different carbon source may require different import or metabolic machinery. Adding amino acids may bypass biosynthetic work. These effects need not be equivalent to changing the concentration of one nutrient in a Monod curve. The coarse parameter kk collects the supply performance of the stated metabolic sector under the stated environment.

A precursor pool makes the supply requirement explicit. Let MAM_A be the available precursor amount in protein-mass equivalents. The balance is M˙A=kME−vtr\dot M_A=kM_E-v_{\mathrm{tr}}. If translation demanded its full rate σMR\sigma M_R regardless of the pool, a supply deficit could drive the computed precursor amount negative. Such a continuation is not a physical growth model.

There are two useful levels of approximation. A small-pool capacity model asks how much protein production can be supported by both processes and uses the smaller capacity. A model retaining the precursor pool also tracks its accumulation and limits translation when the pool is depleted. We first use the small-pool model to derive the allocation prediction. Section 10 supplies an explicit positive dynamical realization and checks the finite-pool correction.

What “balance” will mean

Let a=MA/Ma=M_A/M be precursor amount per unit protein mass. If this ratio is constant during growth, the precursor amount still increases at rate λMA\lambda M_A. Dividing its balance by protein mass gives kϕE=λ(1+a)k\phi_E=\lambda(1+a). The simpler relation kϕE≃λk\phi_E\simeq\lambda uses the additional approximation a≪1a\ll1.

When adding nutrient dependence to a self-production model, supply both the enzyme-dependent precursor flux and a rule that prevents translation from consuming unavailable material.

7A shared protein budget balances supply and translation

The allocation optimum is an intersection of two capacities with opposite dependence on ribosomal fraction.

The two sectors compete for a fixed share of total protein. In the minimal construction, ϕE+ϕR=1\phi_E+\phi_R=1. More ribosomal protein raises translation capacity but leaves less metabolic protein to supply it. With negligible precursor inventory and sufficient control to use the limiting capacity, a balanced protein-growth rate obeys

λ≤k(1−ϕR),λ≤σϕR,0≤ϕR≤1.\lambda\leq k(1-\phi_R),\qquad \lambda\leq\sigma\phi_R,\qquad 0\leq\phi_R\leq1.

The maximum supported growth rate is the smaller of the two bounds. At low ribosomal fraction, translation is scarce. At high ribosomal fraction, metabolic supply is scarce. Producing still more of the already excessive sector does not remove the limiting process. This gives the triangular growth envelope below.

Maximizing growth selects the point where both capacities are used. If one bound were strictly smaller at an interior allocation, shifting a little protein toward the limiting process would increase the bound. The maximum therefore satisfies k(1−ϕR∗)=σϕR∗k(1-\phi_R^*)=\sigma\phi_R^*. Solving gives

ϕR∗=kk+σ,ϕE∗=σk+σ,λ∗=kσk+σ.\pf{\phi_R^*=\frac{k}{k+\sigma}},\qquad \phi_E^*=\frac{\sigma}{k+\sigma},\qquad \pf{\lambda^*=\frac{k\sigma}{k+\sigma}}.

This calculation explains an internal production limit. As nutrient processing becomes very effective, k→∞k\to\infty, the rate approaches the translation capacity σ\sigma. Faster processing cannot make a finite amount of translational machinery produce arbitrarily fast. Conversely, very fast translation leaves nutrient processing as the bottleneck.

Recover a Monod-shaped curve from internal production capacities

To close the opening question, let precursor supply itself saturate with the external nutrient. Introduce a maximum supply capacity kmax⁡k_{\max} and its half-saturation concentration KEK_E. In the same zero-offset, small-pool model with growth-maximizing allocation, substitute

k(c)=kmax⁡cKE+ck(c)=k_{\max}\frac{c}{K_E+c}

into the derived growth rate. The result is

λ∗(c)=σkmax⁡cσKE+(σ+kmax⁡)c=λmax⁡cKc+c,λmax⁡=σkmax⁡σ+kmax⁡,Kc=σKEσ+kmax⁡.\begin{aligned}\lambda^*(c)&=\frac{\sigma k_{\max}c}{\sigma K_E+(\sigma+k_{\max})c}\\&=\lambda_{\max}\frac{c}{K_c+c},\\\lambda_{\max}&=\frac{\sigma k_{\max}}{\sigma+k_{\max}},\qquad K_c=\frac{\sigma K_E}{\sigma+k_{\max}}.\end{aligned}

The whole-cell curve has a Monod form, but both fitted constants combine supply and translation properties. Its half-saturation KcK_c differs from the supply half-saturation KEK_E. Scott et al. develop this relation in their 2014 Box 2. [11]

Different internal capacities can give exactly the same growth curve. For a fixed observed λmax⁡\lambda_{\max} and KcK_c, any σ>λmax⁡\sigma>\lambda_{\max} can be paired with suitable supply parameters. The resulting ribosomal fractions differ because ϕR∗=λ∗/σ\phi_R^*=\lambda^*/\sigma. The exposition constructs and plots two such mechanisms. Measuring composition can distinguish what the growth curve leaves unresolved.

The optimum depends on an objective and on the environment. We optimized steady growth in a fixed environment and charged only these two protein sectors. The calculation does not prove that a cell implements the maximizing allocation. A cell can retain reserve proteins, prepare for future conditions or spend resources on survival. Those possibilities alter the objective or the budget.

Balance, capacity and regulation answer different questions

Supply can balance use while translation is substrate-limited or precursor accumulation inhibits supply. Balance alone therefore does not prove that both capacities are used efficiently. A regulatory mechanism must select an allocation and precursor range that support the predicted performance. Scott et al. (2014) analyze these additional conditions. [11]

8Nutrient changes and translation inhibition move composition in opposite directions

A useful model must distinguish two interventions that both change the growth rate.

Better nutrient supply raises the optimal ribosomal fraction. Hold translation capacity σ\sigma fixed and increase kk. Less metabolic protein is needed for a given supply rate, so more protein can be devoted to translation. Both growth and ribosomal fraction rise. Eliminating kk gives the increasing relation ϕR∗=λ∗/σ\phi_R^*=\lambda^*/\sigma.

Translation inhibition raises the ribosomal fraction while reducing growth. Hold nutrient capacity kk fixed and lower σ\sigma. More ribosomal machinery is needed for each unit of protein production. Its allocation increases, but the slower machinery supports less total growth. Eliminating σ\sigma gives the decreasing relation ϕR∗=1−λ∗/k\phi_R^*=1-\lambda^*/k.

The empirical comparison needs the protein sectors omitted by the first toy model. Let ϕQ\phi_Q be a fixed protein fraction outside the two adjustable sectors. Let ϕR,0\phi_{R,0} be the baseline ribosomal-sector fraction represented by the observed intercept. Define ϕR,max⁡=1−ϕQ\phi_{R,\max}=1-\phi_Q. The remaining metabolic fraction is ϕE=ϕR,max⁡−ϕR\phi_E=\phi_{R,\max}-\phi_R. With effective capacities κT\kappa_T and κN\kappa_N measured per unit protein fraction, the two phenomenological relations become

λ=κT(ϕR−ϕR,0),λ=κN(ϕR,max⁡−ϕR).\begin{aligned}\lambda&=\kappa_T(\phi_R-\phi_{R,0}),\\\lambda&=\kappa_N(\phi_{R,\max}-\phi_R).\end{aligned}

These capacities use a protein-fraction normalization. Scott’s parameters expressed against RNA/protein require the factor ρ\rho from Section 4. The nonzero intercept is an empirical offset with possible mechanistic contributions from inactive or reserve machinery. A growth-law fit alone does not identify its molecular origin.

The two directions survive the added offsets. Varying nutrient capacity at fixed translation capacity follows ϕR=ϕR,0+λ/κT\phi_R=\phi_{R,0}+\lambda/\kappa_T. Varying translation capacity at fixed nutrient capacity follows ϕR=ϕR,max⁡−λ/κN\phi_R=\phi_{R,\max}-\lambda/\kappa_N. Solving the pair also gives

λ=(ϕR,max⁡−ϕR,0)κNκTκN+κT.\lambda=(\phi_{R,\max}-\phi_{R,0})\frac{\kappa_N\kappa_T}{\kappa_N+\kappa_T}.

Chloramphenicol supplies the contrasting experiment. This ribosome-targeting antibiotic reduces effective translation capacity. Scott and colleagues measured RNA/protein at increasing chloramphenicol concentrations within each medium. The ratio rises as growth falls, in contrast to the untreated nutrient series. That qualitative intervention prediction is the key success of the allocation account. The empirical slopes and offsets require calibration. [6]

The scope is a physiological regime, not every form of slow growth. The fixed-sector and nearly constant-capacity assumptions work over particular ranges of steady conditions. Severe starvation, substantial turnover, altered ribosome activity and strong drug effects can change them. Even within the reported data, richer media have somewhat different extrapolated intercepts. The model’s value is that it states which capacity and allocation changes should be checked.

Use the sign of the composition response to distinguish nutrient limitation from translation limitation. Compare the measured observable with a model whose offsets and normalization are explicit.

Part III

Derive growth and composition from the same dynamics

An allocation budget describes what is supportable. A self-production model must also generate that state.

9Balanced growth keeps composition constant while amounts increase

A steady physiological state is an exponential trajectory in amount coordinates.

The meaning of “steady” depends on the observable. Let mim_i denote the amount of component ii. A balanced-growth trajectory has a fixed composition vector uu and a common specific rate λ\lambda:

mi(t)=uieλt,mi(t)mj(t)=uiuj.m_i(t)=u_i e^{\lambda t},\qquad \frac{m_i(t)}{m_j(t)}=\frac{u_i}{u_j}.

Every component increases, but all component ratios remain constant. A culture can therefore have a stable composition while doubling repeatedly. On a log-amount plot, balanced growth appears as parallel lines. A ratio plot shows the same state as a plateau.

Constant concentrations require volume to grow at the same rate as amounts. For a constituent with amount mim_i, define its concentration as qi=mi/Vq_i=m_i/V in cellular volume VV. The symbol qiq_i follows the course convention for a constituent total when several molecular states are included. If the net amount-production rate is FiF_i, differentiation gives

q˙i=FiV−qiV˙V.\dot q_i=\frac{F_i}{V}-q_i\frac{\dot V}{V}.

The familiar dilution term is a consequence of the growing denominator. Define volume growth as λV=V˙/V\lambda_V=\dot V/V. Then q˙i=Fi/V−λVqi\dot q_i=F_i/V-\lambda_Vq_i. Nothing has to destroy the constituent to reduce its concentration. Volume growth can dilute it. Constant biomass density and balanced composition allow us to identify λV\lambda_V, protein-mass growth and biomass growth with the common rate λ\lambda.

Growth dilution and chemostat washout are different processes. The dilution rate DD in Section 3 removes cells from a fixed vessel. The term λVqi\lambda_Vq_i changes concentration inside growing cellular volume. A model can contain both. It must not silently substitute one for the other outside the nonzero steady-chemostat condition.

The balance that a growing-cell model must close

A concentration model needs a production law and a volume law. Writing q˙=Sv−λq\dot q=S v-\lambda q is incomplete until the flux normalization, the growth rate and the relationship between volume and produced material are specified. Balanced growth can justify that closure in a fixed environment. A nutrient shift requires checking it again.

Convert an amount model into a concentration model by differentiating through volume. Use parallel log-amount trajectories and constant composition ratios as two tests of balanced growth.

10A consistent growth eigenmode predicts both the rate and the composition

The eigenvalue calculation is useful only inside a physically possible limitation regime.

We now close the precursor balance without permitting negative precursor amounts. Retain the three amounts MA,ME,MRM_A,M_E,M_R. Hold the environment, capacities and synthesis allocation fixed. Introduce kAk_A, an effective precursor-consumption capacity per unit precursor amount, in inverse hours. Choose the explicit composite translation law

vtr=min⁡(σMR,kAMA).v_{\mathrm{tr}}=\min(\sigma M_R,k_A M_A).

The first argument limits translation by ribosomal machinery. The second limits it by available precursor. This piecewise-linear law is a deliberate coarse model, not an elementary reaction mechanism. It makes translation vanish at zero precursor and at zero ribosomal machinery.

All three balances now belong to one growing system. Metabolic enzymes supply the precursor pool. Translation removes precursor and produces both protein sectors:

M˙A=kME−vtr,M˙E=(1−fR)vtr,M˙R=fRvtr.\begin{aligned}\dot M_A&=kM_E-v_{\mathrm{tr}},\\\dot M_E&=(1-f_R)v_{\mathrm{tr}},\\\dot M_R&=f_Rv_{\mathrm{tr}}.\end{aligned}

On the boundary MA=0M_A=0, translation is zero and M˙A=kME≥0\dot M_A=kM_E\geq0. On the boundary MR=0M_R=0, translation is again zero. The equations therefore preserve nonnegative amounts. The available precursor pool supplies the extra state needed to reconcile production capacity with material availability.

Inside either limitation regime, the amount equations are linear. Put the amounts in the order m=(MA,ME,MR)Tm=(M_A,M_E,M_R)^{\mathsf T}. In a regime with a fixed active minimum, write m˙=Bm\dot m=B m. A balanced trajectory m(t)=ueλtm(t)=u e^{\lambda t} then requires Bu=λuBu=\lambda u. The eigenvalue gives a candidate growth rate. The eigenvector gives its candidate composition.

Check kA(uA)A≤σ(uA)Rk_A(u_A)_A\leq\sigma(u_A)_R, equivalently λA≤σfR\lambda_A\leq\sigma f_R.

The precursor component of the eigenvector closes the mass balance. In either growth eigenmode, the protein fractions equal the synthesis allocations, as in Section 5. The precursor ratio aa is generally nonzero. The balance is therefore k(1−fR)=λ(1+a)k(1-f_R)=\lambda(1+a). The extra precursor stock must grow along with the protein stock.

Check two explicit matrices and their compositions

Take illustrative capacities k=2k=2, σ=3\sigma=3 and kA=8k_A=8, all in inverse hours. First allocate fR=1/3f_R=1/3 to the ribosomal sector. The translation-limited candidate is

BR=(02−3002001),uR=(1/32/31/3),BRuR=1⋅uR.B_R=\begin{pmatrix}0&2&-3\\0&0&2\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad u_R=\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\2/3\\1/3\end{pmatrix},\qquad B_Ru_R=1\cdot u_R.

Its precursor-supported capacity is kA(uR)A=8/3k_A(u_R)_A=8/3, larger than the ribosomal capacity σ(uR)R=1\sigma(u_R)_R=1. The assumed regime is consistent. Protein mass, precursor amount and each protein sector grow at one per hour in this growth eigenmode. The entries of uRu_R sum to more than one because the normalization is to protein mass, not to protein plus precursor.

Next choose fR=1/2f_R=1/2. The consistent precursor-limited matrix is

BA=(−820400400),λA=26−4≃0.899  h−1,uA=(λA/81/21/2).B_A=\begin{pmatrix}-8&2&0\\4&0&0\\4&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \lambda_A=2\sqrt6-4\simeq0.899\;\mathrm h^{-1},\quad u_A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_A/8\\1/2\\1/2\end{pmatrix}.

Here kA(uA)A=λA≃0.899k_A(u_A)_A=\lambda_A\simeq0.899 is below σ(uA)R=1.5\sigma(u_A)_R=1.5. The regime is again consistent. By contrast, the translation-limited calculation at this allocation would require a=2(1/2)/(3/2)−1=−1/3a=2(1/2)/(3/2)-1=-1/3. That eigenvector must be rejected even though its candidate growth eigenvalue is larger.

A dominant eigenvalue describes sustained growth when its growth eigenmode is physically admissible and dynamically selected. For each matrix above, the positive growth eigenvalue is the eigenvalue with largest real part when 0<fR<10<f_R<1 and the capacities are positive. That fact is not enough by itself. The translation-limited matrix contains a negative off-diagonal entry and does not preserve positivity for arbitrary states. Its linear flow is valid only while its limitation condition holds. The full minimum model switches matrices when that condition changes.

Direct dynamics let us test whether the predicted composition is approached from a chosen initial state. The following trajectories start at (MA,ME,MR)=(0.2,0.8,0.2)(M_A,M_E,M_R)=(0.2,0.8,0.2) in arbitrary mass units. Each choice holds the allocation and the three capacities fixed. The log-amount curves approach a common slope, while the ribosomal fraction approaches its allocation. Advancing the time cursor reads the computed state on those trajectories.

The capacity envelope is recovered as precursor turnover becomes rapid. As precursor turnover kAk_A becomes large, the precursor-limited eigenvalue tends to k(1−fR)k(1-f_R). The consistent rate tends to min⁡[σfR,k(1−fR)]\min[\sigma f_R,k(1-f_R)], exactly the two-sector capacity envelope. The precursor ratio tends to zero at the optimal crossing. It need not vanish at every nonoptimal allocation. Finite precursor inventory adds material that must also be reproduced, so the optimal rate is lower than that limiting envelope predicts.

The positive model also supports a convergence argument. At constant allocation, the amount balances give MR−fRM=MR(0)−fRM(0)M_R-f_RM=M_R(0)-f_RM(0). For strictly positive initial amounts and capacities, with 0<fR<10<f_R<1, protein mass grows without bound and the ribosomal fraction approaches fRf_R. The precursor-ratio balance then selects its unique positive limiting ratio. The exposition gives the full proof. Fixed allocation can produce balanced growth without being optimal.

What does the growing precursor inventory cost?

At the best allocation, the increasing translation-limited rate meets the decreasing precursor-limited rate. The crossing has fR=λ/σf_R=\lambda/\sigma and a=λ/kAa=\lambda/k_A. Substituting into the balanced supply condition k(1−fR)=λ(1+a)k(1-f_R)=\lambda(1+a) gives

λσ+λk+λ2kkA=1.\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}+\frac{\lambda}{k}+\frac{\lambda^2}{kk_A}=1.

The first term pays for ribosomal protein. The second pays for metabolic protein supplying new protein. The third pays for additional metabolic protein supplying the growing precursor stock. It is an enzyme cost, not a third protein sector or the precursor-to-protein mass ratio.

For k=2k=2, σ=3\sigma=3 and kA=8k_A=8 per hour, the optimum is about 1.108 per hour, below the small-pool bound 1.2. Its ribosomal fraction is about 0.369. The exposition derives the positive root and provides a calculator. These values are illustrative model predictions.

The same method extends to more detailed self-production networks. Roy, Goberman and Pugatch’s 2021 PNAS paper analyzes coupled autocatalytic machinery, including transcription and translation, by identifying limitation regimes and checking growth solutions within them. Several internal limitation states can share a growth-law relation, so the relation need not identify the full state. The useful method is to write the production network, identify the active limitations, solve for a growth eigenmode and test its consistency. [7]

For a self-production model, calculate a growth eigenvalue and its composition together. Reject any growth eigenmode with negative amounts or a violated limitation inequality, then check how the full dynamics approaches it.

Part IV

Interpret allocation as investment and return to flux budgets

The same production constraints explain both a growing machine and the reduced budget used to optimize it.

11Productive investment turns a growth model into an allocation problem

The economic analogy becomes precise when the stocks, production rates and objective are specified.

A self-replicating cell reinvests production in productive capacity. The metabolic sector produces inputs for the ribosomal sector. The ribosomal sector produces new metabolic and ribosomal machinery. An allocation to either sector changes both today’s production constraint and tomorrow’s stock of machinery. This is the concrete connection to an economy that uses part of its output to build more productive equipment.

Cellular constructionCorresponding two-sector economy
Metabolic protein mass MEM_EStock of equipment that supplies production inputs
Ribosomal-sector protein mass MRM_RStock of equipment that constructs new equipment
Precursor supply kMEkM_EInput production supported by the supplying sector
Translation capacity σMR\sigma M_RCapacity to manufacture new productive stock
Synthesis allocation fRf_RFraction of new equipment invested in the construction sector
Balanced growthStocks expand at a common rate with constant proportions

The analogy is exact only for an economy with these specified equations. We have assumed fixed conversion capacities, a homogeneous measure of productive mass and a declared allocation rule. We have not included prices, households, strategic behavior, depreciation or a general production function. A Cobb–Douglas or Solow model adds different assumptions. The transfer of reasoning comes from matching the production structure, not from calling all growth equations equivalent.

To choose an allocation, specify what is being maximized. Return to the small-precursor model and maximize steady growth in a constant environment. Its decision variables are the ribosomal protein fraction and the growth rate. The constraints are linear:

maximizeϕR,λλsubject toλ≤σϕR,λ≤k(1−ϕR),0≤ϕR≤1,λ≥0.\begin{aligned}\text{maximize}_{\phi_R,\lambda}\quad&\lambda\\\text{subject to}\quad&\lambda\leq\sigma\phi_R,\\&\lambda\leq k(1-\phi_R),\\&0\leq\phi_R\leq1,\quad\lambda\geq0.\end{aligned}

The linear program is a geometric form of the supply-and-demand argument. Each inequality removes unsupported combinations of growth and composition. The maximum is the highest remaining point. Because both sectors are required, the optimum is an interior allocation at which both capacities constrain growth.

An equivalent cost calculation shows why both sectors matter. Supporting rate λ\lambda requires at least λ/σ\lambda/\sigma of the protein budget in translation and λ/k\lambda/k in metabolic supply. Adding the minimum requirements gives

λσ+λk≤1⟹λ≤kσk+σ.\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}+\frac{\lambda}{k}\leq1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda\leq\frac{k\sigma}{k+\sigma}.

This proof does not need a search over allocations. Each inverse capacity is a protein cost per unit growth. The total cost cannot exceed the available protein budget. If a fixed fraction ϕQ\phi_Q is committed elsewhere, replace the right-hand side by 1−ϕQ1-\phi_Q. A changed budget changes the optimum even if both processing capacities remain unchanged.

Optimal balanced composition is not a law for reaching that composition. The linear program supplies a target. It does not specify the regulatory mechanism that sets fR(t)f_R(t) or the time required to build the target fractions. During environmental change, a cell can benefit from a reserve or from a temporarily suboptimal allocation. The optional nutrient-shift direction will ask when those costs pay back.

Charging for machinery can change which metabolic strategy is favored. Molenaar et al.'s self-replicator models make this cellular-investment principle explicit. A pathway with a lower nutrient yield can require less protein per unit production. Their model also includes membrane and transport requirements omitted here. The exposition works a two-pathway allocation problem to show how nutrient yield and protein cost select different strategies. [12]

Turn a production model into an investment problem by naming the stocks, capacities, resource budget and objective. Keep the optimum separate from the controller and dynamics that might realize it.

12Proteome-constrained FBA compresses the costs of self-production

A cost on flux can be derived by eliminating the machinery required to carry that flux.

We can now explain the protein budget that Lecture 13 imposed. Consider nonnegative metabolic fluxes vjv_j, all normalized per unit total protein mass. Split reversible processes into directional fluxes when using this form. Let ϕE,j\phi_{E,j} be the protein fraction allocated to the enzyme machinery supporting process jj. Let aja_j be that process’s capacity per unit allocated protein fraction. The capacity condition is vj≤ajϕE,jv_j\leq a_j\phi_{E,j}.

Each flux therefore has a minimum protein requirement. Rearranging gives ϕE,j≥vj/aj\phi_{E,j}\geq v_j/a_j. The translation sector likewise requires at least ϕR,0+λ/κT\phi_{R,0}+\lambda/\kappa_T under the steady relation in Section 8. Add these requirements to the fixed sector ϕQ\phi_Q and impose a total fraction of at most one:

∑jvjaj+λκT≤1−ϕQ−ϕR,0.\pf{\sum_j\frac{v_j}{a_j}+\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_T}\leq1-\phi_Q-\phi_{R,0}}.

Stoichiometric balance supplies a different constraint from protein capacity. Let SS be the metabolic stoichiometric matrix and let bb list the precursor amounts required to produce one unit of new total protein together with its fixed share of non-protein biomass. A balanced construction can write Sv=bλSv=b\lambda, or include −b-b as the biomass column and write the augmented steady balance as zero. The biomass column describes what must be made. The protein inequality describes the machinery needed to sustain its production.

The familiar CAFBA form follows by collecting these costs. Denote the carbon-uptake flux by vCv_C, its protein cost by wCw_C, the other metabolic costs by wjw_j and the growth-associated translation cost by wRw_R. Then a coarse resource constraint has the form

wCvC+∑jwjvj+wRλ≤ϕavailable.w_Cv_C+\sum_jw_jv_j+w_R\lambda\leq\phi_{\mathrm{available}}.

The available fraction subtracts fixed and baseline commitments under the chosen sector definition. Mori and colleagues’ 2016 constrained allocation flux balance analysis develops this kind of allocation constraint with metabolic flux balance. Lecture 13 used μ\mu for the specific growth rate. The symbol λ\lambda here denotes that same quantity when the biomass flux is normalized to actual balanced growth. [8]

The reduction is useful because it keeps production costs while removing many state variables. An explicit self-production model can retain enzyme amounts, precursor pools, changing volume and synthesis allocation. A static proteome-constrained FBA model compresses these into capacities and a budget. The reduction is appropriate when the stated steady composition, effective capacities and objective describe the question. A nutrient shift or a changing reserve can require the omitted states again.

The question we can now answer

A cell grows faster when its productive sectors can jointly supply and reproduce the required machinery at a higher rate. The nutrient-growth curve is an observable consequence. Ribosomal and metabolic fractions expose the internal production tradeoff. A consistent growth eigenmode supplies the rate and composition together. A flux budget is a reduced representation of those production requirements.

The remaining modeling task is to explain how allocation is selected. Neither a fitted growth law nor a steady optimum specifies the regulatory architecture that controls synthesis. Lecture 16 takes up the broader mechanistic cell model: production, binding, regulation, growth and dilution must be represented consistently. Lecture 15 first steps down to the design of individual proteins, the components whose activities enter these capacities.

Precursor availability supplies one concrete regulatory signal. A mismatch between supply and use changes precursor abundance and tRNA charging. Scott et al. (2014) connect this information to ppGpp-mediated ribosome synthesis and analyze conditions under which supply-driven activation approaches efficient allocation. This is an additional mechanism with an operating range. The lineage explains the biological and theoretical connection. [11]

Derive a protein cost from a catalytic capacity, combine it with a translation cost, and recognize what a proteome-constrained FBA model retains or omits about a self-producing cell.

Optional directions · scoped for later development

Changing environments change the investment question

The main construction concerns sustained growth in a constant environment. These branches ask when that objective or timescale is insufficient.

A1Nutrient shifts test the value of reserve machinery

The next experiment changes the environment before composition can catch up. A nutrient upshift can change precursor supply or ribosome activity quickly. Existing protein fractions change only through synthesis, removal and dilution. A useful extension would start from the measured shift, show which capacity changes first, and compare a cell carrying reserve machinery with one optimized only for the old steady condition.

This branch is deliberately left as a development stub. The source route is Mori, Erickson, Hwa and colleagues’ 2017 work on proteome reserve and nutrient shifts. Lecture 13 already presents specific reductions from those papers. A future Lecture 14 extension should derive the relevant state equations from the self-production construction here, preserve the different assumptions of the two papers, and compare upshifts with downshifts. It should quantify the transient benefit and the steady cost of a reserve before claiming that the reserve is optimal. [9] [10]

For a growth-shift experiment, separate the capacity that changes immediately from the composition that must be rebuilt before choosing a transient model.

A2Population growth makes survival and switching part of the allocation problem

A varying environment can change what counts as successful growth. A phenotype that grows rapidly in one episode can fare poorly in the next. Population composition and survival then matter alongside the steady growth rate of one cell type. The natural comparison follows multiplication across a specified sequence of environmental episodes, rather than optimizing a single constant environment.

The passive-decision mechanism needs its own test. The lecturer’s current bacterial-passive-population-decision project suggests examining a constant phenotype-switching rate during a shared-resource window. Resource depletion can change the time available to switch and thereby produce a population-dependent outcome without a cell directly sensing population density. Demonstrating that mechanism, fitting measured switching fractions and proving optimality are three separate tasks.

This branch also remains a development stub. The intended sequence is to define the environmental ensemble and population-growth objective, derive a minimal switching-and-resource model, then compare passive switching with an explicitly sensing strategy at matched costs. Quantitative agreement and near-optimality are not assumed. The cellular investment model above supplies the production tradeoff, but it does not settle the population strategy.

Before interpreting phenotypic diversity as bet-hedging, specify the environments, the multiplication and survival objective, and the mechanistic alternatives being compared.
