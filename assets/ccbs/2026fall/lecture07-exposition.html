From a molecular state list to a predictive model

A calculation chapter on Markov dynamics, detailed balance, physical weights and fast biological regulation.

CCBS · Fall 2026 · Lecture 7 · Lecturer-written exposition · Revised 22 September 2026

The task is to turn a biological description into a calculation you can defend. A promoter switches, an enzyme changes conformation, or a receptor binds a ligand. Which states must be distinguished? What sets the waiting time? When can equilibrium weights replace a kinetic solve? What measurement would reveal that the replacement failed?

This chapter builds that competence in four steps. First construct a jump model and calculate its dynamics. Then test whether its stationary distribution is an equilibrium distribution. Next use physical state weights to derive regulatory responses. Finally restore finite pools, changing inputs and unresolved mechanisms. A gated receptor supplies a complete worked test. Three optional applications develop the same tools beyond the equilibrium shortcut.

The mathematics assumes basic differentiation, integration and probability. Matrix notation is introduced where it is needed. Numerical examples are illustrative unless explicitly identified as reconstructions using published model parameters. Neither kind is presented as new experimental data. The core selects the classroom argument. This chapter supplies the methods needed to repeat and extend it independently.

Part I

Build and solve a molecular jump model

Learn to predict occupancies, count fluctuations and arrival times from a specified set of states and transition hazards.

1Choose a state that retains the needed information

A state is a predictive description, not merely a measured variable. In an autonomous deterministic model, a state x(t)x(t) obeys x˙=f(x)\dot x=f(x). Given that state and a well-posed rate function, the future trajectory is fixed. For a spring, position alone is insufficient. The same position with opposite velocities leads to different motion. Position and velocity together form the state.

A stochastic state determines a future probability law. Write ξ(t)\xi(t) for a random molecular configuration. The Markov assumption says that, conditional on the present state and the specified environment, earlier history adds no predictive information. One promoter might have states OFF and ON. A molecule-count model instead has states NX(t)=0,1,2,…N_X(t)=0,1,2,\ldots. Random trajectories differ even when they start in the same state.

Constant transition hazards require a fixed environment in addition to the Markov assumption. If an externally controlled ligand changes with time, a Markov model can have time-dependent hazards. This chapter first holds external conditions fixed, giving a time-homogeneous continuous-time Markov chain. Later we let the conditions change and test whether the fast state follows them.

Checkpoint: is “gene OFF” a sufficient state?

Suppose OFF pools two hidden configurations. One activates at 1 s−11\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}, the other at 10 s−110\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}, and they do not interconvert during an OFF episode. Equal initial weights give survival S(t)=12e−t+12e−10tS(t)=\tfrac12e^{-t}+\tfrac12e^{-10t}. Its instantaneous activation hazard is [e−t+10e−10t]/[e−t+e−10t][e^{-t}+10e^{-10t}]/[e^{-t}+e^{-10t}]. This falls from 5.5 s−15.5\,\mathrm{s}^{-1} toward 1 s−11\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}. The duration already spent OFF predicts the future. A single fixed-rate OFF state is therefore not exact.

Choose state variables by predictive sufficiency, and test a proposed lumped state for hidden memory.

2Derive the time and identity of the next event

A hazard specifies a short-time conditional probability. At a fixed state, let event channel rr have hazard ar≥0a_r\geq0. The probability that this event occurs in the next Δt\Delta t is arΔt+o(Δt)a_r\Delta t+o(\Delta t). Hazards have inverse-time units. Multiple-jump probabilities are of smaller order than Δt\Delta t in this finite-rate model.

Survival converts that local statement into a waiting-time law. Let TT be the time to the next event, S(t)=Pr⁡(T>t)S(t)=\Pr(T>t), and a0=∑rara_0=\sum_r a_r. Conditional on no event yet, the state has not changed. For constant hazards,

S(t+Δt)=S(t)[1−a0Δt]+o(Δt),S′(t)=−a0S(t),S(0)=1,S(t)=e−a0t,fT(t)=a0e−a0t.\begin{aligned} S(t+\Delta t)&=S(t)[1-a_0\Delta t]+o(\Delta t),\\ S'(t)&=-a_0S(t),\qquad S(0)=1,\\ S(t)&=e^{-a_0t},\qquad f_T(t)=a_0e^{-a_0t}. \end{aligned}

The mean waiting time is ∫0∞S(t) dt=1/a0\int_0^\infty S(t)\,dt=1/a_0. The ratio S(t+s)/S(t)=e−a0sS(t+s)/S(t)=e^{-a_0s} proves memorylessness: surviving for time tt does not change the residual waiting-time law. If a0=0a_0=0, the state is absorbing and the next-event time is infinite.

Competing events give a joint density, not a second waiting-time assumption. Write RR for the random channel index in this calculation. The probability density that the first event is channel rr at time tt is the probability of surviving until tt, multiplied by that channel's hazard:

fT,R(t,r)=are−a0t=a0e−a0t⏟event-time densityara0⏟event probability.f_{T,R}(t,r)=a_re^{-a_0t} =\underbrace{a_0e^{-a_0t}}_{\text{event-time density}} \underbrace{\frac{a_r}{a_0}}_{\text{event probability}}.

This factorization gives Gillespie's two draws. Draw T=−ln⁡U/a0T=-\ln U/a_0 with UU uniform on (0,1)(0,1). Choose event rr with probability ar/a0a_r/a_0. Update the state and recompute all hazards. The same law follows from the minimum of independent exponential clocks, since their survival probabilities multiply. The independent-clock construction applies at the current state, not unchanged throughout the future trajectory.1

Checkpoint: what changes under a time-varying input?

If hazards depend on prescribed time, then S(t)=exp⁡[−∫0ta0(u) du]S(t)=\exp[-\int_0^t a_0(u)\,du]. The event identity conditional on an event at time tt has probability ar(t)/a0(t)a_r(t)/a_0(t). The simple constant-rate exponential draw is no longer valid unless the changing input has been resolved appropriately.

Derive the next-event distribution and recognize when Gillespie's constant-hazard step applies.

3Assemble a probability-conserving generator

Each source state supplies one column of the generator. For distinct states i,ji,j, define qijq_{ij} as the hazard from jj to ii. The second index is the source. Let pi(t)=Pr⁡[ξ(t)=i]p_i(t)=\Pr[\xi(t)=i] and collect these probabilities in a column pp. The two-index quantity qijq_{ij} is a transition hazard. A one-species subscript such as qEq_E will denote a molecular total, as in earlier lectures.

The diagonal records all ways to leave the source. Set qjj=−∑i≠jqijq_{jj}=-\sum_{i\ne j}q_{ij}. Counting arrivals and departures over a short interval gives

pi(t+Δt)=pi(t)+Δt[∑j≠iqijpj(t)+qiipi(t)]+o(Δt),p˙i=∑jqijpj,p˙=Qp,1⊤Q=0.\begin{aligned} p_i(t+\Delta t)&=p_i(t)+\Delta t\left[\sum_{j\ne i}q_{ij}p_j(t)+q_{ii}p_i(t)\right]+o(\Delta t),\\ \dot p_i&=\sum_jq_{ij}p_j,\qquad \dot p=Qp,\qquad \mathbf1^\top Q=0. \end{aligned}

Here 1\mathbf1 is the column of ones and ⊤\top means transpose. The zero column sums imply d(1⊤p)/dt=0d(\mathbf1^\top p)/dt=0. Nonnegative off-diagonal entries prevent probability from flowing out of a state that currently has zero probability. These two checks are the first diagnostics for an assembled generator.

The same matrix acts backward on observables. Give state jj a numerical value gjg_j. Its ensemble mean is ⟨g⟩=g⊤p\langle g\rangle=g^\top p. Differentiating identifies the observable generator L\mathcal L:

d⟨g⟩dt=g⊤Qp=⟨Lg⟩,(Lg)j=(Q⊤g)j=∑i≠jqij(gi−gj).\frac{d\langle g\rangle}{dt} =g^\top Qp=\langle\mathcal Lg\rangle,\qquad (\mathcal Lg)_j=(Q^\top g)_j=\sum_{i\ne j}q_{ij}(g_i-g_j).

The forward equation evolves probability columns. The backward operator acts on a function of the starting state. This distinction will determine the transpose in the first-passage equation.

Checkpoint: assemble a three-state chain.

Take transitions 0→10\to1, 1→01\to0, 1→21\to2, and 2→12\to1 with hazards 2, 1, 4 and 7 per minute. These are state-transition arrows. The generator is

Q=(−2102−5704−7)min−1.Q=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1&0\\2&-5&7\\0&4&-7\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{min}^{-1}.

Column 1, the middle column, contains two exits. Their sum is 5 per minute, so its diagonal is −5, not −1 or −4.

4Separate stationary occupancy from relaxation speed

A two-state promoter can be solved exactly. Label OFF as 0 and ON as 1. Let activation and deactivation hazards be a>0a>0 and b>0b>0. Its generator and ON probability equation are

Q=(−aba−b),p˙on=a(1−pon)−bpon.Q=\begin{pmatrix}-a&b\\a&-b\end{pmatrix},\qquad \dot p_{\mathrm{on}}=a(1-p_{\mathrm{on}})-bp_{\mathrm{on}}.

Subtract the stationary value to isolate the decaying part. Setting the derivative to zero gives pon∗=a/(a+b)p_{\mathrm{on}}^*=a/(a+b). For e=pon−pon∗e=p_{\mathrm{on}}-p_{\mathrm{on}}^*, the equation becomes e˙=−(a+b)e\dot e=-(a+b)e. Therefore

pon(t)=aa+b+[pon(0)−aa+b]e−(a+b)t,trelax=1a+b.p_{\mathrm{on}}(t)=\frac{a}{a+b} +\left[p_{\mathrm{on}}(0)-\frac{a}{a+b}\right]e^{-(a+b)t}, \qquad t_{\mathrm{relax}}=\frac1{a+b}.

The ratio fixes occupancy while the sum fixes speed. Multiplying both hazards by ten leaves the stationary fraction unchanged and divides the relaxation time by ten. If both fraction and relaxation time are measured, the two hazards become identifiable within this model: a=pon∗/trelaxa=p_{\mathrm{on}}^*/t_{\mathrm{relax}} and b=(1−pon∗)/trelaxb=(1-p_{\mathrm{on}}^*)/t_{\mathrm{relax}}.

Checkpoint: distinguish three times.

For a=2,b=1 s−1a=2,b=1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}, the mean OFF holding time is 1/a=0.5 s1/a=0.5\,\mathrm{s}. The mean ON holding time is 1/b=1 s1/b=1\,\mathrm{s}. Ensemble relaxation takes 1/(a+b)=1/3 s1/(a+b)=1/3\,\mathrm{s}. A residence time and a relaxation time answer different questions.

Infer what occupancy data determine, what they leave undetermined, and which transient measurement supplies the missing clock.

5Recover the count equation and its Poisson law

A chemical master equation is a generator equation on molecule-count states. For species X1,…,XnX_1,\ldots,X_n, let NX(t)N_X(t) be the random count vector, with entries NXi(t)N_{X_i}(t). Reaction channel rr changes the vector by γr\gamma_r and has propensity ar(n)a_r(n) in count state nn. Insert its hazard into Qn+γr,nQ_{n+\gamma_r,n}. Sum contributions when distinct channels have the same endpoints. The forward equation becomes

p˙(n,t)=∑r[ar(n−γr)p(n−γr,t)−ar(n)p(n,t)].\dot p(n,t)=\sum_r\left[a_r(n-\gamma_r)p(n-\gamma_r,t)-a_r(n)p(n,t)\right].

The first term counts arrivals from a predecessor count state. An impossible predecessor contributes zero. The second term counts departures. The graph's nodes are entire molecular populations, not individual chemical species. This is the connection between a reaction list, the count-state graph and the master equation.

Constant addition and first-order removal give a birth-death chain. The composite chemical accounting is ∅⇝X\varnothing\rightsquigarrow X, X⇝∅X\rightsquigarrow\varnothing. Declare the separate propensities a+(n)=b0a_+(n)=b_0 and a−(n)=dna_-(n)=dn, with b0>0,d>0b_0>0,d>0. An event adds or removes one molecule. The probability equation is

p˙n=b0pn−1+d(n+1)pn+1−(b0+dn)pn,p−1=0.\dot p_n=b_0p_{n-1}+d(n+1)p_{n+1}-(b_0+dn)p_n,\qquad p_{-1}=0.

The boundary at zero forces zero stationary edge current. Define Jn=b0pn∗−d(n+1)pn+1∗J_n=b_0p_n^*-d(n+1)p_{n+1}^*. Stationarity gives Jn=Jn−1J_n=J_{n-1}. At state zero there is no incoming current from a negative count, so J0=0J_0=0. Successive ratios and normalization then give

pn+1∗=ρn+1pn∗,ρ=b0d,pn∗=p0∗ρnn!,1=p0∗∑n=0∞ρnn!=p0∗eρ,pn∗=e−ρρnn!.\begin{aligned} p_{n+1}^*&=\frac{\rho}{n+1}p_n^*,\qquad \rho=\frac{b_0}{d},\\ p_n^*&=p_0^*\frac{\rho^n}{n!},\qquad 1=p_0^*\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^n}{n!}=p_0^*e^\rho,\\ p_n^*&=e^{-\rho}\frac{\rho^n}{n!}. \end{aligned}

This normalizable stationary law is Poisson. Directly shifting the sum gives ⟨NX⟩=ρ\langle N_X\rangle=\rho and ⟨NX(NX−1)⟩=ρ2\langle N_X(N_X-1)\rangle=\rho^2, hence Var⁡NX=ρ\operatorname{Var}N_X=\rho. Positive removal is essential. With d=0,b0>0d=0,b_0>0, counts drift upward and no stationary law exists. A normalizable count law does not establish physical equilibrium of the unresolved synthesis and removal mechanisms.

The mean equation follows from the backward operator without closing a hierarchy. For g(n)=ng(n)=n, one addition changes gg by +1 and one removal changes it by −1. Thus Lg=b0−dn\mathcal Lg=b_0-dn and

d⟨NX⟩dt=b0−d⟨NX⟩,d⟨X⟩dt=b0Ω−d⟨X⟩,X=NXΩ,Ω=NAV.\frac{d\langle N_X\rangle}{dt}=b_0-d\langle N_X\rangle,\qquad \frac{d\langle X\rangle}{dt}=\frac{b_0}{\Omega}-d\langle X\rangle,\qquad X=\frac{N_X}{\Omega},\quad\Omega=N_AV.

Here VV is the fixed volume in liters when XX is molar, and NAN_A is Avogadro's constant. The count propensity b0b_0 is an event rate. Because each event adds one molecule, its numerical value also gives molecules added per time. Dividing by Ω\Omega produces a concentration flux.

6Calculate a first-arrival time with the backward equation

A first-arrival question needs a target and a starting state. Let AA be a target set. Let τj\tau_j be the expected first time to reach AA starting from state jj. Set τj=0\tau_j=0 on the target. Assume the target is reached with finite mean time. A finite irreducible chain and a nonempty target satisfy this condition.

Condition on the first holding interval to derive the equation. The departure hazard from jj is aj=−qjja_j=-q_{jj}. Its mean holding time is 1/aj1/a_j. The next state is ii with probability qij/ajq_{ij}/a_j. Consequently

τj=1aj+∑i≠jqijajτi,−1=∑iqijτi=(Q⊤τ)j,j∉A.\begin{aligned} \tau_j&=\frac1{a_j}+\sum_{i\ne j}\frac{q_{ij}}{a_j}\tau_i,\\ -1&=\sum_iq_{ij}\tau_i=(Q^\top\tau)_j,\qquad j\notin A. \end{aligned}

The reduced matrix retains its original diagonal. Let BB be the states outside the target. Remove target rows and columns from QQ, then solve QBB⊤τB=−1Q_{BB}^{\top}\tau_B=-\mathbf1. Do not recompute the diagonal after deleting target transitions. Those exits are exactly how probability is absorbed. The transpose appears because arrival time is a function of the starting state.

A closed/open/ON model gives an explicit biological example. Treat these labels as inaccessible, accessible and transcriptionally active configurations. They are a modelling choice, not a universal three-state description of mammalian promoters. Let closed-to-open, open-to-closed and open-to-ON hazards be a,b,ca,b,c. The equations are

τclosed=1a+τopen,τopen=1b+c+bb+cτclosed,τopen=1+b/ac,τclosed=1a+1c+bac.\begin{aligned} \tau_{\mathrm{closed}}&=\frac1a+\tau_{\mathrm{open}},\\ \tau_{\mathrm{open}}&=\frac1{b+c}+\frac{b}{b+c}\tau_{\mathrm{closed}},\\ \tau_{\mathrm{open}}&=\frac{1+b/a}{c},\qquad \tau_{\mathrm{closed}}=\frac1a+\frac1c+\frac{b}{ac}. \end{aligned}

Substitution of the first equation into the second produces the last line. The extra term b/(ac)b/(ac) is the delay caused by returns to the closed state. With a=2,b=1,c=4 min−1a=2,b=1,c=4\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}, the answers are 0.375 min from open and 0.875 min from closed. Any ON-to-open rate is irrelevant to this first arrival, since the clock stops at ON.

Checkpoint: can removal of the target be treated as a smaller closed chain?

No. With one starting state and one target connected by hazard kk, the retained matrix is [−k][-k]. It gives τ=1/k\tau=1/k. Replacing its diagonal by zero would remove the absorption and make the equation unsolvable.

7Solve a stationary ring and its current

Stationarity requires zero net accumulation at each node. A stationary probability column p∗p^* satisfies Qp∗=0Qp^*=0 and 1⊤p∗=1\mathbf1^\top p^*=1. A finite irreducible chain has a unique positive stationary distribution. Numerically, replace one redundant row of Qp∗=0Qp^*=0 with normalization. Check the residual, nonnegativity and normalization after solving.

Three states reveal what node balance leaves free. Orient the ring 1→2→3→11\to2\to3\to1. Call its forward hazards a,b,ca,b,c and reverse hazards r,s,ur,s,u, respectively. All are positive and have inverse-time units. Define the one-way flux ϕi→j=qjipi\phi_{i\to j}=q_{ji}p_i and net current Ji→j=ϕi→j−ϕj→iJ_{i\to j}=\phi_{i\to j}-\phi_{j\to i}. Node balance gives

Eliminate the probabilities to obtain the current. The first two equalities imply p2∗=(ap1∗−Jcycle)/rp_2^*=(ap_1^*-J_{\mathrm{cycle}})/r and p3∗=[abp1∗−(b+r)Jcycle]/(rs)p_3^*=[abp_1^*-(b+r)J_{\mathrm{cycle}}]/(rs). Insert the latter into the third equality. This gives (abc−rsu)p1∗=(bc+rc+rs)Jcycle(abc-rsu)p_1^*=(bc+rc+rs)J_{\mathrm{cycle}}. Repeating the cyclic relabeling and imposing normalization yields

w1=rc+bc+rs,w2=as+us+ac,w3=ub+ab+ur,D=w1+w2+w3,pi∗=wiD,Jcycle=abc−rsuD.\begin{aligned} w_1&=rc+bc+rs,&w_2&=as+us+ac,&w_3&=ub+ab+ur,\\ D&=w_1+w_2+w_3,\qquad p_i^*=\frac{w_i}{D},\qquad J_{\mathrm{cycle}}=\frac{abc-rsu}{D}. \end{aligned}

The weights in this kinetic solve have units of inverse time squared. They are not yet Boltzmann factors. Each term is the product of the hazards in a directed spanning tree whose arrows lead to its root. The general matrix-tree formula extends this construction to larger finite graphs.2

Equal occupancies do not imply equal currents. If all six hazards are 1/s, then pi∗=1/3p_i^*=1/3 and the current is zero. If every forward hazard is 2/s and every reverse hazard is 1/s, the same probabilities coexist with Jcycle=1/3 s−1J_{\mathrm{cycle}}=1/3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}. Forward traffic is 2/3 per second on each edge and reverse traffic is 1/3 per second. The probabilities stay constant because the circulating inflow equals circulating outflow.

8Convert an enzyme mechanism into hazards, flux and work

The ring can represent the states of one supplied enzyme. Resolve the elementary pairs

E+S⇌k−Sk+SCES,CES⇌kcat−kcat+CEP,E+P⇌k−Pk+PCEP.\begin{aligned} E+S&\xrightleftharpoons[k_{-S}]{k_{+S}}C_{ES},\\ C_{ES}&\xrightleftharpoons[k_{\mathrm{cat}}^-]{k_{\mathrm{cat}}^+}C_{EP},\\ E+P&\xrightleftharpoons[k_{-P}]{k_{+P}}C_{EP}. \end{aligned}

Take free substrate SS and product PP as maintained reservoirs. The enzyme states are E,CES,CEPE,C_{ES},C_{EP}. In that order, the three clockwise hazards are k+SS,kcat+,k−Pk_{+S}S,k_{\mathrm{cat}}^+,k_{-P}. Their reverse hazards are k−S,kcat−,k+PPk_{-S},k_{\mathrm{cat}}^-,k_{+P}P. Each association constant has concentration-inverse/time units. Multiplication by its free ligand converts it into an inverse-time hazard.

One cycle current gives three distinct dimensional outputs. For one enzyme, JcycleJ_{\mathrm{cycle}} counts net conversions per time. For enzyme concentration qEq_E, the concentration throughput is v=qEJcyclev=q_EJ_{\mathrm{cycle}}. For an actual enzyme count NEN_E, the expected total event current is NEJcycleN_EJ_{\mathrm{cycle}}. None of these quantities is a microscopic association constant.

The rate-product ratio identifies the cycle's dimensionless driving. Define the affinity

A=ln⁡k+SS kcat+k−Pk−Skcat−k+PP.\mathcal A=\ln\frac{k_{+S}S\,k_{\mathrm{cat}}^+k_{-P}} {k_{-S}k_{\mathrm{cat}}^-k_{+P}P}.

For a thermodynamically consistent enzyme tightly coupled to one substrate-to-product conversion, with no other work exchange, local detailed balance gives A=(μS−μP)/(kBT)\mathcal A=(\mu_S-\mu_P)/(k_BT). Here chemical potentials are per molecule. Molar chemical potentials instead require RTRT in the denominator. Keeping one convention prevents an Avogadro-factor error.3

Current times affinity is a dissipation rate only with those physical assumptions. At steady state, the entropy-production rate per enzyme is S˙prod=kBJcycleA\dot S_{\mathrm{prod}}=k_BJ_{\mathrm{cycle}}\mathcal A. The dissipated power is TS˙prodT\dot S_{\mathrm{prod}}. Multiplying all hazards by the same factor leaves affinity and occupancies unchanged, but multiplies current and power. A large driving force does not guarantee a large throughput.

Checkpoint: what maintains the reservoirs?

Supplying SS and removing PP maintains the chemical free-energy difference. Each boundary event need not hydrolyze ATP directly. The larger metabolic network may supply the free energy. Without maintenance, a closed compatible system changes its substrate/product ratio and eventually removes the driving. Dissipation is not useful work output unless a coupled load has also been specified.

A glycolytic conversion with an explicit energy bill

Triosephosphate isomerase supplies a concrete substrate/product pair. In glycolysis, dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) interconverts with glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (GAP). Downstream reactions consume GAP. The overall relation DHAP↭GAP\mathrm{DHAP}\leftrightsquigarrow\mathrm{GAP} does not itself consume ATP. Use the three-state enzyme as a coarse teaching mechanism for this conversion, not as a complete experimentally resolved isomerase mechanism.

Flamholz and colleagues optimized glycolytic metabolite concentrations between 1 μM1\,\mu\mathrm M and 10 mM10\,\mathrm{mM}, with transformed energies at pH 7.5 and ionic strength 0.2 M. 10Reverse a stationary trajectory law

Time reversal compares probabilities of histories, not individual movies. A random equilibrium trajectory usually looks different from its own reversal. Reversibility means that each allowed trajectory and its reversed trajectory have equal probability density when both begin in the stationary ensemble.

Bayes' rule gives the reversed hazard. For a stationary process with probabilities p∗>0p^*>0, a reversed jump from jj to ii corresponds to an original jump from ii to jj. Dividing that stationary joint probability by the probability of the reversed starting state yields

qijrev=lim⁡Δt↓0Pr⁡[ξ(t−Δt)=i∣ξ(t)=j]Δt=qjipi∗pj∗,i≠j.q^{\mathrm{rev}}_{ij} =\lim_{\Delta t\downarrow0} \frac{\Pr[\xi(t-\Delta t)=i\mid\xi(t)=j]}{\Delta t} =q_{ji}\frac{p_i^*}{p_j^*},\qquad i\ne j.

Stationarity ensures that the reversed departure sum equals the original departure sum. Therefore their diagonals also agree. The identity Qrev=QQ^{\mathrm{rev}}=Q holds exactly when detailed balance holds.

The same result can be checked for an entire jump path. Consider states i0,…,imi_0,\ldots,i_m, with a specified duration spent in each visited state during a fixed observation window. The forward path density contains its initial probability, the product of its jump hazards and the exponential survival factors. The reversed path contains the same survival factors in reverse order. Their ratio is

Pr⁡[forward path]Pr⁡[reversed path]=pi0∗pim∗∏ℓ=0m−1qiℓ+1,iℓqiℓ,iℓ+1.\frac{\Pr[\text{forward path}]}{\Pr[\text{reversed path}]} =\frac{p_{i_0}^*}{p_{i_m}^*} \prod_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\frac{q_{i_{\ell+1},i_\ell}}{q_{i_\ell,i_{\ell+1}}}.

At detailed balance, each hazard ratio is the ratio of the destination and source equilibrium probabilities. The product telescopes and cancels the initial-probability ratio. Conversely, comparing one-jump stationary histories recovers pairwise detailed balance.

Equilibrium still contains molecular traffic. Equal forward and reverse frequencies need not be small. Increasing both hazards can produce many more jumps without changing weights or net current. The theorem here concerns discrete molecular configurations and counts, which are unchanged by physical reversal of time. Mechanical states with velocities require reversing those velocities as well.

21Distinguish hidden dissipation from hidden memory

Resolving states and resolving transition channels are separate tasks. Two physical mechanisms can connect the same pair of observed states. If the mechanisms exchange different chemical species or different amounts of fuel, their thermodynamic reverse partners must be tracked separately. Summing their hazards may preserve the observed Markov dynamics while concealing physical circulation.3

Two states already suffice to hide a driven cycle when there are two channels. Let channel α have hazards 2/s from 0 to 1 and 1/s from 1 to 0. Let channel β have hazards 1/s and 2/s in the same directions. The aggregate graph has hazards 3/s both ways and stationary probabilities p0∗=p1∗=1/2p_0^*=p_1^*=1/2. Its two resolved currents are

Jα=12(2)−12(1)=12 s−1,Jβ=−12 s−1.J_\alpha=\frac12(2)-\frac12(1)=\frac12\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}, \qquad J_\beta=-\frac12\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

The cycle goes forward through α and back through β. Its affinity is ln⁡4\ln4. Under a physical local-detailed-balance assignment, its stationary entropy production is kB(ln⁡2) s−1k_B(\ln2)\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}. The aggregate state path has exactly the same law as a genuinely equilibrium single-channel chain with hazards 3/s each way. No statistic of that state path alone can distinguish the two channel assignments.

Pooling hidden states can instead destroy the Markov property. The two-exponential OFF survival from section 1 retains the age of an OFF episode as predictive information. A sufficient condition for exact aggregation at all initial distributions is that every microscopic state in one block has the same total transition hazard into each other block. This is the strong lumpability condition. Fast internal mixing can sometimes justify an approximate aggregate model, but requires a separate time-scale analysis.

Checkpoint: which experiment is needed?

Non-exponential dwell times challenge a fixed-rate state description. Exponential dwell times do not establish physical equilibrium, because hidden parallel channels can preserve them exactly. To test those channels, measure fuel turnover, labeled molecular transfers or mechanism-specific event directions.

Choose a test that matches the missing information: state memory and physical channel dissipation are not the same defect.

24Calculate error, yield and cost in two-stage proofreading

A second rejection stage can amplify an existing discrimination. Start after an initial selection has produced an incoming wrong/right ratio f0<1f_0<1. Each arrival enters a paid intermediate. From a correct intermediate, commitment has hazard WW and discard has hazard f0ℓf_0\ell. From a wrong intermediate, commitment still has hazard WW, but discard has hazard ℓ\ell. These are exit hazards in a limiting attempt model. They do not resolve a complete thermodynamic mechanism.

Competing clocks give the success probability of each attempt. Integrating the commitment/event density gives sright=W/(W+f0ℓ)s_{\mathrm{right}}=W/(W+f_0\ell) and swrong=W/(W+ℓ)s_{\mathrm{wrong}}=W/(W+\ell). Multiply each incoming flux by its success probability. The wrong/right product ratio is

26Work a new model independently and test its assumptions

A finished model should expose what an experiment could disprove. State the subsystem, state variables, resolved channels, controlled inputs, output and time window. Problem A: a competitive sensor.

A receptor binds either A or B, never both. The free concentrations are maintained. Derive its A-bound probability and explain what a dose-response fit would infer if B were ignored.

Solution. The three weights are 1,A/KA,B/KB1,A/K_A,B/K_B. Hence pA=(A/KA)/(1+A/KA+B/KB)p_A=(A/K_A)/(1+A/K_A+B/K_B). It does not measure the isolated microscopic KAK_A.

Problem B: an equilibrium constant and a first-passage time.

A passive two-state receptor has bound/free weight ratio 4. Does this determine its mean binding time from free?

Solution. No. Hazards a=4k0,b=k0a=4k_0,b=k_0 for any positive rate scale k0k_0 give bound fraction 4/5. The mean first binding time is 1/(4k0)1/(4k_0). Measure a holding time or relaxation transient to identify the speed.

Problem C: repair a cycle.

A three-state ring has forward hazards (2, 3, 4)/s and reverse hazards (1, 6, 1)/s. Change only the third reverse hazard to make the graph detailed balanced.

Solution. The forward product is 24/s³. The reverse product is 6u6u/s², so choose u=4 s−1u=4\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}. Starting from weight 1 at state 1 gives state-2 weight 2 and state-3 weight 1. Normalization yields peq=(1,2,1)⊤/4p^{\mathrm{eq}}=(1,2,1)^\top/4. Check all three pairs, not just the product.

Problem D: a moving rare state.

A fast-state approximation has an absolute probability-error bound of 0.002. The activity is produced only by a state whose equilibrium probability is 0.001. Is the reduced activity necessarily accurate to 1%?

Solution. No. The stated bound exceeds the predicted rare-state probability. Activity-weighted error or a relative bound for that particular state is needed. A good approximation for the dominant populations can be poor for a rare functional state.

Problem E: infer equilibrium from a histogram.

All three enzyme states have stationary probability 1/3. Does that show the enzyme performs no net conversion?

Solution. No. The two rings in section 7 have this same histogram and different currents. Count directional transitions or independently measure substrate-to-product throughput. If only a two-state projection is observed, also examine whether physical channels have been hidden.

The method ends with separate claims, not one global equilibrium label. State sufficiency justifies the dynamical variables. Fixed conditions justify time-homogeneous hazards. Cycle compatibility justifies equilibrium weights on resolved channels. Fast attraction and slowly moving inputs justify a quasi-equilibrium reconstruction. Conservation connects free species to totals. Activities connect states to function. Each claim has its own calculation and possible failure.
